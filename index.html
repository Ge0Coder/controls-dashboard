☀ Morning Briefing 2026-07-31 09:46 UTC

CISA has issued a fresh urgent advisory to water and wastewater utilities after coordinated attacks compromised PLCs at dozens of Minnesota systems — the attack pattern is internet-exposed controllers with weak or default credentials, and it will not stop at water. Before lunch today, run a Shodan or Censys search on your own public IP ranges for Modbus (502), EtherNet/IP (44818), S7 (102), and vendor HMI web ports, and confirm every hit is either offline or behind a VPN with MFA. Also review the KEV additions to KNX, Fortinet, Cisco FMC, and SonicWall — any of those sitting at your IT/OT boundary need patching this week. Assume you are being scanned right now; the attackers are not targeting you specifically, but they will find you if you're exposed.

Today's Knowledge Bite

The KNX protocol, now on CISA's KEV list, was standardised in 1990 and predates most modern authentication concepts entirely — its original 'security' relied on physical access to the twisted-pair bus. When KNX went IP-routable, that assumption travelled with it, which is why CVE-2023-4346 and similar issues keep surfacing decades later. It's a reminder that field protocols age slower than the threat model around them.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was largely codified not in a university lab but on ships — Nicolas Minorsky derived the three-term control law in 1922 while analysing the automatic steering of the USS New Mexico, by observing how experienced helmsmen anticipated the ship's motion rather than just reacting to heading error. His insight that the human helmsman was mentally integrating past error and differentiating current error is the reason your loop tuner today still has an I and a D term. It's a rare case where reverse-engineering a skilled human operator produced a mathematical framework that outlived the original application by a century.

Tech Spotlight

Deterministic Ethernet via IEEE 802.1 Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) is quietly moving from spec sheets to real projects, particularly as vendors align OPC UA FX (Field eXchange) with TSN for controller-to-controller and controller-to-device traffic. The promise is compelling: a single Ethernet backbone that carries motion-grade cyclic data, standard IT traffic, and safety messages, all with bounded latency and no need for separate Profinet IRT, EtherCAT, or Sercos physical layers. In practice, adoption is being held back by the switch ecosystem. TSN requires every switch in the path to support the relevant sub-standards (802.1AS for time sync, 802.1Qbv for scheduled traffic, 802.1CB for frame replication), and mixed-vendor interoperability is still uneven. Engineering tools also lag — configuring a TSN schedule today feels closer to writing a spreadsheet than the plug-and-play Profinet experience most integrators expect. Practically, if you're specifying a greenfield line in 2026-2027, ask vendors specifically which TSN profiles their PLCs, drives, and switches support, and whether they've been tested against the OPC Foundation's FX conformance suite. Retrofitting TSN into an existing brownfield network almost never pays off; the sweet spot is new cells where you'd otherwise deploy two parallel networks.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (risk assessment and system design) and 62443-4-2 (component requirements) remain the two parts most actively cited in procurement specifications this year, particularly as European operators prepare for NIS2 enforcement audits and US water utilities react to the Minnesota incidents. Expect more RFQs to require vendor certification against 62443-4-2 SL2 or SL3 for new PLCs and RTUs — Siemens, Rockwell, Schneider, and ABB all now have certified product lines, but many mid-tier vendors do not, and that will start to filter procurement lists. On the US side, NIST is continuing work on SP 800-82 Rev 3 supplements addressing cloud-connected OT and remote engineering access, reflecting how much of the attack surface has shifted from the plant floor to vendor-hosted portals. If you're writing or updating a site cyber programme, aligning your remote-access architecture to the SP 800-82 remote-access guidance and 62443-3-3 SR 1.13 (access via untrusted networks) simultaneously will satisfy both auditors and insurers. Also worth tracking: ISA/IEC is progressing work on 62443-2-2 (security program ratings), which aims to give asset owners a scorecard-style way to demonstrate programme maturity — think of it as the operational counterpart to the product-focused 4-x parts. Early drafts suggest it will map cleanly onto NIS2 reporting obligations.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's incident is the coordinated compromise of PLCs at multiple Minnesota water utilities that prompted CISA's fresh advisory. Attackers identified controllers exposed directly to the public internet — a mix of small PLCs and HMI panels used for chemical dosing, pumping, and tank level control — and gained access via default or weak credentials and unauthenticated Modbus/HTTP interfaces. Reports indicate setpoint manipulation and HMI defacement at several sites, though operators say safety instrumented functions and manual overrides prevented any water-quality impact. How it happened is depressingly familiar: controllers were placed on cellular modems or ISP connections for remote monitoring convenience, with public IPs indexed by Shodan and Censys. In at least one case, the vendor's factory-default web interface was reachable with no authentication at all. Once inside, minimal skill was required — the HMI itself provided the interface to change setpoints. Three lessons for controls engineers. First, no PLC or HMI should ever have a public IP; put every remote site behind a broker-based VPN or a zero-trust gateway with MFA. Second, inventory ruthlessly — most affected utilities did not know a given controller was internet-reachable until CISA told them. Third, design HMIs so that dangerous setpoint changes require a second confirmation or supervisory approval; a defaced screen is annoying, but an unconfirmed chlorine setpoint change is a public health event.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The headline item for controls engineers this week is CISA's urgent advisory to the water and wastewater sector following coordinated intrusions that compromised dozens of Minnesota utilities via internet-exposed PLCs. The pattern mirrors prior campaigns against Unitronics devices: opportunistic scanning of public IPs, exploitation of default or weak credentials, and manipulation of HMI screens or setpoints. CISA is again urging operators to remove controllers from the public internet, enforce unique strong credentials, disable unused services, and put remote access behind a VPN with MFA. If you operate any small municipal or industrial site, assume your controllers are being scanned right now. On the enterprise side, two developments have indirect but real OT implications. JetBrains patched an unauthenticated RCE in TeamCity (CVE-2026-63077) via the agent polling protocol — a concern for shops that use TeamCity to build firmware, HMI images, or PLC logic artefacts, as a compromised build server means compromised downstream binaries. Separately, Microsoft's Azure Cosmos DB 'CosmosEscape' flaw exposed primary keys granting full read/write, worth checking if any of your historian, MES, or asset-management SaaS backends touch Cosmos. AI supply-chain risk continues to escalate: Anthropic disclosed that its own Claude models were used to deploy a malicious Python package that in turn compromised three organisations, echoing OpenAI's earlier disclosure. Engineers casually using LLM-generated code for scripts, PLC helper tools, or Ignition modules should treat that output as untrusted third-party code — scan it, pin dependencies, and never pip-install straight from an AI suggestion on an engineering workstation that touches production. Rounding out the week, Apple pushed patches across all operating systems, CareCloud disclosed a 350,000-record breach traced back to an AWS environment, and SANS ISC highlighted an SSH botnet that fingerprints victim hardware before deploying miners — a tactic increasingly seen against exposed jump hosts and Linux-based edge gateways used in remote OT sites.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The most OT-relevant KEV addition this cycle is CVE-2023-4346 in the KNX protocol's Connection Authorization Option 1, added 15 July. The flaw's overly restrictive lockout can be abused to purge devices and set a BCU key, effectively bricking a building automation bus. If you run KNX for HVAC, lighting, or facility systems on any site — especially data centres, hospitals, or pharma — audit whether IP-side KNXnet interfaces are exposed, and enable KNX Secure or Data Secure where the hardware supports it. Network-edge devices dominate the rest of the list and are directly relevant to the DMZ between IT and OT. Cisco Secure FMC (CVE-2026-20316, hard-coded password), Fortinet FortiOS (CVE-2025-68686), FortiSandbox command injection pair, SonicWall SMA1000, Check Point SmartConsole auth bypass, and Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator command injection collectively cover much of the perimeter and SD-WAN gear sitting in front of plant networks. Any of these being compromised gives an attacker a foothold adjacent to Purdue Level 3.5. Prioritise patching for anything that terminates vendor remote-access tunnels. The SharePoint deserialization entries (CVE-2026-50522, CVE-2026-58644) and the SharePoint missing-auth flaw (CVE-2026-56164) matter because SharePoint remains the de-facto document store for P&IDs, SOPs, and control narratives at many sites — a compromise leaks engineering intelligence useful for a follow-on OT attack.Read more →
