Today's Knowledge Bite

The KNX protocol, now flagged in CISA's KEV catalog, was originally standardized in 1990 as the European Installation Bus (EIB) and predates most modern industrial fieldbuses. It runs happily over a single twisted pair with devices drawing power from the bus itself, which is why it colonized building automation so thoroughly — but that same 1990s design philosophy is why default installations still trust anyone who can touch the wire.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was formalised not in a research lab but on a US Navy destroyer. In 1922, Nicolas Minorsky published the mathematical treatment of three-term control after watching helmsmen steer the USS New Mexico and observing that experienced sailors instinctively applied proportional correction, anticipated overshoot (derivative), and compensated for persistent wind and current (integral). Every temperature loop you tune today traces its lineage to a sailor's wrist on a ship's wheel a century ago.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) continues its slow march from marketing slide to actual plant deployment. The premise is straightforward: replace the patchwork of proprietary real-time Ethernet variants — PROFINET IRT, EtherCAT, Sercos, Ethernet/IP with CIP Motion — with a single standards-based stack where OPC UA PubSub carries the data model and IEEE 802.1 TSN provides deterministic delivery with sub-microsecond jitter. In principle you can run motion control, safety, and MES traffic on the same switch fabric, converging what today needs multiple physical networks. The practical reality in 2026 is more nuanced. Silicon is finally shipping in volume from the major switch vendors, and Siemens, B&R, Rockwell, and Beckhoff all have TSN-capable I/O and controllers, but interoperability between vendors — the whole point of the exercise — is still uneven. Engineering tools assume you own the whole network, PTP grandmaster selection is a footgun, and few integrators have the network engineering depth to configure stream reservation and gate control lists correctly. For most brownfield sites, the honest answer is that TSN will arrive as a side effect of buying newer drives and PLCs, not as a deliberate migration project. If you are specifying a greenfield line in the next 18 months, it is worth insisting on TSN-capable infrastructure even if you do not turn on the deterministic features immediately. The switches cost roughly the same, and you buy yourself optionality for a converged OT/IT network later without another rip-and-replace.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-2-1, the standard covering security program requirements for the asset owner, was reissued in 2024 and adoption is now visible in tender documents across process industries and utilities. If you have not looked at it recently, the revised version is a substantial rewrite: it aligns more cleanly with ISO 27001 structure, introduces explicit security program elements (SPEs), and drops much of the prescriptive language of the older edition in favour of maturity-based expectations. For engineering managers this means auditors are increasingly asking not just whether you have a patch policy, but whether you can evidence its maturity level against defined criteria. On the product side, IEC 62443-4-2 certifications continue to accumulate — Siemens, Schneider, Rockwell, Honeywell, and Emerson all now have certified components in their current product lines, and specifying a minimum Security Level (typically SL 2 for general process industry, SL 3 for critical infrastructure) in purchase orders is becoming standard practice. Worth checking that your current standard specifications reflect this; many engineering departments still cite the 2009-era language. NIST is also worth watching: SP 800-82 Rev 3 remains the practical reference for OT security in North America, and CISA's Cross-Sector Cybersecurity Performance Goals continue to be updated with OT-specific guidance. For anyone doing regulated work in the EU, remember NIS2 transposition is now enforced in most member states, and the fines are real.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the 2021 Oldsmar water treatment intrusion in Florida, because the follow-up investigations that concluded over the subsequent years contain lessons that are still being missed. An operator noticed the mouse cursor moving on a SCADA HMI and watched an attacker attempt to raise the sodium hydroxide setpoint from around 100 ppm to 11,100 ppm. The operator reversed the change immediately, and downstream pH alarms and dosing limits would likely have caught it regardless, but the incident became the reference case for remote-access risk in small utilities. What actually happened technically was mundane: a shared TeamViewer instance on an operator workstation, weak reused passwords, an unsupported Windows 7 host, and no MFA. The attacker was almost certainly opportunistic rather than targeted. Later analysis suggested the initial mouse movement may even have been a legitimate remote session from a supervisor, and the setpoint change was a separate event — the point is that the site had no way to tell the difference. The lessons remain unglamorous but valid: remove shared-session remote access tools from control workstations in favour of brokered access with individual accounts and MFA; ensure that safety-critical setpoints have engineering limits enforced at the controller, not just the HMI; and keep the alarm and interlock layer independent of the SCADA layer so that a compromised HMI cannot silently defeat the process safeguards. If your site cannot answer 'who was logged into that HMI at 13:32 last Tuesday' with confidence, Oldsmar is still your incident to learn from.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The most directly OT-relevant story this week comes from NMFTA researchers, who revealed that a safety recall on the Bendix EC80 heavy-truck brake controller quietly also patched remote code execution and denial-of-service vulnerabilities. The disclosure is a reminder that vehicle and mobile-equipment ECUs share the same embedded-code weaknesses seen in fixed plant PLCs, and that safety recalls are increasingly the delivery vehicle for silent security fixes. Fleet operators and any engineers touching J1939 or CAN-connected assets should treat recall notices as security patches and track them accordingly. On the enterprise-IT side that inevitably bleeds into OT, SecurityWeek covered a wave of stories worth noting: critical flaws in Belgian eID software used by banks and government agencies, a one-click Rovo AI vulnerability at Atlassian that exposed Confluence and Jira data, and continued cyberattacks on North Carolina port operations. The port incidents in particular are a data point for anyone running terminal automation, crane control, or logistics-adjacent SCADA — port disruption often propagates into scheduling, weighbridge, and yard-control systems even when the OT network itself is untouched. SANS ISC's guest diary on automated SSH actors compromising exposed hosts in 22 seconds is a useful gut-check for jump-server and engineering-workstation hygiene. If your remote-access bastion, historian, or Linux-based edge gateway is reachable from the internet with password auth enabled, assume it is being brute-forced continuously. Their coverage of the keyv/cacheable npm supply-chain worm is also worth reading if your team builds HMI dashboards, Node-RED flows, or any JavaScript-based operator tooling — the advice was counterintuitive: do not immediately revoke tokens, because doing so can lock you out of the forensic trail. Finally, Black Hat USA 2026 wrapped up with the usual flood of vendor announcements. Nothing seismic for pure ICS practitioners, but expect a renewed marketing push around AI-driven OT anomaly detection and asset discovery platforms in the coming weeks.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA's KEV additions over the past few weeks are heavy on perimeter and identity infrastructure that OT sites frequently depend on. Two entries stand out for controls engineers: CVE-2023-4346 in the KNX Protocol Connection Authorization mechanism, added 15 July, which lets an attacker exhaust the lockout counter and purge devices or lock the BCU key on unhardened installations — directly relevant to anyone running KNX for building automation, HVAC, or lighting integrated with plant utilities. If you have KNX gear, verify that the additional security options (KNX Secure) are enabled rather than relying on the default Option 1 authorization. The rest of the list is IT infrastructure that typically sits adjacent to OT DMZs: Cisco Secure FMC hard-coded password (CVE-2026-20316), Fortinet FortiOS and FortiSandbox command injection bugs, Check Point SmartConsole authentication bypass, Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator OS command injection, and two N-able N-central authentication bypasses where the second was an incomplete patch of the first. Any of these on the IT/OT boundary is a fast path into Purdue Level 3 and below. Progress LoadMaster and JetBrains TeamCity round out the list with unauthenticated RCE — TeamCity in particular is worth checking if your engineering group uses it to build firmware or HMI images, since a compromised build server is a supply-chain event. Practical action this week: cross-check your firewall, VPN concentrator, and remote-management inventory against these CVEs, and specifically confirm KNX Secure is enforced on any building-automation segments that touch process utilities.Read more →
