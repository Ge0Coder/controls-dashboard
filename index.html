Today's Knowledge Bite

The 4-20 mA current loop standard that still dominates process instrumentation was chosen partly because 4 mA — not zero — is the live-zero: any broken wire, corroded terminal, or dead transmitter reads 0 mA and is immediately distinguishable from a legitimate low-scale measurement. That single design decision, made in the 1950s, is arguably the earliest widely-deployed example of what we'd now call 'fail-safe by protocol design' in industrial systems.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was formalised not by an academic but by Nicolas Minorsky in 1922, while analysing the automatic steering of the USS New Mexico. He derived the three-term structure by watching how experienced helmsmen actually steered — they anticipated the ship's inertia (derivative), corrected for persistent wind offset (integral), and reacted to immediate heading error (proportional). Nearly every control loop running in your plant today is, in effect, a mathematical abstraction of a human sailor's intuition from over a century ago.

Tech Spotlight

Deterministic Ethernet via Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) continues its slow march into plant floors, and OPC UA over TSN is finally moving from trade-show demos to real installations. The premise is straightforward: run motion control, safety, and business data over a single converged Ethernet fabric, with TSN's traffic scheduling (IEEE 802.1Qbv) guaranteeing microsecond-level delivery for the packets that need it while best-effort traffic uses the same wires. For controls engineers, that means you could in principle replace EtherCAT, PROFINET IRT, and standard Ethernet segments with one network — reducing cabling, switches, and the number of protocol stacks your team needs to maintain. The practical reality is messier. TSN requires every switch in the path to support the relevant sub-standards, and vendor interoperability — despite years of Avnu Alliance certification work — is still inconsistent between silicon generations. Configuring the TSN schedule itself is non-trivial; most engineers will rely on tooling from Siemens, B&R, or Rockwell rather than hand-crafting Qbv gate control lists. There's also a security angle: converging OT and IT traffic on one fabric means your VLAN segmentation and switch hardening become safety-relevant, not just IT concerns. Adoption today is concentrated in high-speed packaging, semiconductor tooling, and automotive body shops where the motion-control determinism justifies the complexity. If you're spec'ing new greenfield lines in the next 18 months, it's worth at least ensuring the switches you buy are TSN-capable so you're not locked out later — but don't retrofit a working PROFINET line just because TSN is fashionable.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) continues to be the standard most often cited in procurement documents, and the 2020 edition remains current — but the IEC 62443-2-1 revision covering security program requirements for asset owners was updated in 2024 and is now being referenced in more RFPs, particularly in Europe under NIS2. If your organisation's OT security program still points to the 2010 edition of 2-1, that reference is out of date and the new document is materially different in structure, moving toward a maturity-model approach aligned with ISO 27001. On the NIST side, SP 800-82 Rev 3 (Guide to OT Security) has been the de facto US federal reference since late 2023 and increasingly appears in insurance questionnaires. It expanded scope beyond ICS to include building automation, transportation systems, and IIoT, which matters if you're responsible for facility systems that historically fell between IT and OT ownership. Worth noting: Rev 3 explicitly maps its controls to IEC 62443 and the NIST CSF, so aligning to one increasingly gives you credit toward the others. For engineers writing functional specifications, the practical takeaway is to cite specific parts and editions (e.g. 'IEC 62443-4-2:2019 SL-2') rather than blanket references to '62443 compliance,' which are effectively meaningless. Vendors will happily claim compliance to the parts that suit their product and stay silent on the ones that don't.Read more →

Incident of the Week

In early 2024, a US municipal water utility in Muleshoe, Texas experienced an OT intrusion attributed to the Russia-aligned group CyberArmyofRussia_Reborn, in which attackers manipulated HMI setpoints causing a water tower to overflow before staff noticed and switched to manual control. The initial access vector was a remotely accessible HMI with weak or default credentials exposed to the internet — the same pattern seen in the earlier Oldsmar, Florida incident and dozens of unreported near-misses since. No customers lost service and no contamination occurred, but tens of thousands of gallons were lost and the utility had to run in manual mode for days while forensics and remediation completed. The intrusion succeeded despite the site having a functional SCADA system and a nominally segmented network, because the HMI was published through a remote-access product configured for convenience rather than security. Once inside, the attackers had full operator-equivalent privileges — there was no separate authentication between 'view' and 'control' actions, and no rate-limiting on setpoint changes. Three lessons remain directly applicable. First, inventory every path from the internet to an HMI or engineering workstation, including vendor remote-support tools you didn't install yourself. Second, implement physical or logical interlocks that make abnormal setpoint excursions require a second action — a keyswitch, a supervisor login, or a hard-coded rate limit in the PLC logic. Third, alarm on the operator action itself, not just the process consequence: a setpoint change from an unusual source at 3 AM should page someone regardless of whether the tank has yet overflowed.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The dominant story today is an unauthenticated root RCE in Cisco Secure Email Gateway (CVE-2026-76461), already under active exploitation and added to CISA's KEV within 24 hours. While email gateways aren't process-control kit, most plants rely on corporate mail relays that sit adjacent to the IT/OT DMZ and often carry vendor notifications, alarm digests, and remote-support workflows. If your SEG is a Cisco appliance, treat this as urgent: patch, isolate the management interface, and hunt for anomalous outbound connections from the appliance itself. Apple's annual OS refresh (iOS 27 and macOS 'Golden Gate' 27) closes 261 vulnerabilities, several kernel-level. Controls engineers increasingly carry iPads for HMI mirroring, mobile alarm apps, and vendor commissioning tools — patch these fleet devices before they touch the plant network. In parallel, Japan's Digital Agency disclosed a 240,000-record breach traced to a vulnerable VPN product, a familiar pattern that keeps proving that unpatched remote-access concentrators remain the single most reliable initial-access vector into sensitive environments, including OT. On the AI front, Microsoft published a 'Humanist AI Code of Conduct' attempting to formalise where defensive AI research ends and offensive capability begins — worth reading if your organisation is piloting AI copilots against historian or MES data. SANS also flagged a novel supply-chain abuse where an autonomous agent is harvesting stolen LLM API keys and reselling inference capacity; expect this pattern to appear in phishing lures aimed at engineering staff whose accounts have generous cloud AI entitlements. Finally, the ClickFix social-engineering technique continues to spread, this time via a compromised HBO Max Reddit account pushing malware to both Windows and macOS. CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added an unusually heavy batch of KEV entries in the last week, dominated by perimeter and identity infrastructure that OT environments rely on for remote access. The Cisco SEG root RCE (CVE-2026-76461) and Cisco FMC/SCC authentication bypass (CVE-2026-20079) are the highest-priority items — both are unauthenticated, network-reachable, and frequently deployed at the IT/OT boundary. Citrix NetScaler (CVE-2026-19490) and Fortinet FortiOS/FortiSASE (CVE-2025-25249) round out a bad week for SSL VPN concentrators; if your remote engineering access still terminates on one of these, assume compromise until you can verify patch level and review authentication logs. Two entries deserve special attention for OT teams. MikroTik RouterOS picked up two KEVs (CVE-2026-86060 privilege escalation and CVE-2026-67277 btest kernel disclosure) — MikroTik gear shows up frequently in cellular gateways and small-site industrial routers where patching cadence is weak. ConnectWise ScreenConnect (CVE-2026-84869) allows file transfer and execution across active remote sessions without host confirmation, a scenario that directly threatens integrators and OEMs who use ScreenConnect for plant support. Also note N-able N-central (CVE-2026-86218) pre-auth RCE, relevant if your MSP touches OT assets. For remediation, prioritise anything internet-facing first (Cisco SEG, NetScaler, Fortinet, MikroTik, N-central), then remote-support tooling (ScreenConnect), then internal identity/artifact stores (JFrog Artifactory has three separate KEVs this cycle, suggesting sustained attacker interest).
