Today's Knowledge Bite

The original Modbus protocol, designed by Modicon in 1979 for the 984 PLC, used a 7-bit ASCII mode before RTU binary mode was added — and the spec is still officially maintained as a free, open standard nearly 50 years later. That longevity is also why it has no authentication: when it was designed, the threat model was a noisy factory floor, not a hostile network. Every modern "secure Modbus" variant is essentially trying to retrofit trust onto a protocol that predates TCP/IP.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronic computing by decades — Elmer Sperry's gyrocompass-based ship autopilots in the 1910s used mechanical proportional-integral-derivative action, and Nicholas Minorsky formalised the maths in 1922 by observing how a helmsman actually steered a ship. Minorsky noticed the helmsman didn't just react to current heading error (P), but also to how long the error had persisted (I) and how fast it was changing (D). Every loop you tune today is a direct descendant of one man watching a sailor at a wheel and writing down what he saw.

Tech Spotlight

Today's spotlight is on OPC UA FX (Field eXchange), the controller-to-controller extension of OPC UA that's now moving from spec to silicon. Unlike classic OPC UA, which assumed a client-server hierarchy from PLC up to SCADA, OPC UA FX defines publisher-subscriber semantics and deterministic behaviour suitable for PLC-to-PLC and PLC-to-drive communication — the territory historically owned by PROFINET IRT, EtherCAT, and EtherNet/IP. Combined with Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) at layer 2, it promises a vendor-neutral, deterministic fieldbus over standard Ethernet. The practical implication for controls engineers is significant but not immediate. Siemens, Rockwell, Beckhoff, B&R, and Schneider have all demonstrated interoperability at Hannover Messe over the past three years, and first-generation products are shipping, but the ecosystem of I/O blocks, drives, and safety devices remains thin compared to PROFINET or EtherCAT. Expect to see OPC UA FX appear first in greenfield hybrid lines (especially automotive and battery manufacturing) where multi-vendor cells are the norm, before it filters down to general machinery. Adoption challenges are real: TSN requires managed switches with proper scheduling support, configuration tooling is still maturing, and diagnostic workflows differ markedly from established fieldbuses. If you're scoping a project for 2027 onwards, it's worth asking your integrator what their OPC UA FX roadmap looks like — but for a refit on an existing line, sticking with your current fieldbus is still the safer bet.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443 continues to expand into product-specific territory. IEC 62443-4-2, which defines technical security requirements for components (embedded devices, host devices, network devices, software applications), is now the de facto certification target for new PLCs, RTUs, and gateways — and increasingly an explicit requirement in tenders from European utilities and major asset owners. If you're specifying new equipment, ask vendors for their 62443-4-2 Security Level (SL) certification rather than accepting generic "62443 compliant" claims, which usually mean the manufacturer's processes (4-1) but not the product itself. On the framework side, NIST SP 800-82 Revision 3 remains the most useful free reference for OT-specific risk management and pairs well with the IEC 62443 zones-and-conduits model. For engineers writing or reviewing functional specs, the practical takeaway is to define security requirements in the same document as your I/O list and control philosophy — retrofitting SL-2 or SL-3 capabilities after FAT is painful and expensive. Also worth tracking: the ISA/IEC 62443-3-2 risk assessment methodology is being incorporated by reference into several national OT regulations, including the EU NIS2 implementing acts. Documented zone-and-conduit diagrams with target SLs are becoming an audit expectation, not a nice-to-have.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's case study is the 2024 American Water Works cyber incident, which remains instructive as related forensic detail continues to emerge in industry reporting. American Water, the largest regulated water utility in the US, detected unauthorised activity in October 2024 that forced it to shut down its customer portal and pause billing. Crucially, the company stated that water and wastewater operations were not impacted — the intrusion was contained to the corporate IT environment. That containment was not luck; it reflected segmentation between business systems (billing, customer service, SAP) and the SCADA networks running treatment plants and distribution. What happened, in essence: attackers gained access to corporate systems, likely via credential compromise or a public-facing application, and the utility's incident response playbook called for proactive isolation of customer-facing services while OT remained on its protected side of the Purdue boundary. Public reporting suggests no PLC, HMI, or SCADA system was touched. The lessons for controls engineers are concrete. First, the IT/OT boundary is your single most valuable security control — invest in proving it works through tabletop exercises, not just diagrams. Second, business-side outages can still cripple operations indirectly (no billing, no customer contact, regulatory reporting delays), so OT teams should plan for degraded-mode operation when corporate systems are unavailable. Third, segmentation is only as good as the documented data flows crossing it; every historian replication, AV update path, and remote vendor tunnel is a potential bypass that needs explicit review.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

CISA issued a busy slate of ICS advisories today. The standout is ScadaBR 1.2.0, with four CVEs including missing authentication on critical functions enabling unauthenticated remote code execution (CVSS 9.1). Any plant using this open-source SCADA package should isolate it immediately, restrict network exposure, and plan migration — there is no patched release yet that fully addresses the chain. Equally urgent is the Siemens RUGGEDCOM APE1808 advisory: the device hosts Palo Alto PAN-OS, and the User-ID/Captive Portal buffer overflow (CVE-2026-0300, already on CISA's KEV list since 6 May) permits unauthenticated root RCE. If you run RUGGEDCOM APE1808 as a perimeter or cell-zone firewall, treat it as an active incident. ABB CoreSense HM and M10 transformer monitoring units have a path traversal flaw allowing unauthenticated access to restricted directories and full compromise — relevant to utilities and any substation using these for DGA/bushing monitoring. Updates are available and should be staged in. Siemens also issued a multi-product industrial device advisory covering a DoS vulnerability across a broad family; check the CSAF list for your installed base. Kieback & Peter DDC4000-series building controllers (≤1.12.14) carry a browser-takeover flaw via CVE-2026-4293 — primarily a risk for engineers using web HMIs on shared workstations. ZKTeco CCTV cameras round out the list with an authentication bypass leaking credentials. On the broader supply-chain front, SANS ISC reports the TeamPCP campaign has escalated significantly: a confirmed compromise of the Checkmarx Jenkins plugin and a self-spreading Mini Shai-Hulud worm now propagating across npm and PyPI. While these primarily threaten IT/devops, any engineering workstation pulling Python libraries for data historians, MQTT clients, or PLC tooling (pycomm3, snap7, opcua-asyncio, etc.) is in scope. Pin dependencies, verify hashes, and avoid `pip install` from engineering laptops connected to the OT network. SecurityWeek also notes Microsoft patched two exploited zero-days (UnDefend and RedSun Defender) and rolled out a mitigation for the YellowKey BitLocker bypass. Engineering workstations and HMI PCs running Windows should be in this month's patch cycle.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA's KEV additions this week heavily favour engineering-workstation and remote-access exposure. CVE-2026-0300 (Palo Alto PAN-OS Captive Portal, unauth root RCE) is the most consequential for OT — it directly underpins today's Siemens RUGGEDCOM APE1808 advisory, meaning a firewall meant to protect your cell/area zone may itself be the entry point. Apply Palo Alto's fix and Siemens' interim countermeasures (disable Captive Portal where not needed, restrict management plane access) immediately. Also notable: Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Controller authentication bypass (CVE-2026-20182), Ivanti EPMM authenticated RCE (CVE-2026-6973), and a fresh batch of old Microsoft chestnuts (CVE-2008-4250 Conficker-era SMB, plus 2009–2010 IE/Acrobat bugs) re-added on 20 May — almost certainly because they're still being found unpatched in long-lived OT environments running legacy Windows. If you still operate XP/Server 2003/Win7 HMIs, this is your reminder that MS08-067 is still being exploited in 2026. The SimpleHelp and ConnectWise ScreenConnect entries (CVE-2024-57726/57728, CVE-2024-1708) are a direct warning for any site using these for vendor remote support — a common pattern for OEM service contracts.
