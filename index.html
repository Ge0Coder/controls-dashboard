Today's Knowledge Bite

The Lantronix-style serial device server, which keeps appearing in KEV entries, traces its lineage to the early 1990s when factories needed to keep using PLCs and instruments built around RS-232 and RS-485 even as Ethernet took over the plant. Three decades later, a substantial share of Modbus RTU and DF1 traffic in operating plants still passes through these little black boxes — meaning a command injection in a $200 device can sit directly in the control path of equipment worth millions.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was effectively codified not in a university lab but on a US Navy ship. Nicolas Minorsky published his analysis of automatic ship steering in 1922 after watching helmsmen on the USS New Mexico, and he realised they were unconsciously applying proportional, integral, and derivative actions — anticipating the ship's swing, not just reacting to it. Nearly every loop you tune today descends from one engineer carefully watching sailors turn a wheel.

Tech Spotlight

Today's spotlight is on OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking), which is finally moving from trade-show demos into real deployments. The promise is straightforward: a single Ethernet network that carries both deterministic, sub-millisecond control traffic (the kind that today rides on Profinet IRT, EtherCAT, or Sercos) and best-effort IT traffic like historian data, video, and engineering tools, with cryptographic identity and authorisation built in via OPC UA's security model. The TSN side handles the timing guarantees through IEEE 802.1Qbv scheduled traffic, frame preemption, and precise clock sync via 802.1AS. For controls engineers, the practical implication is the eventual collapse of the fieldbus zoo. Instead of choosing Profinet vs. EtherNet/IP vs. EtherCAT based on which vendor's drives you bought, you would specify a TSN-capable switch infrastructure and rely on OPC UA PubSub for both controller-to-controller and controller-to-device communication. Siemens, Rockwell, B&R, and Beckhoff have all shipped pieces of this, and the OPC Foundation's Field Level Communications initiative has matured the companion specifications. The adoption barriers are real: TSN switches remain expensive and configuration tooling is immature, engineering practice for end-to-end stream reservation isn't standardised across vendors, and certified TSN-capable I/O is still thin on the ground. Expect the next two to three years to be dominated by greenfield motion-control and semiconductor-fab deployments before TSN reaches general-purpose process and discrete plants.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443 continues to be the most consequential standards activity for controls engineers, and the 62443-2-1:2024 revision — covering security program requirements for asset owners — is now propagating into procurement language. If you write specifications, expect to see more requests for evidence of a documented Cybersecurity Management System (CSMS), not just product certifications. The shift is meaningful: vendors can ship 62443-4-2 certified components, but if the asset owner cannot demonstrate 2-1 program maturity, the certified components do not actually deliver assured risk reduction. On the NIST side, the SP 800-82r3 guide on OT security remains the practical reference for mapping IT-style controls into ICS contexts, and it pairs cleanly with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 'Govern' function — useful when you need to explain to plant management why patch cadence and remote-access governance are board-level concerns. For incident reporting, US asset owners should also be tracking CIRCIA reporting timelines as final rules continue to bed in; covered entities will need to report substantial cyber incidents within 72 hours and ransomware payments within 24. Worth noting for those working with safety systems: ISA TR84.00.09, which addresses cybersecurity for safety instrumented systems, is the document to reach for when arguing that a SIS must be independent of the BPCS not only functionally but also from a network and credentials standpoint. It is one of the few documents that crisply explains why a 'flat' control network is a safety problem, not just a security one.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's case study is the 2024 SonicWall CVE-2024-40766 saga, resurfaced by SANS ISC's diary noting that the patch fixed the bug but nobody fixed the configuration. The original flaw allowed unauthenticated access to SSL VPN resources on SonicOS appliances. Many organisations applied the firmware patch promptly, but the local user accounts and pre-existing passwords on those appliances — which had been exposed during the vulnerable window — were left unchanged. Ransomware affiliates, notably Akira, spent the following year quietly logging into 'patched' firewalls with valid credentials and pivoting straight into flat OT-adjacent networks. The mechanics matter to controls engineers because these SonicWall appliances are extremely common as the perimeter device in front of small-to-midsize plants, water utilities, and remote sites. Once inside, the attackers found minimal east-west segmentation between the corporate VPN landing zone and engineering workstations, RDP jump hosts, and HMI servers. In several documented cases, ransomware reached historians and engineering laptops within hours. Three lessons: first, treat every credential that existed on a vulnerable device during its exposure window as compromised — rotate them as part of patching, not as a separate project. Second, perimeter patches do not substitute for internal segmentation; a Purdue Level 3.5 DMZ exists precisely so that a compromised VPN does not equal a compromised plant. Third, audit your remote-access appliances for orphaned local accounts left by integrators and former staff; these are the accounts attackers prefer because nobody monitors their logins.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The headline development for plant-floor and manufacturing IT teams this week is CISA's confirmation that PTC Windchill and FlexPLM are being actively exploited in the wild via CVE-2026-12569, an unauthenticated remote code execution flaw. Windchill is the system of record for product structures, CAD data, BOMs, and manufacturing process plans at a huge number of discrete manufacturers, so a compromise here is not abstract — it can mean tampered work instructions, leaked IP, or a pivot point into MES and shop-floor networks. Patch immediately and audit Windchill's external exposure, including any reverse-proxied access used by suppliers. On the geopolitical side, Russia's Turla group has been observed deploying a new backdoor called StockStay against Ukrainian government and military targets. While not directly an ICS payload, Turla has a long track record of pivoting from IT footholds into operationally significant systems, and the tooling typically resurfaces in criminal ecosystems within months. Operators of critical infrastructure in NATO countries should treat StockStay IOCs as relevant once published. In the AI/integration space, the Model Context Protocol (MCP) has been overhauled into an enterprise-ready specification. The relevance for OT is that MCP is increasingly the glue connecting AI assistants to engineering tools, historians, and document repositories. The new spec pushes more security responsibility down to developers and integrators — which in OT environments often means controls engineers running pilot deployments without a clear security baseline. SANS ISC also flagged a useful operational reminder via CVE-2024-40766 (SonicWall): the patch fixed the bug, but a year later organisations are still being breached because nobody reset the local user credentials that were already exposed pre-patch. That pattern — patch applied, residual configuration debt ignored — is endemic in OT environments where firewalls and remote-access appliances sit in DMZs between corporate and process networks.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

This week's KEV additions are heavy on edge and management-plane devices that frequently sit on the IT/OT boundary. PTC Windchill (CVE-2026-12569) is the most directly OT-relevant: unauthenticated RCE in a manufacturing PLM, now confirmed exploited. Lantronix EDS5000 (CVE-2025-67038) is squarely an OT device — a serial-to-Ethernet device server used to bring legacy RS-232/485 equipment onto IP networks — and the flaw allows root-level OS command injection via the username field. If you have EDS5000s bridging legacy drives, meters, or RTUs, treat this as urgent; these devices rarely get patched on schedule and are often reachable from engineering VLANs. The Ubiquiti UniFi OS trio (CVE-2026-34908/34909/34910) matters because UniFi gear has quietly proliferated in small plants, remote pump stations, and substations as a cheap managed-network option. Combined command injection, path traversal, and access-control bypass on the controller is effectively a full network takeover. Cisco Unified CM (SSRF) and Catalyst SD-WAN Manager (two entries) round out the management-plane theme — SD-WAN orchestrators in particular are increasingly used to stitch together multi-site OT WANs, and a compromised vManage is a compromised network fabric. Practical remediation priorities: patch Windchill this weekend if exposed; inventory and segment Lantronix and UniFi devices off engineering networks; verify SD-WAN Manager is not reachable from plant networks; and for Check Point CVE-2026-50751 (IKEv1 auth bypass), confirm remote-access VPNs used by vendors and integrators have been updated and that IKEv1 is disabled where possible in favour of IKEv2.Read more →
