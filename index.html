Today's Knowledge Bite

The original PID controller equations predate electronics entirely — Nicolas Minorsky formalised three-term control in 1922 while analysing how the USS New Mexico's helmsmen steered the ship. He observed that experienced helmsmen didn't just react to heading error but also to how fast the error was changing and how long it had persisted, which is exactly proportional, derivative and integral action. Every PID block in your DCS today is a descendant of watching sailors turn a wheel.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The centrifugal flyball governor that James Watt popularised on steam engines in 1788 is often called the first automatic feedback controller, but it's also arguably the first control system whose stability was analysed mathematically — by James Clerk Maxwell in his 1868 paper "On Governors." Maxwell showed that as governors were made more sensitive to improve regulation, engines began to hunt and oscillate, and he derived the conditions on the characteristic equation roots that would guarantee stability. That paper is the direct ancestor of the Routh-Hurwitz criterion you probably learned as an undergraduate, meaning the entire field of classical control theory started because Victorian engines wouldn't stop wobbling.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues to inch toward real deployment, and it's worth understanding what it actually promises versus what's shipping today. The core idea is to unify what have historically been two separate networks in a plant — the deterministic fieldbus (Profinet IRT, EtherCAT, Sercos III) and the information layer (Ethernet/IP, OPC UA over TCP) — onto a single standard Ethernet infrastructure where TSN provides bounded latency and jitter through IEEE 802.1Qbv time-aware scheduling, and OPC UA PubSub provides the semantic data model above it. For a controls engineer this matters because it eventually means one cable plant, one set of managed switches, and one addressing scheme carrying motion control traffic, safety data (via OPC UA Safety), and MES/historian traffic simultaneously with guaranteed timing for the deterministic classes. Vendor support is real but uneven: Siemens, B&R, Beckhoff, Rockwell and others have demonstrated interop, but the tooling for configuring TSN schedules across a mixed-vendor network is still immature, and troubleshooting a misconfigured gate control list is nothing like troubleshooting a Profinet topology. The practical adoption blockers today are switch cost (TSN-capable managed switches remain a premium item), the shortage of engineers who understand both networking and motion control deeply enough to commission these systems, and the fact that most brownfield sites simply don't need the convergence badly enough to justify a rip-and-replace. Expect to see it first in greenfield semiconductor, EV battery, and high-speed packaging lines where the motion determinism plus data density genuinely pays back.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443 continues to be the reference standard most frequently cited in OT procurement specifications, and the -4-2 component requirements are now regularly appearing as pass/fail criteria in RFQs for PLCs, drives, and network equipment. If you're specifying new equipment, asking for a specific Security Level target (SL-C 2 is a reasonable baseline for most process plants, SL-C 3 for higher-consequence systems) and requiring the vendor to provide the certification evidence rather than just marketing claims is now standard practice. Note that certification is per-component and per-version — a certified PLC family doesn't mean the specific firmware you're deploying carries the same coverage. On the framework side, NIST SP 800-82 Revision 3 remains the practical reference for OT risk management and continues to be the document most auditors will cite when assessing US critical infrastructure programs. It aligns cleanly with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0's Govern function, which formalised what many OT programs were already doing informally — assigning executive accountability for OT cyber risk rather than leaving it stranded between plant operations and corporate IT. Worth flagging for European readers: the NIS2 transposition deadlines have passed in most member states, and enforcement is now the phase we're in. The scope covers substantially more manufacturing sub-sectors than NIS1 did, and the personal liability for management bodies has real teeth. If your organisation is still treating NIS2 as an IT compliance project rather than an OT governance issue, that's a gap worth escalating.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the 2021 Oldsmar, Florida water treatment intrusion, because it keeps being cited in policy discussions but the technical lessons are often mangled. What happened: an unauthorised remote user connected to a plant HMI via TeamViewer (which the plant used for legitimate remote support) and briefly changed the sodium hydroxide setpoint from around 100 ppm to 11,100 ppm. An operator watching the screen saw the cursor move, reverted the change within minutes, and multiple downstream controls (pH monitoring, dosing limits) would have caught the change before it reached consumers. How it happened: shared TeamViewer credentials across staff, no MFA, the workstation was running Windows 7, and remote access was not brokered through a jump host with logging. The initial access vector was almost certainly credential reuse or brute force against the exposed TeamViewer instance. Notably, later reporting suggested the "cursor moving" may have been another operator rather than an attacker, but the underlying security gaps were absolutely real regardless of whether this specific event was malicious. Three lessons that still apply: first, remote access to HMIs must go through a brokered, MFA-protected, session-recorded path — never direct RDP or vendor remote-support tools exposed to the internet. Second, engineered safeguards (chemical dosing limits configured in the PLC logic itself, independent pH monitoring with alarming) are what actually prevented consumer harm, which is the entire argument for defense-in-depth beyond the network layer. Third, shared credentials in OT environments are still endemic and are the single easiest thing to fix this quarter if you haven't already.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

This week's most relevant story for plant floor engineers is the disclosure of refrigeration system vulnerabilities highlighted in SecurityWeek's roundup. Industrial refrigeration controllers (common in food and beverage, cold storage, pharmaceutical and chemical facilities) frequently sit on flat networks with weak segmentation and are often exposed to remote maintenance vendors. Vulnerabilities in these systems can translate directly into product loss, regulatory non-compliance around cold-chain integrity, and in ammonia-based systems, safety consequences. Engineers running Danfoss, Copeland, Bitzer or comparable controllers should be checking vendor advisories and confirming these devices are behind properly configured firewalls rather than reachable via port-forwards or misconfigured VPNs. August Patch Tuesday from Microsoft was unusually large at 418 fixes, including 62 critical vulnerabilities and one being actively exploited. Of particular interest for OT are the QUIC and DNS Server remote code execution bugs, and Windows privilege escalation flaws that will inevitably touch engineering workstations, historians, and HMI servers running on Windows Server. Plan your patch validation cycles now, since these will need to move through your test environment before production windows open. On the aviation side, DEF CON produced the usual mix of headline-grabbing research, including work on Boeing 737 systems and reports of a DEF CON attendee blamed for a Delta flight disruption. While aviation isn't ICS in the traditional sense, the research techniques against embedded avionics closely mirror those used against safety-instrumented systems and PLCs, and are worth watching. Separately, Google Cloud published a post-quantum cryptography roadmap targeting 2029 readiness, which is a useful reference point for OT vendors and asset owners planning the eventual migration of long-lived field devices whose firmware crypto libraries will outlive most current PQC transition plans. Finally, the Trivy/LiteLLM supply chain story is a reminder that most affected organisations were exposed before the malicious packages were even published, which reinforces the argument for tight controls on what dependencies your OT-adjacent tooling (MES integrations, data historian connectors, custom Python scripts on engineering laptops) is allowed to pull in.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The most operationally significant KEV additions this fortnight are Cisco Secure Firewall ASA/FTD (CVE-2026-20349, a heap inspection DoS) and Cisco Secure Firewall Management Center (CVE-2026-20316, a hard-coded password allowing unauthenticated low-privilege login). Cisco ASA and FTD are extremely common as the perimeter between IT and OT DMZs, and any DoS on that boundary either creates production disruption or tempts engineers into unsafe bypass configurations. The FMC hard-coded credential is arguably worse because compromise of the management plane means an attacker can reshape your entire firewall ruleset. Prioritise these on your change control queue and audit FMC access logs for the low-privilege account used by the vulnerability. Also notable for OT-adjacent environments: Fortinet FortiOS (CVE-2025-68686) around symlink persistence bypass, Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator (CVE-2026-16812) command injection, and Progress LoadMaster (CVE-2026-8037) unauthenticated command injection. Any of these sitting in front of remote sites, SD-WAN connected plants, or vendor jump environments should be patched immediately. The recurring N-able N-central authentication bypasses (CVE-2026-18556 and its incomplete-patch sibling CVE-2026-18577) are relevant if your MSP or internal IT uses N-central to manage OT-adjacent Windows assets — worth a direct question to your provider today. The Microsoft SharePoint deserialisation flaw (CVE-2026-50522) and Windows AFD.sys use-after-free (CVE-2026-68820) round out the list of items likely to affect Level 3 systems in Purdue-model environments.
