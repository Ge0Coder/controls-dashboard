Today's Knowledge Bite

The Modbus protocol, still the lingua franca of industrial devices in 2026, was designed in 1979 for Modicon PLCs communicating over RS-232 at 9600 baud. Its 16-bit register addressing and function codes have survived nearly five decades essentially unchanged, migrating from serial to TCP with no cryptographic authentication ever added to the base standard. Every time you troubleshoot a Modbus/TCP link today, you're using a protocol older than the IBM PC.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronics: Nicolas Minorsky derived the three-term control law in 1922 while studying automatic ship steering for the US Navy, by watching how experienced helmsmen actually turned the wheel — they applied correction proportional to the heading error, integrated persistent bias, and anticipated the ship's rate of turn. His paper 'Directional Stability of Automatically Steered Bodies' essentially reverse-engineered human intuition into mathematics, and every temperature loop you tune today is a descendant of a 1920s naval experiment. The derivative term, notoriously, is still the one most operators disable — just as skeptical helmsmen did a century ago.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues to inch from marketing decks into actual deployments in 2026, particularly in automotive body shops and semiconductor fabs where deterministic Ethernet is displacing PROFINET IRT and EtherCAT for new lines. The pitch is compelling: a single converged network carrying deterministic control traffic (sub-millisecond cycle times, bounded jitter) alongside video, HMI polling and MES data, all using open standards rather than vendor-specific hardware. Under the hood, IEEE 802.1Qbv time-aware scheduling reserves transmit windows for control frames, while OPC UA Pub/Sub with the UADP binary encoding provides the payload semantics. The practical challenges are less glamorous. TSN requires end-to-end support: every switch, every NIC, and precise time synchronisation via PTP (IEEE 1588) with grandmaster redundancy. Mixing TSN-capable and legacy switches in a ring will silently break determinism. Engineering tools are still maturing — configuring stream reservations across a multi-vendor network often means hand-editing YANG models or wrestling with each vendor's proprietary orchestrator. Most greenfield adopters report that the cabling and switch cost premium is modest, but the commissioning time is not, and troubleshooting requires new skills (wireshark with gPTP dissectors, TSN stream analyzers) that most controls teams don't yet have. Standards Watch

IEC 62443-4-2 (component security requirements) continues to see rising adoption as a procurement gate, with several large end-users in oil & gas and power generation now requiring 4-2 SL2 certification for new PLCs and RTUs in tender documents. Vendors including Siemens, Rockwell, Schneider and ABB have expanded their certified product catalogues over the last year, but engineers should read the certificates carefully — the certified configuration is often narrower than the shipping default (specific firmware version, specific communication modules, security features enabled). Buying a certified product and deploying it in a non-certified configuration gives you paperwork, not protection. On the framework side, NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 remains the reference for OT risk management in US federal and adjacent environments, and ISA is progressing work on 62443-2-2 (security program ratings) which aims to give operators a defensible way to score their program maturity against 62443 rather than relying on adjacent frameworks like NIST CSF. For engineers, the practical takeaway is that auditors are increasingly asking not just 'do you have segmentation' but 'can you evidence which zone and conduit each asset belongs to' — the zone-and-conduit model from 62443-3-2 is becoming the expected documentation artefact, not an optional exercise.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the 2021 Oldsmar, Florida water treatment near-miss, given its continued relevance to how remote access is architected on small utility sites. An operator at the plant noticed his mouse cursor moving on its own through the TeamViewer session used for remote support; the intruder briefly changed the sodium hydroxide setpoint from 100 ppm to 11,100 ppm before the operator reverted it. No harm reached the public because a human happened to be watching the HMI in real time — the engineered safeguards downstream would have caught it too, but that was luck of design, not intent. The root causes were mundane and depressingly common: a shared TeamViewer password reused across staff, no MFA, direct internet exposure of the remote access tool, and Windows 7 endpoints still in service. There was no jump host, no session recording, and no alerting on setpoint changes outside operational bands. Lessons for controls engineers in 2026 remain sharp: remote access to HMIs or engineering workstations must go through a brokered, MFA-protected jump host with session recording; safety-critical setpoints should have hard bounds enforced in the PLC logic itself, not just the HMI; and any change to a chemical dosing, pressure or temperature setpoint outside a defined range should raise an immediate alarm regardless of source. If your site's answer to 'how does the integrator connect after hours' is a vendor-branded remote tool with a shared password, you are running the Oldsmar architecture.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The week's most OT-relevant story is a fresh batch of refrigeration system vulnerabilities disclosed by researchers, following broader scrutiny of industrial cooling controllers used across food processing, cold-chain logistics and pharma. While the specific vendor details are still filtering out, engineers running Danfoss, Copeland or similar controllers should be checking for firmware advisories and re-verifying that these devices are not directly reachable from IT VLANs. Refrigeration is one of those quiet corners of OT where safety, spoilage risk and regulatory exposure (HACCP, GxP) intersect — a compromised setpoint is not just a nuisance. At DEF CON, researchers demonstrated attack paths against a Boeing 737, once again reminding transport and aerospace engineers that assumptions about air-gapped avionics networks age poorly. A separate DEF CON-linked incident is being blamed for a Delta flight disruption, and a North Korean IT worker reportedly breached a US federal agency — the latter reinforcing that insider-shaped supply chain risk is not hypothetical. If your integrators or MSPs hire remote contract engineers, revisit identity verification and privileged-access review. On the IT side that bleeds into OT, Microsoft's August Patch Tuesday shipped 418 CVEs including one exploited-in-the-wild bug and critical QUIC and DNS Server RCEs. Historian, MES and engineering workstations built on Windows Server will need attention in the next maintenance window. Google Cloud published a post-quantum cryptography roadmap targeting 2029 readiness — worth noting for anyone specifying long-lived OT PKI or VPN infrastructure today, because certificates issued now may still be in service when PQ migration becomes mandatory. Finally, the LiteLLM/Trivy story is a useful cautionary tale: initial reports blamed a malicious LiteLLM package, but the actual root cause was Trivy scanner exposure that pre-dated the packages. As OT teams increasingly adopt open-source vulnerability scanners and AI middleware in their engineering toolchains, this is a reminder to attribute incidents carefully before hardening the wrong thing.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA's KEV additions over the last two weeks lean heavily on network perimeter and management infrastructure — exactly the boxes that sit between corporate IT and plant networks. CVE-2026-20349 (Cisco ASA/FTD heap issue causing device reload) and CVE-2026-20316 (hard-coded password in Cisco FMC) are the standouts for OT, since many sites use ASA/FTD pairs as their IDMZ firewall. A reboot loop on that appliance during an active batch or continuous process is a control-network availability event, not just an IT problem. Fortinet FortiOS (CVE-2025-68686) and Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator (CVE-2026-16812, OS command injection) round out the perimeter concerns, and N-able N-central had two back-to-back auth bypasses (CVE-2026-18556 and its incomplete-patch sibling CVE-2026-18577) — relevant if your MSP manages plant assets through N-central. On the application side, JetBrains TeamCity (unauth RCE via agent protocol), Progress LoadMaster (unauth command injection), Metabase (SQL injection to admin), and both Langflow entries indicate active targeting of DevOps and AI/LLM tooling that has quietly crept into engineering environments. Two WordPress Core CVEs chain to RCE — check any plant or corporate-adjacent WordPress instances hosting documentation or dashboards. Practical action: prioritise patching or compensating controls on ASA/FTD and FMC this week, verify N-central is fully patched (both CVEs), and audit whether TeamCity, Metabase or Langflow instances exist anywhere in your engineering pipeline — they often arrive via data-science or integrator teams without formal review.Read more →
