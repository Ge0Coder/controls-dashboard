Today's Knowledge Bite

The first documented industrial control system compromise via an insider predates the internet: in 1982, a disgruntled operator at a Siberian gas pipeline allegedly triggered a massive explosion, though the more famous (and disputed) story involves CIA-planted trojanised SCADA software. What's less disputed is that insider-caused OT incidents still outnumber external nation-state attacks in most industry datasets — the Kemuri water plant, Maroochy Shire sewage, and now this week's extortion case all involved people with legitimate access.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller we take for granted was effectively invented twice, decades apart, for entirely different problems. Nicolas Minorsky derived the three-term controller in 1922 by watching helmsmen steer the USS New Mexico and noticing that good helmsmen anticipated the ship's rate of swing (derivative) and corrected for persistent crosswinds (integral) rather than just reacting to current heading error. The algorithm then sat largely unused in process industries until the 1940s, when pneumatic controller manufacturers like Taylor and Foxboro commercialised it — meaning the mathematical foundation of most of the loops running in your plant today came from a Russian-American naval engineer studying human behaviour, not from control theory.

Tech Spotlight

Deterministic Ethernet via Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) continues its slow march into process automation, and OPC UA over TSN is now shipping in production from several major vendors including Siemens, B&R, and Rockwell. The promise is compelling: a single converged network carrying hard real-time motion control traffic, standard process I/O, video, and enterprise data, with guaranteed latencies in the tens of microseconds and no need for separate Profinet IRT, EtherCAT, or Sercos rings. In practice, adoption on brownfield sites is slower than the marketing suggests. TSN requires managed switches that support IEEE 802.1Qbv scheduled traffic and 802.1AS time sync end-to-end — one unmanaged switch or legacy device breaks the determinism. Configuration tooling is still vendor-specific despite the standards, and troubleshooting a TSN schedule mismatch is considerably harder than diagnosing a Profinet update rate problem. For greenfield automotive, semiconductor, and packaging lines the economics already work; for refineries and water utilities, expect another three to five years before it displaces proven fieldbuses. Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) remains the standard most frequently misapplied in brownfield OT projects. The core concept — partitioning the system into zones and conduits, then assigning a target Security Level (SL-T) to each — sounds straightforward, but teams often skip the cyber risk assessment step and jump straight to SL-2 across the board because 'that's what the EPC spec says.' The standard actually requires a documented risk assessment per zone, with tolerable risk defined by the asset owner, before any SL-T is assigned. Also worth tracking: ISA/IEC 62443-2-1, which was substantially rewritten in 2024 to align with the Part 3 and Part 4 technical requirements, is now the reference for operator cybersecurity management systems and is increasingly cited by insurers and auditors. If your site's OT security program was built against the old 2010 edition, a gap analysis is overdue. On the US side, NIST SP 800-82r3 (Guide to OT Security) remains the most practical companion document for engineers who need to translate 62443 concepts into day-to-day procedures, particularly around patch management windows and change control for safety-related systems.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's insider extortion sentencing is the clearest teaching case we've had in a while. According to the reporting, the engineer — a core infrastructure engineer at an industrial firm — used his legitimate credentials to delete administrator accounts, reset hundreds of passwords (locking out colleagues and automation service accounts), and then demanded 20 bitcoin to restore access. He is now in federal prison. While details on whether process control systems were directly affected are limited, the attack pattern maps directly onto what a similarly placed OT administrator could do to a DCS domain, historian cluster, or engineering workstation farm. The mechanics are depressingly simple: a privileged user with unmonitored access and no peer review on destructive actions can cause catastrophic availability loss in minutes. There was apparently no four-eyes requirement for bulk password resets, no alerting on mass account deletion, and no offline break-glass credentials readily available to recover. Three lessons for controls engineers. First, inventory every account that can modify PLC programs, DCS configurations, or AD group memberships, and make sure at least two people must concur for destructive bulk operations. Second, keep genuinely offline credentials — printed, sealed, in a safe — for your domain controllers, engineering workstations, and critical network gear; cloud password managers do not help when the attacker has domain admin. Third, log privileged OT actions to a system the privileged users cannot reach: a one-way syslog feed to a security SIEM or even a simple append-only historian tag is better than nothing.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The week's most instructive story for controls engineers is the sentencing of a former core infrastructure engineer who deleted admin accounts, reset hundreds of passwords, and demanded 20 bitcoin to spare his employer's servers. This is a textbook insider-threat case and a reminder that privileged access management, break-glass accounts stored offline, and separation of duties are not bureaucratic overhead — they are the only controls that work when a trusted administrator turns hostile. Plant engineering teams, where one or two individuals often hold the keys to the entire DCS or historian environment, are especially exposed to this failure mode. On the IT-adjacent front, unpatched AhsayCBS backup server vulnerabilities (CVE-2026-105133/105134) are being actively exploited to bypass authentication and inject OS commands. Many OT sites use third-party backup appliances to protect engineering workstations and historians; if yours sits on a shared management VLAN, treat it as an urgent review item. Separately, Google ccTLD hijacks (.gh, .sl, .as) resulted in attacker-issued HTTPS certificates for Google domains — a reminder that certificate pinning and DNS integrity matter even for cloud-based OT tooling and remote-access brokers. The Midnight Mimosa campaign preloading malware onto budget Android devices in 150+ countries should prompt a quick look at any tablets or handhelds used on the plant floor for HMI mirroring, mobile operator rounds, or vendor service work. Low-cost devices procured outside standard IT channels frequently end up bridging corporate Wi-Fi and process networks. Finally, SANS ISC's reminder that TLP is not a substitute for internal information classification is worth passing on to any team sharing OT vulnerability data with integrators or vendors — TLP:AMBER on an email does not tell your contractor how to actually store a P&ID or network diagram.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA's KEV additions this week are heavy on edge and remote-access infrastructure that commonly fronts OT environments. Three Citrix NetScaler flaws (CVE-2026-88771, 88772, 88779) cover unauthenticated RCE, memory corruption, and DoS; Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager (CVE-2026-76504) allows unauthenticated admin access via URI hex-encoding abuse; Fortinet FortiMail (CVE-2026-104286) permits arbitrary file write pre-auth; and F5 BIG-IP APM (CVE-2026-94127) has an unauthenticated heap overflow when OAuth is configured. Any of these appliances sitting between enterprise and plant networks, or terminating vendor remote-access VPNs, should be patched this weekend and their session logs reviewed for anomalous admin activity. MikroTik RouterOS (CVE-2026-67279) is particularly relevant for smaller sites and remote pump/lift stations where MikroTik gear is often used as a cheap cellular edge router — the chained exploit yields unauthenticated code execution. Also notable is CISA back-filling several older vulnerabilities (BIND 2015, Struts 2016, ProFTPD 2015, ONLYOFFICE 2021) into the KEV, suggesting fresh exploitation activity against legacy systems.
