Today's Knowledge Bite

The SIMATIC S7-1500 MFP (Multi-Functional Platform) runs a hardened real-time PLC firmware and a general-purpose GNU/Linux subsystem side-by-side on the same CPU, allowing engineers to deploy C/C++ or containerized apps that exchange data with the control logic through shared memory. It's one of the few PLCs that intentionally hosts a Linux attack surface — which is why most of today's advisory concerns the Linux side, not the IEC 61131 runtime. For most process installations, that Linux subsystem is unnecessary and can be left disabled, dramatically reducing exposure.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronics by decades — Elmer Sperry's gyroscopic ship autopilots in the 1910s already implemented proportional-plus-derivative action using purely mechanical linkages and hydraulic amplifiers, and the US Navy's experiments with them during World War I produced the first empirical tuning rules. Nicholas Minorsky's 1922 mathematical analysis of ship steering was essentially a reverse-engineering of what helmsmen were doing intuitively: anticipating the ship's inertia. Ziegler and Nichols didn't publish their famous tuning rules until 1942, meaning engineers were successfully tuning PID loops for nearly thirty years before anyone wrote down a method for it.

Tech Spotlight

Virtual PLCs are quietly moving from vendor demos to production pilots. Siemens' SIMATIC S7-1500V, Rockwell's FactoryTalk Logix Echo-adjacent virtual controller work, Beckhoff's TwinCAT running in containers, and CODESYS Virtual Control all take the same IEC 61131 runtime that normally lives on dedicated hardware and run it inside a container or VM on commodity x86 or ARM compute. The pitch is lifecycle flexibility: spin up controllers on demand, patch the underlying OS without touching the engineering project, and consolidate many small cells onto a redundant edge server. The practical catch is determinism. A virtualized runtime depends on CPU pinning, hugepages, PREEMPT_RT kernels, and a well-behaved NIC with TSN or at least proper DPDK offload to hit sub-millisecond cycle times reliably. If your motion or safety loop needs 500µs jitter guarantees, a vPLC on a shared hypervisor is not there yet. Where it does shine today is batch, utilities, water, and discrete manufacturing with 10–50ms cycle budgets. The other elephant is cybersecurity surface. A vPLC inherits the attack surface of the host OS, the orchestrator (Kubernetes, Docker, or vendor-specific), and the virtual networking stack. The S7-1500 MFP advisories today are a preview of what vPLC advisories will routinely look like — Linux CVEs mixed with runtime CVEs — and asset owners will need to decide whether their change-control process can absorb monthly host-OS patching on a controller.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-4-2 certification is becoming a de facto procurement requirement in European utilities and large EPCs, driven partly by NIS2 enforcement that formally began biting across the EU in 2025. If you're specifying new controllers or RTUs in 2026, expect bid documents to require 4-2 Security Level 2 as a minimum, with SL3 for safety-adjacent components. The ISA Global Cybersecurity Alliance has been publishing component mapping guidance that helps engineers translate 4-2 CRs (Component Requirements) into concrete tendering language, which is worth reading before your next capital project. On the NIST side, SP 800-82 Revision 3 remains the practical touchstone for OT security program design in North America, and recent CISA cross-sector performance goals (CPGs) continue to align with it. For engineers, the useful bit is Appendix G's control overlays — they map NIST 800-53 controls to realistic ICS implementation guidance, including where a control simply cannot apply (e.g., MFA on a legacy HMI) and what compensating controls look like. Quieter but relevant: IEC 62443-3-2 (risk assessment and zone/conduit design) is increasingly cited in insurance underwriting questionnaires. Carriers want evidence that zones and conduits are documented and that risk ratings justify the chosen security levels. If you don't already have a zone-and-conduit diagram alongside your P&IDs and network diagrams, that's becoming an audit gap.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A worthwhile re-examination this week is the 2023 Aliquippa, Pennsylvania water authority incident, where the Cyber Av3ngers group compromised a Unitronics Vision series PLC controlling a booster station. The attackers didn't exploit a sophisticated zero-day — the PLC was internet-exposed with the default password "1111" on TCP/20256, and they defaced the HMI screen with a political message. Operators noticed because the HMI was unreachable and switched to manual control; no water quality impact occurred, but the station ran in manual for days. What happened technically: Unitronics PLCs expose a proprietary PCOM protocol by default, and many integrators left them port-forwarded for remote support. The attackers scanned Shodan, authenticated with the vendor default, and used legitimate PLC functions to write new display content and stop the program. The lessons remain uncomfortably current. First, inventory your internet-exposed OT — Censys and Shodan still find thousands of PLCs on public IPs, and your remote-support convenience is someone's attack path. Second, change vendor defaults as commissioning policy, not as an afterthought; this is a 62443-4-2 CR 1.5 requirement for a reason. Third, assume HMI loss and practice manual-mode operation — the Aliquippa operators recovered quickly because they could, and that muscle memory is a cheap, high-value control.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

CISA published a sizable batch of Siemens advisories today that directly impact plant-floor fleets. The headline item is multiple vulnerabilities in the GNU/Linux subsystem of the SIMATIC S7-1500 CPU 1518(F)-4 PN/DP MFP firmware V3.1.7 (including SIPLUS variants). Fixes are still being prepared, so Siemens is pointing operators to interim mitigations — segment these MFP CPUs behind cell firewalls, restrict access to the Linux-side services, and treat the MFP's app runtime as a hostile boundary against the real-time core. Also notable: the Industrial Edge App Publisher has a certificate-validation plus unescaped-rendering chain that enables full RCE on an engineering workstation via a man-in-the-middle, and the Siemens Software Center DLL hijack (CVE-2026-110936) has been added to CISA KEV, meaning federal timelines now apply and the exploitation risk is confirmed. Rounding out the Siemens set are Capital Embedded AR Classic (OOB read affecting AUTOSAR-based ECU software), a SENTRON 3NA COM Fuse DoS triggered by a malformed network-management message (recovery may require re-establishing the network — relevant for anyone deploying the communicating NH fuse monitoring in LV switchgear), and a Kiloview E3 NDI Encoder bundle of nine CVEs that fully compromises the device. The Kiloview advisory is a reminder that AV/broadcast gear is increasingly part of OT networks in utilities and transport control rooms, and is rarely patched. On the IT-adjacent side, SANS and SecurityWeek wound down a hectic PHP2Shell week: honeypot operators observed real shells landing from two IPs on 24 October, confirming the vulnerability is reliably exploitable in the wild. Any Linux-based HMI, historian web front end, or vendor portal running PHP should be verified patched. Separately, Node2Shell — an out-of-bounds write in Google's Brotli decoder — is now driving coordinated patching across Node.js, .NET, PowerShell and Python packages. Many SCADA vendors ship Node.js runtimes inside their HMI or dashboard products; expect vendor advisories to trickle out over the next fortnight. Finally, Dutch police arrested two suspects behind an NFC relay fraud scheme that drained over $400,000 from bank accounts via a malicious app and social engineering. Not OT, but a useful reminder for engineers using NFC-based badge access to control cabinets or MFA tokens that relay attacks against contactless credentials are now operational, not theoretical.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The KEV catalog gained Siemens Software Center (CVE-2026-110936) on 20 October — a DLL search-path hijack requiring local authenticated access, which sounds minor until you remember the Software Center is the install/update tool for most Siemens engineering software. A compromised engineer laptop plus this flaw equals arbitrary code execution during the next TIA Portal or PCS 7 update cycle. Patch the Software Center on all engineering workstations, and audit who has local admin on those machines. Also freshly added and OT-relevant: Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator SQLi (CVE-2026-114625) affecting SD-WAN deployments that increasingly front remote sites and substations; Cisco Secure Email Gateway path traversal (CVE-2026-76519); Palo Alto PAN-OS authenticated code injection (CVE-2026-0322); Ivanti Connect Secure auth bypass (CVE-2026-104935); and BeyondTrust Remote Support auth bypass (CVE-2026-102764) — the latter is widely used for third-party vendor access into OT environments and should be prioritized. For plant engineers, the practical takeaway is to focus patching cycles this week on the perimeter and remote-access stack (Ivanti, PAN-OS, BeyondTrust, VeloCloud) and on the engineering-workstation toolchain (Siemens Software Center). The appearance of older vulns like ISC BIND CVE-2015-5477 and Apache Struts CVE-2016-3081 in recent KEV updates also indicates attackers are still finding unpatched legacy infrastructure — a reminder to sweep DMZ and historian-adjacent Linux boxes.Read more →
