Today's Knowledge Bite

The humble 4–20 mA current loop, still the dominant analogue signalling standard in process plants, was deliberately designed with a live-zero at 4 mA so that a broken wire reads as 0 mA and is instantly distinguishable from a valid low reading. That one design choice, dating to the 1950s, has probably prevented more unsafe process trips than any digital diagnostic since.

⚛

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was formalised not in a university lab but on US Navy warships in the 1920s, when Nicolas Minorsky analysed how helmsmen steered the USS New Mexico and realised they intuitively applied proportional, integral, and derivative action to the rudder. His 1922 paper described the three-term controller mathematically for the first time, decades before it became the backbone of process control. The next time you tune a loop, you are essentially replicating the muscle memory of a sailor compensating for ocean swell.

Tech Spotlight

APL — Advanced Physical Layer, or Ethernet-APL — is quietly reshaping how process plants will be wired over the next decade. It extends 10BASE-T1L single-pair Ethernet into hazardous areas, delivering 10 Mbit/s and bus power to field devices over distances up to 1000 m, with intrinsically safe variants for Zone 0/Division 1. In practical terms, that means a Coriolis flowmeter or smart valve positioner can speak native OPC UA or PROFINET straight back to the control system, instead of being stranded behind a HART multiplexer or a Foundation Fieldbus H1 segment. For controls engineers, the appeal is obvious: one cable type from the DCS to the field, full-bandwidth access to device diagnostics, and no more protocol bridging. The implications include richer asset-management data, faster commissioning, and the ability to actually use the digital information that smart field devices have been producing for twenty years but nobody could extract at scale. Adoption challenges are real, though. Field switches and power supplies are still expensive compared to a terminal strip, and installer familiarity with structured Ethernet in Ex areas is limited. Cybersecurity is also a legitimate concern — pushing routable IP all the way to the sensor increases the attack surface, so segmentation, device authentication, and the OPC UA security model become non-negotiable. Expect brownfield uptake to lag greenfield for years, but new builds in chemicals, pharma, and LNG are already specifying APL as default.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2, which covers risk assessment and system design for the zones and conduits model, continues to be the standard most directly cited in asset-owner procurement specifications. If you are writing an FDS or URS this quarter, expect the EPC to ask you which Security Level Target (SL-T) each zone must meet, and to require evidence that the chosen PLC or RTU firmware is certified to the corresponding component standard IEC 62443-4-2. The gap between SL-T 2 and SL-T 3 is where most vendors live or die; the jump requires cryptographic authentication of every control-protocol session, which many legacy PLCs still cannot provide without a bump-in-the-wire module. NIST has continued iterating on the SP 800-82 Rev 3 guide to OT security, and the companion NIST IR 8183 Manufacturing Profile is increasingly referenced by US critical-infrastructure asset owners as a lightweight mapping between NIST CSF 2.0 and shop-floor realities. For engineers, the useful bit is Appendix G of 800-82r3, which gives concrete overlays for safety-instrumented systems — including the long-overdue acknowledgement that an SIS should be treated as a distinct security zone from the BPCS even when they share a vendor platform. On the ISA side, the ISA/IEC 62443-2-1 revision formally published earlier this year reshaped the requirements around the cybersecurity management system (CSMS), aligning its structure much more closely with ISO 27001 Annex A. If your organisation is pursuing both certifications, the overlap is now substantial enough to justify a unified control set rather than running two parallel programmes.Read more →

Incident of the Week

In early 2024, a water utility in Muleshoe, Texas suffered an intrusion attributed to the Russia-aligned group CyberArmyofRussia_Reborn that caused a storage tank to overflow by tens of thousands of gallons. The attackers reached the HMI directly over the internet via a VNC instance protected only by a default or weak password, manipulated setpoints, and the operator discovered the overflow visually before the tank's own level logic halted the fill cycle. No safety injury resulted, but the town briefly lost pressure and had to switch to manual operations. The mechanics are depressingly familiar: an engineering remote-access tool exposed to the public internet, no MFA, and no network-level restriction on which source addresses could reach the HMI. Shodan-style discovery is trivial for this class of exposure, and the attackers appeared opportunistic rather than targeted — the utility was simply reachable and poorly defended. Three lessons stand for controls engineers. First, inventory every piece of remote-access software your integrators have left behind on HMIs and engineering stations — TeamViewer, VNC, AnyDesk, RDP exposed through port-forwards on the plant router. Second, assume that any HMI setpoint change can and will be driven to its software limits, and ensure independent protective functions (mechanical overflow weirs, high-high level switches wired to the SIS, pump interlocks) exist in hardware or in a separated logic solver. Third, visual-only detection of abnormal process behaviour is not a control; invest in alarm rationalisation and rate-of-change alerts so the HMI shouts before the tank spills.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

Yesterday's Patch Tuesday was one for the record books: Microsoft shipped fixes for 1,413 vulnerabilities, including CVE-2026-96951, a Windows Shell command injection that is already being exploited in the wild and has been added to CISA's KEV catalogue. For plant floor environments that still rely on Windows-based engineering workstations, HMIs, and historian servers, this one deserves priority attention even if your usual patch window is months out. Pair that with Chrome and Firefox pushing another 114 browser fixes, and anyone running web-based SCADA front ends has a busy week ahead. The perimeter vendors also had a rough day. Citrix, Fortinet, and Ivanti collectively patched 29 vulnerabilities, several of them critical, covering authentication bypass, remote code execution, and credential disclosure. Ivanti Connect Secure in particular is in the KEV list again (CVE-2026-104935), and Fortinet FortiMail and Citrix NetScaler have multiple recent exploited entries. These are the devices most commonly used to terminate remote vendor access into OT networks, so any delay in patching them translates directly into exposure of your DMZ and jump hosts. SAP has issued critical patches for NetWeaver and S/4HANA that could allow full system compromise. This matters for OT because MES, batch records, and production scheduling increasingly ride on SAP integrations; a compromised ERP can inject bad orders or recipes into the production environment. Separately, SANS published a reminder about VBA macros embedded in AutoCAD .dwg files — a reminder that engineering drawing exchanges with EPCs and vendors remain a credible malware delivery path that bypasses most email filtering assumptions. On the geopolitical and insider-risk side, US authorities raided 40 Texas locations in a crackdown on North Korean IT worker laptop farms, seizing 187 machines. OpenAI reported a listening device found in a conference room, with an employee arrested. Both stories reinforce that supply chain and insider threats are no longer abstract — if you use offshore contract developers or integrators, revisit how you vet them and what they can touch in your control systems network.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added the exploited Windows Shell flaw CVE-2026-96951 and Ivanti Connect Secure CVE-2026-104935 to the KEV catalogue on 13 October. Both have direct OT relevance: Windows is pervasive on engineering workstations and HMIs, while Ivanti Connect Secure is a favourite remote access gateway for third-party integrators. The Ivanti bug is an unauthenticated authentication bypass leading to arbitrary command execution — treat any internet-facing Ivanti appliance as presumed compromised until patched and reviewed for indicators. The past two weeks also saw multiple Citrix NetScaler entries (CVE-2026-88771, 88772, 88779), Fortinet FortiMail (CVE-2026-104286), Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager (CVE-2026-76504), and MikroTik RouterOS (CVE-2026-67279) added. Collectively these cover almost every category of edge device that typically sits between corporate IT and the OT DMZ. If any of these are in your Purdue Level 3.5 or remote site WAN edge, prioritise them above the application-layer vulnerabilities. For OT teams, the practical takeaway is to track KEV additions against your network edge inventory rather than trying to patch everything. Compensating controls — disabling external management interfaces, enforcing jump host–only access, and MFA on all remote entry — buy time when a maintenance window is weeks away. Also note the resurrection of older bugs (BIND 2015, Struts 2016, ProFTPD 2015) in KEV, a reminder that long-lived OT-adjacent Linux systems and historians are still being actively exploited through decade-old flaws.Read more →
