Today's Knowledge Bite

The KNX bus, which underpins a huge share of European building automation, still allows unauthenticated broadcast writes on legacy TP1 twisted-pair segments by design — the original 1990s spec assumed physical access equalled authorisation. KNX Secure was only ratified in 2017, and adoption in installed base remains under 20 percent, which is why an account-lockout flaw from 2023 is only now hitting CISA's KEV as attackers catch up to the opportunity.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronics entirely: Elmer Sperry's 1911 gyroscopic ship autopilot and Nicolas Minorsky's 1922 mathematical analysis of USS New Mexico's steering both implemented proportional-integral-derivative control using purely mechanical linkages, hydraulic amplifiers, and flyweights. Minorsky derived the three-term structure not from theory but by watching how a skilled helmsman anticipated the ship's yaw — the derivative term was literally a mathematical model of a human's predictive eye. Every loop you tune today is a direct descendant of someone trying to write down what an experienced sailor did instinctively.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) is quietly moving from demo-booth curiosity to shipping product on the plant floor. The idea is to combine OPC UA's semantic, secure, publish-subscribe messaging with the deterministic, sub-millisecond scheduling of IEEE 802.1 TSN, so that a single standard Ethernet network can carry motion control, safety, general I/O, and IT traffic without the historical need for parallel fieldbuses like PROFINET IRT, EtherCAT, or Sercos III. For controls engineers the practical implication is significant: one cable plant, one set of switches, and one namespace from sensor to MES. Rockwell, Siemens, B&R, and Beckhoff all have TSN-capable hardware in the market, and the OPC Foundation's Field Level Communications (FLC) working group has stabilised the companion specs for controller-to-controller and controller-to-device profiles. Where it gets interesting is that safety over OPC UA (via the Safety companion spec, IEC 61784-3 compliant) can ride the same wire, potentially collapsing safety PLCs and standard PLCs onto shared infrastructure. The adoption friction is real, however. TSN requires switches that support the right subset of 802.1Q amendments (Qbv, Qbu, Qcc at minimum), and interoperability between vendors' TSN configuration models is still maturing. Most sites piloting this today are greenfield automotive, battery, or semiconductor projects where the cost of custom fieldbus infrastructure was already high. For a brownfield refinery or water utility, OPC UA over standard Ethernet remains the pragmatic step; TSN is the next decade's story.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2, the risk assessment and system design standard, continues to be the most-cited reference in procurement specifications this year, and IEC is progressing work on aligning 62443-2-1 (security programme requirements for asset owners) with the newer 62443-4-1 and 4-2 product requirements so that owner-operator and vendor obligations map cleanly. If you're writing or reviewing FATs and SATs, the practical takeaway is to require vendors to state their 4-2 Security Level Capability (SL-C) per zone and conduit, not just a blanket 'IEC 62443 compliant' claim. On the NIST side, the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 Govern function has now been in force long enough that early adopters are publishing lessons learned, and NIST SP 800-82 Revision 3 remains the most useful bridging document between IT-flavoured guidance and OT reality. It is worth pairing with CISA's Cross-Sector Cybersecurity Performance Goals, which have been updated with more OT-specific asks around default password elimination and network segmentation — both directly relevant to the Minnesota water incident this week. For those in building automation and mixed IT/OT campuses, keep an eye on ISA/IEC 62443's ongoing work with the KNX Association and BACnet/SC (Secure Connect). The KEV entry for KNX this week is a nudge that these historically 'facilities' networks are now firmly in scope for the same standards discipline applied to process control.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's spotlight is the Minnesota water systems intrusions currently under federal investigation. Reporting indicates multiple small municipal utilities saw suspicious activity on HMI and PLC-adjacent systems, with attribution pointing toward Iranian-aligned actors — echoing the 2023 CyberAv3ngers campaign that defaced Unitronics Vision PLCs at the Municipal Water Authority of Aliquippa, Pennsylvania. Early indicators suggest the same familiar pattern: internet-exposed engineering interfaces, default or weak credentials, and PLCs configured for remote programming access without any VPN or firewall between them and the wider internet. The mechanics matter because they are boringly preventable. In the Aliquippa case, a Unitronics PLC was reachable on TCP/20256 from the public internet with the default password '1111'. Attackers pushed a defacement to the HMI screen and, critically, could have manipulated set points for booster pump control. There is no evidence yet that the Minnesota incidents caused physical process impact, but the same class of exposure keeps recurring at small utilities where a single operator wears the IT, OT, and compliance hats. Three takeaways for controls engineers: first, run an external attack-surface scan against your own public IP ranges this week — Shodan and Censys will show you what an adversary sees, and finding an exposed PLC yourself is far better than the alternative. Second, change every vendor default credential on every controller, HMI, and cellular modem, and document it in your asset inventory. Third, if remote support is a business requirement, terminate it into a hardened jump host with MFA, never directly onto the controller. These three controls would have stopped both Aliquippa and, almost certainly, whatever is unfolding in Minnesota.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The week's most consequential story for plant floor engineers is the ongoing investigation into cyberattacks against several Minnesota municipal water systems, which officials and outside experts are attributing to Iranian-aligned actors. This continues a pattern seen since 2023 in which unsophisticated but effective intrusions into small water utilities exploit exposed HMIs, default credentials on Unitronics-style PLCs, and internet-facing engineering workstations. If you run a water, wastewater, or any small-utility SCADA environment, treat this as a direct prompt to re-verify that no controllers are reachable from the public internet and that vendor default passwords have been rotated. On the enterprise IT side that inevitably bleeds into OT via jump hosts and shared identity, a Cisco Secure Firewall Management Center hard-coded credential flaw (CVE-2026-20316) was added to CISA's KEV, along with fresh Fortinet FortiOS, FortiSandbox, and Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator issues. These are exactly the perimeter and segmentation devices sitting between corporate networks and Purdue Level 3 in most sites, so patching windows should be pulled forward rather than deferred to the next scheduled outage. AI-related security news continues to accelerate: OpenAI and Anthropic both disclosed that their models were used to facilitate intrusions, including one where a security firm was compromised via a malicious Python package that Claude was manipulated into publishing. For controls engineers this is a reminder that any AI coding assistant used to write PLC helper scripts, historian queries, or Python glue for OPC UA clients needs the same package-provenance discipline as any other software supply chain input. Finally, the EU announced a Brussels-based enforcement team for the AI Act covering deepfakes and AI-assisted hacking, and Google's AI vulnerability discovery agent uncovered a 13-year-old Chrome flaw. The trend line is clear: defenders and attackers are both automating vulnerability discovery, and the half-life of latent bugs in long-lived OT software stacks is getting shorter.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The KEV additions with the most direct OT relevance this cycle are CVE-2023-4346 in the KNX protocol's Connection Authorization Option 1, and the Cisco FMC hard-coded password issue (CVE-2026-20316). KNX is pervasive in building automation, campus HVAC, and increasingly appears bridged into industrial networks at pharma, semiconductor, and data centre sites. The flaw lets an attacker abuse a weak lockout mechanism to purge devices and lock BCU keys, effectively a denial-of-engineering attack on a live building. Enable the additional KNX Secure options, restrict KNXnet/IP routing to trusted VLANs, and audit any IP-side exposure of KNX gateways. Beyond that, the Fortinet FortiOS symlink persistence bypass (CVE-2025-68686), FortiSandbox command injection pair, Check Point SmartConsole authentication bypass, and Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator command injection all target the network security and SD-WAN layer that most sites rely on to segment IT from OT. If any of these appear in your DMZ or at remote-site edges, treat them as emergency changes. The two Microsoft SharePoint deserialization bugs are worth flagging to IT counterparts because SharePoint is where drawings, P&IDs, and MoC documents typically live, and a breach there is a rich reconnaissance goldmine before any OT intrusion attempt.Read more →
