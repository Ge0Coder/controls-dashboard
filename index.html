Today's Knowledge Bite

The Modbus protocol, still ubiquitous in 2026, was published by Modicon in 1979 as a way for their new 084 PLC to talk to a serial terminal — meaning the protocol running your process today is older than TCP/IP's public deployment. Its 16-bit register limit and lack of authentication weren't design flaws at the time; they were reasonable choices for a point-to-point RS-232 world where the physical wire was the security boundary.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronics: Elmer Sperry's ship autopilots in the 1910s and Nicolas Minorsky's 1922 analysis of USS New Mexico's steering used pneumatic and mechanical realizations of proportional, integral, and derivative action decades before op-amps existed. Minorsky derived the three-term control law by watching how helmsmen actually steered — anticipating (derivative), correcting steady offset (integral), and reacting to error (proportional). Every loop you tune today is, in a real sense, a mathematical formalization of what an experienced sailor did by feel a century ago.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues its slow march from trade-show demo to production floor. The pitch is compelling: a single deterministic Ethernet fabric carrying both real-time motion control traffic and higher-level information model data, replacing the current mess of Profinet IRT, EtherCAT, Sercos, and separate OT LANs. TSN provides bounded latency via time-aware scheduling (IEEE 802.1Qbv), while OPC UA PubSub over UDP carries the payloads with a rich, self-describing type system. Practically, adoption in 2026 is still gated by three things. First, switch and NIC support for the full TSN feature set is finally broad but interoperability between vendors' scheduling implementations remains fiddly — pilot deployments still tend to be single-vendor. Second, engineering tools for configuring streams, gate control lists, and PTP time domains lag the underlying silicon; you need someone comfortable with both networking and controls, which is a rare skillset. Third, retrofit economics are hard: replacing a working Profinet line to gain determinism you already have is a tough business case. The sweet spot right now is greenfield lines with mixed vendor motion and vision systems, or facilities standardizing on a converged network for future flexibility rather than immediate need.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443 continues to be the reference OT security standard, and the 62443-2-1 revision published earlier this cycle is now showing up as a contractual requirement in more RFPs — particularly for utilities and pharma. The revised part shifts the emphasis from a static IACS security program document to an ongoing security management system with defined maturity levels, mirroring how ISO 27001 evolved. If you haven't looked at it recently, the practical implication is that auditors will increasingly expect evidence of continuous activity (patch cadence, incident drills, access reviews) rather than a binder on a shelf. On the NIST side, the SP 800-82 Rev 3 guidance remains the pragmatic companion for engineers who need to map generic cybersecurity controls onto ICS realities like safety interlocks, deterministic timing, and legacy device constraints. It's worth pairing with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 profile work several ISACs have published for specific verticals. Worth watching: the ISA/IEC 62443-4-2 device certification ecosystem has broadened significantly, and more PLC and RTU vendors are shipping SL-2 or SL-3 certified firmware variants. When specifying new controllers, asking for the certification level and the associated security capabilities matrix is now a reasonable procurement question rather than an aspirational one.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the 2023 Aliquippa, Pennsylvania water authority incident, where attackers affiliated with the CyberAv3ngers group compromised a Unitronics Vision-series PLC controlling a booster station. The intrusion wasn't sophisticated: the PLC was internet-exposed on its default TCP/20256 port with the factory-default password '1111'. The attackers defaced the HMI with anti-Israel messaging and forced the station into manual operation, but they had the access to do far worse. What made this incident instructive is how ordinary it was. No zero-day, no supply-chain compromise, no custom malware — just a controller shipped with a weak default and connected directly to the internet by a small utility that didn't have dedicated OT security staff. The vendor's guidance to change defaults existed; nobody had read it. Three takeaways for controls engineers: first, inventory every PLC, RTU, and gateway with an IP address and confirm none are directly internet-reachable — Shodan and Censys make this a five-minute exercise. Second, treat default credentials as a commissioning defect, not a hardening task, and don't sign off on a system until they're changed and documented. Third, small sites without SOC coverage need simple, high-signal alerting — even a basic 'PLC mode changed to program' notification would have caught this. The lowest-tech attacks continue to work because the lowest-tech defenses aren't in place.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The most operationally significant item today is SonicWall's disclosure of two actively exploited zero-days in SMA1000 secure-access appliances (CVE-2026-83549 and CVE-2026-83548) that can be chained for unauthenticated remote code execution. Many manufacturing and utility sites use SMA appliances as the remote-vendor gateway into their DMZs, so a compromise here bypasses one of the primary controls separating IT from OT. If your remote-support path terminates on an SMA1000, treat this as an immediate patch and hunt priority and consider disabling external access until the fix is applied. Equally alarming from a supply-chain perspective is the BGP hijack that pushed a malicious Virtualizor update using a technically valid TLS certificate for Softaculous domains. This is a textbook reminder that TLS alone does not validate a software update — network-path takeovers can defeat certificate trust when the attacker briefly owns the destination prefix. Controls engineers who rely on vendor auto-update channels for HMIs, historians, or virtualized SCADA hosts should verify that updates are signed at the package level, not merely delivered over HTTPS. On the AI side, OpenAI's Astra reportedly crossed the threshold for autonomous discovery and exploitation of zero-days across defended systems, and Anthropic rolled out Enterprise Frontier Safeguards after handling recent misuse incidents. For OT this pushes the timeline forward on AI-assisted attackers finding logic bugs in exposed engineering interfaces. Finally, the 23-year-old Sality P2P botnet was disrupted — a modest housekeeping win, but a reminder that legacy Windows engineering workstations remain fertile ground for old malware families.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA's KEV additions over the past two weeks lean heavily toward remote-access and collaboration infrastructure that commonly sits at the IT/OT boundary. The two PaperCut NG/MF flaws (CVE-2026-82078 and CVE-2026-81578) chain unauthenticated config modification with unsafe reflection for RCE — relevant because print servers are frequently domain-joined and reachable from plant networks. The Citrix NetScaler ADC/Gateway issue (CVE-2026-8452), Oracle HTTP Server/WebLogic proxy plug-in flaw (CVE-2026-21962), and VMware vCenter path traversal (CVE-2026-59310) all directly threaten the virtualization and access layers that host SCADA, MES, and historian workloads. Several Linux kernel entries (CVE-2026-53362 IPv6 privilege escalation, plus older CVE-2022-0995) matter for the growing population of Linux-based edge gateways, IIoT brokers, and containerized control nodes. TrueConf Server's two flaws on TCP/4307 (CVE-2026-72529/72530) allow unauthenticated RCE and are worth checking if your site uses it for operations-room collaboration. Practical remediation: prioritize the perimeter and hypervisor CVEs first, verify SMA/NetScaler/vCenter patch levels, and ensure any exposed management ports are firewalled to jump hosts only. Where patches are not yet deployable in production, compensating controls like ACLs on 4307/TCP, restricting PaperCut admin access to a management VLAN, and monitoring vCenter for anomalous file operations will meaningfully reduce exposure.Read more →
