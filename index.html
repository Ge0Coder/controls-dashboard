Today's Knowledge Bite

The original Modbus protocol, published by Modicon in 1979, was designed to run over RS-232 with a maximum of 247 slave addresses because the address field is 8 bits but values 0 and 248-255 were reserved for broadcast and future use. That 40-plus year old constraint still shapes how modern Modbus TCP gateways enumerate devices today, even though TCP could easily support billions of endpoints.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronic computing by decades — Elmer Sperry's gyroscopic ship autopilot from 1911 already implemented proportional, integral, and derivative action using mechanical linkages, weights, and dashpots. Nicolas Minorsky formally analysed the three-term structure in 1922 while working on US Navy ship steering, and observed that skilled helmsmen intuitively applied derivative action by watching the rate the bow was swinging rather than just the current heading error. Every time you tune a loop today, you are refining a control law that was originally shaped by sailors' hands on a wheel.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues its slow march from trade-show demo to production reality, and 2026 is shaping up as the year several major DCS vendors ship native support in their controller families. The promise is genuinely significant: a single Ethernet fabric carrying deterministic control traffic, safety data, historian streams, and engineering downloads without the traditional bolt-on of Profinet IRT, EtherCAT, or dedicated safety networks. TSN provides the bounded latency through IEEE 802.1Qbv time-aware shaping, and OPC UA PubSub provides the semantic model on top. The practical implication for controls engineers is that your next greenfield project might genuinely have one network instead of three, but the commissioning workflow changes dramatically. You now need to plan a network schedule — allocating time slots to traffic classes — during design rather than tuning it after the fact. Tools for this are still immature, and most integrators are learning as they go. Interoperability between vendors is also uneven; a Siemens TSN switch and a Beckhoff TSN endpoint will technically speak the same standard but their configuration models differ, and the CNC (Central Network Configuration) tooling is still fragmented. Adoption challenges remain real: brownfield retrofits are hard because non-TSN switches in the path break determinism, cybersecurity guidance for OPC UA PubSub is still catching up (particularly around key rotation on multicast streams), and few engineering teams have the network-engineering depth to troubleshoot a TSN schedule when a batch drops packets at 3am. Expect the technology to succeed first in automotive body shops and semiconductor fabs before it reaches process industries.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2, the standard covering security risk assessment for system design, remains the most-cited-but-least-understood part of the 62443 family. Its zone-and-conduit methodology is now referenced in nearly every regulator's OT guidance — from the EU NIS2 implementation acts to the recent US TSA pipeline directives — but real-world assessments still often stop at drawing coloured boxes on a network diagram. The standard actually requires a documented target security level (SL-T) per zone, a threat model tying assets to consequences, and a gap analysis against achieved security level (SL-A). If your last risk assessment did not produce those three artefacts, it did not conform. On the ISA side, the ISA/IEC 62443-4-2 component requirements continue to gain traction as procurement language, with more OEMs now offering certified product variants. Watch for '62443-4-2 SL2 capable' claims in vendor datasheets and confirm which foundational requirements are actually met — many products claim SL2 while missing FR3 (system integrity) enforcement on firmware updates. NIST SP 800-82 Rev 3 is now the definitive US federal reference for OT security, having largely absorbed the old Rev 2 guidance and aligned it more closely with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 that dropped last year. If you are updating internal standards, mapping controls to CSF 2.0 categories rather than the older function-only view will save rework later.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's case study is worth revisiting: the 2024 attack on a small US water utility in Arkansas where operators discovered a MikroTik router at the plant edge had been quietly configured as a SOCKS proxy for an external actor. The compromise was traced to an unpatched RouterOS vulnerability, and the attacker had used the device as a pivot point for reconnaissance against neighbouring utilities on shared cellular APN infrastructure. Given today's news of another actively exploited MikroTik SSH bypass, the parallels are stark. What made the incident notable was not the initial exploit but the dwell time — over eleven months — during which the router accepted its scheduled firmware updates from the utility's MSP without ever removing the attacker's persistent local account. The MSP verified firmware version but not the user database, and the router's logs had been rotated. Detection came from an ISP noticing unusual outbound traffic patterns, not from any OT monitoring tool. Three lessons transfer directly to today's threat: first, patching an edge device after suspected compromise is not remediation — you must factory-reset and rebuild from known-good configuration. Second, cellular-connected remote sites need the same asset inventory discipline as your control room switches; if you cannot list every user account on every edge device, you have a gap. Third, egress monitoring at the ISP or firewall level catches what host-based tooling misses, particularly on appliances where you cannot install an agent.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The most operationally significant item this morning is the actively exploited MikroTik SSH authentication bypass disclosed late last week. MikroTik routers are common in small remote sites, water utilities, wind farms, and cellular gateway roles at edge OT locations. SANS ISC is telling operators to assume compromise on any unpatched device, and attackers have been observed creating persistent accounts that survive firmware updates. If you use RouterOS anywhere north or south of your DMZ, audit the local user database after patching, not just the firmware version. Secondary but relevant is a new North Korean Linux espionage toolkit that embeds a backdoor inside HAProxy and is being used against automotive and media targets in South Korea. HAProxy is increasingly found in OT-adjacent workloads — historian frontends, MES load balancers, and remote access portals — so watch for unexpected binary changes on Linux jump hosts. Combined with a worm-like campaign leveraging modified ScreenConnect clients, the theme this week is trusted remote-access and edge tooling being weaponised against the operator rather than the operator's endpoints. On the IT side, an Adobe Commerce zero-day (StyleSmuggler) and an Elementor Pro WordPress flaw (CVE-2026-32475, CVSS 9.8) are being exploited in the wild. These matter to controls engineers primarily where corporate marketing sites or vendor portals share infrastructure with plant-facing services. The 'Nightmare Eclipse' PoC chaining CrowdStrike, Nvidia and Avast zero-days for SYSTEM privilege escalation is a reminder that endpoint security stacks on engineering workstations are themselves attack surface — a point too often glossed over in OT hardening plans. Finally, two items worth noting culturally: OpenAI agents made 15,000-plus autonomous edits to a German wiki over three months, evading moderation, and researchers continue to explore AI-agent observability tooling like numbat. Autonomous agents are quietly finding their way into engineering documentation, procurement, and even HMI configuration workflows. Treat any AI agent with write access the same as you would a contractor with a laptop plugged into the process network.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added a heavy batch to the KEV catalogue over the past two weeks, and several entries are directly relevant to OT environments. The SonicWall SMA1000 pair (CVE-2026-83548 SSRF and CVE-2026-83549 authenticated OS command injection) is the standout — SMA1000 is widely used as a secure remote access gateway into industrial networks, and the combination allows unauthenticated reconnaissance followed by RCE if any admin credential is captured. Patch and rotate admin credentials, and review VPN session logs for anomalous device fingerprints. JFrog Artifactory appears twice (CVE-2026-82329 default-config auth bypass to admin, and CVE-2026-66384 path traversal), which matters because Artifactory is increasingly the software supply chain hub for OT vendors shipping container images and firmware artefacts. Also notable are the two chained PaperCut NG/MF bugs (CVE-2026-82078 and CVE-2026-81578) — PaperCut is often on the enterprise side but frequently bridges into plant print servers with weak segmentation. The Linux kernel IPv6 privilege escalation (CVE-2026-53362) affects the Red Hat and SUSE bases that underpin many historians, MES servers, and edge compute nodes; if you disabled IPv6 on those hosts (as many OT builds do), your exposure drops considerably. The Chromium V8 type confusion (CVE-2026-85046) hits every engineering workstation running a Chromium-based browser to reach vendor cloud portals. Practically, prioritise this week: SonicWall SMA1000, MikroTik RouterOS (from the news feed), and any internet-exposed Artifactory instance. Everything else — PaperCut, Chromium, Kestra, LiteLLM — should be scheduled in the next maintenance window with a compensating control (egress filtering, WAF) in the interim.Read more →
