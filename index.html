Today's Knowledge Bite

The KNX BCU key that's now under active attack is, by default on many older installations, all zeros — 00 00 00 00. The protocol was designed in the 1990s when 'the network' meant a twisted pair in the wall, so authentication was largely an afterthought. Even today, roughly 60% of KNX/IP installations surveyed by researchers ship with either the default key or no key at all.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronics entirely — Nicolas Minorsky derived the three-term control law in 1922 by watching helmsmen steer the USS New Mexico. He noticed experienced helmsmen didn't just correct for current heading error (proportional); they also anticipated based on how fast the ship was swinging (derivative) and accounted for persistent drift like wind or current (integral). Every PID loop you tune today is a mathematical description of what a good sailor already knew instinctively a century ago.

Tech Spotlight

This week's spotlight is on Ethernet-APL (Advanced Physical Layer), which is finally hitting meaningful field deployment in 2026. Ethernet-APL brings 10 Mbps two-wire Ethernet, intrinsic safety, and up to 1000m reach to process instrumentation — meaning a Coriolis flowmeter or pH probe in Zone 1 can talk native TCP/IP (and PROFINET or EtherNet/IP or OPC UA over it) on the same two wires that used to carry a 4-20mA loop with HART. The practical implication is enormous: field devices become first-class network citizens with full diagnostic and configuration data available without HART multiplexers, WirelessHART gateways, or fieldbus segment coupler complexity. Asset management tools see the instrument directly. A modern APL switch can power the field device (like PoE, but hazardous-area rated) and provide the network media conversion in one box. Adoption challenges are real, though. APL requires new field switches, new instruments (existing HART devices don't magically become APL), and — critically — a much more rigorous approach to network security at the field level. When your temperature transmitter has an IP address, it's addressable by anything else on the network. Vendors are still catching up on secure-by-default configurations, and few plants have the OT-network-monitoring maturity to handle 10,000 endpoints where previously there were 500 loops.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (Security Risk Assessment for System Design) continues to be the standard most engineers under-utilise. It provides a structured way to partition a system into zones and conduits, assign target security levels (SL-T), and document the rationale — which is exactly what auditors under NIS2 and the EU Cyber Resilience Act are now asking to see. If you've never formally documented zones and conduits for your plant, this is the year to start; regulators are moving from 'do you have a policy' to 'show me the assessment.' On the KNX incident above, note that ISO/IEC 14543-3 (the KNX standard itself) was amended years ago to include KNX Data Secure and KNX IP Secure, but the standard permits legacy mode for backward compatibility. This is a recurring pattern in OT standards — the secure option exists but is optional, and market pressure to interoperate with 20-year-old devices keeps insecure defaults alive. IEC 62443-4-2 component requirements would forbid this pattern in new products; the friction is retrofitting. NIST is also progressing SP 800-82 Rev 4 through public comment, refining guidance on cloud-connected OT, remote access, and — new in Rev 4 — explicit treatment of AI/ML in industrial contexts. Worth reading the draft before it lands, particularly the sections on managing vendor remote-access tooling, which now touches almost every plant.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's case study is the 2023-2024 Municipal Water Authority of Aliquippa incident, which is receiving renewed attention as similar TTPs have surfaced in recent European water utility probes. Attackers affiliated with CyberAv3ngers accessed a Unitronics Vision series PLC exposed to the internet with a default password (1111) and TCP port 20256 open. They defaced the HMI screen and stopped a booster pump serving part of the distribution system. Operators reverted to manual control and no water quality impact occurred. What made this a wake-up call was the sheer banality of it: no zero-day, no APT tradecraft, no ICS-specific malware. Just Shodan, a default password, and a PLC that shouldn't have been on the public internet. Subsequent scanning found dozens of similar Unitronics devices exposed across US utilities, and the pattern repeated with other small-vendor PLCs commonly used in water, wastewater and small manufacturing. The lessons remain sharp for 2026: first, inventory your internet-exposed OT assets using an external view (Shodan, Censys, or a third-party attack surface tool) — you will find things you didn't know were there. Second, change default credentials as part of commissioning acceptance, not as a 'we'll get to it' item. Third, small vendors and 'appliance-style' PLCs often ship with weak defaults because they target integrators who prioritise ease of setup; procurement specifications need to require secure-by-default posture.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The most consequential item for controls engineers this week isn't in the ICS-labelled feeds — it's CISA's addition of CVE-2023-4346 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue. This is a KNX protocol authorisation weakness affecting building automation networks worldwide. Active exploitation means attackers can brute-force the BCU key on unprotected installations and effectively brick every device on the bus. Facility engineers running Siemens, ABB, Schneider or JUNG KNX gear in commercial buildings, data centres and manufacturing sites should treat this as urgent. The fact that a 2023 CVE has just entered KEV three years later underscores how slowly building-controls patch cycles move. On the IT side, WordPress WP2Shell (CVE-2026-60137/63030) is being exploited in the wild. That matters because plant-floor teams frequently stand up WordPress-based dashboards, maintenance portals or vendor documentation sites on the corporate side of the DMZ — these often carry OT credentials or provide pivot points into engineering workstations. Chrome 150 also patches six use-after-free bugs; HMI operator stations that still browse the web should be updated promptly. SANS ISC flags renewed internet-wide scanning for the Hikvision Intelligent Security API. Hikvision cameras are ubiquitous in industrial CCTV deployments and are frequently dual-homed between IT and OT segments. If you have Hikvision NVRs or cameras reachable from process networks — even for a legitimate reason like video-based safety confirmation — verify firmware level and ensure the API is not exposed. Finally, in the 'signs of the times' category, the Hugging Face compromise reportedly involved an autonomous AI agent as the attacker. Combined with OpenClaw agents being weaponised via WhatsApp, this points to a maturing class of AI-driven intrusion tooling. Expect this technique class to reach OT-adjacent targets — vendor cloud portals, remote-access gateways, and asset-inventory SaaS — within the next 12 months.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The most OT-relevant KEV addition in the last 30 days is CVE-2023-4346, the KNX Protocol Connection Authorization Option 1 flaw. An attacker with network access to a KNX/IP installation can exhaust the account lockout counters, then brute-force or reset the BCU key, purging device configuration and rendering entire installations unresponsive. Remediation is not a simple patch — it requires enabling KNX Data Secure or KNX IP Secure, segmenting KNX/IP off any routable network, and disabling KNXnet/IP tunnelling from untrusted zones. This is a project, not a Patch Tuesday task. CVE-2026-12569 in PTC Windchill and FlexPLM is the other one to prioritise. Windchill sits at the heart of many manufacturers' product lifecycle and BOM management, and unauthenticated RCE against it can leak proprietary CAD, recipes and control logic — or provide a staging point for supply-chain implants pushed to engineering workstations. Patch immediately if internet-facing; assume compromise if you cannot verify exposure. The SharePoint deserialization pair (CVE-2026-58644, CVE-2026-45659) and the two Fortinet FortiSandbox command injections deserve attention because both technologies commonly sit on the enterprise/OT boundary. FortiSandbox in particular is often trusted to detonate files bound for engineering workstations — a compromise there is a direct route into the Purdue Level 3 environment.Read more →
