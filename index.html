Today's Knowledge Bite

The original 4-20 mA current loop standard, still ubiquitous on plant floors today, was chosen partly because 4 mA is comfortably above the threshold needed to detect a broken wire (0 mA) while remaining low enough that even a short-circuit fault cannot ignite a flammable atmosphere under intrinsic-safety rules. That single design choice — a live-zero signal — has quietly protected refineries and chemical plants for over 60 years and is one reason analog signalling refuses to die despite decades of digital fieldbus evolution.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was formalised not in a university lab but aboard US Navy ships in the 1920s, when Nicolas Minorsky was analysing the automatic steering of the USS New Mexico. He observed that a skilled helmsman didn't just react to the current heading error (proportional) but also to how long the ship had been off course (integral) and how fast it was swinging back (derivative). His 1922 paper essentially reverse-engineered human intuition into three terms — and nearly every temperature loop, flow loop, and motion axis in industry today is still tuned against that same century-old insight about how a good helmsman thinks.

Tech Spotlight

This week's spotlight is on OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking). Traditional OPC UA is excellent for interoperable, secure data exchange but it runs over best-effort Ethernet, which is unsuitable for hard real-time control loops. TSN is a set of IEEE 802.1 standards that add deterministic scheduling, bounded latency, and time synchronisation to standard Ethernet, allowing motion control traffic to coexist on the same wire as SCADA polling and video streams without jitter. The practical implication is that the historical split between fieldbus (Profinet IRT, EtherCAT, Sercos) and IT/SCADA networks can start to collapse onto a single converged infrastructure. That means fewer gateways, simpler cable plants, and — importantly — a common security model based on OPC UA's built-in authentication and encryption rather than a patchwork of proprietary protocols. Adoption challenges remain significant. TSN requires switches with hardware support for the relevant sub-standards (802.1Qbv scheduling, 802.1AS-Rev time sync), and different vendors have shipped different subsets. Engineering tooling for configuring TSN schedules is still immature, and troubleshooting a misbehaving stream on a converged network is harder than on a dedicated fieldbus. Expect greenfield installations from major vendors (Siemens, Rockwell, B&R, Beckhoff) to lead, with brownfield migration taking most of the decade.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443 continues to be the reference framework worth tracking. The 62443-2-1 revision, focused on security program requirements for asset owners, has been undergoing significant restructuring to align terminology with 62443-3-3 (system security requirements) and 62443-4-2 (component requirements). If you are building or refreshing your OT cybersecurity management system, use the harmonised risk-assessment methodology in 62443-3-2 as your zone-and-conduit foundation rather than reinventing it in NIST CSF terms alone. On the US side, NIST SP 800-82 Rev 3 remains the authoritative guide for securing OT and has increasingly been cited by insurers and auditors as a baseline. It now maps cleanly to the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, which added the Govern function — a useful hook for engineering managers who need to justify OT security spend to a board that thinks in enterprise risk terms. Worth watching: ISA is continuing work on ISA/IEC 62443-4-1 certification uptake among component vendors. When specifying new PLCs, drives, or historians, asking for 62443-4-1 secure-development-lifecycle certification (not just 4-2 product certification) is one of the more effective ways to shift the market toward vendors who actually patch what they ship.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's retrospective focuses on the 2021 Oldsmar, Florida water treatment near-miss, which remains one of the most instructive OT incidents publicly documented. An operator noticed his mouse cursor moving on its own on an HMI, and shortly afterwards saw the sodium hydroxide setpoint changed from about 100 ppm to 11,100 ppm — a level that could have caused serious harm had the operator not immediately reverted it and had downstream pH monitoring not been in place as a second layer of defence. The intrusion vector was mundane: a shared TeamViewer remote-access account used across multiple workstations, no MFA, weak passwords, and Windows 7 endpoints reachable from the internet. There was no zero-day, no nation-state tooling — just an exposed remote-access tool and reused credentials. Investigators never publicly attributed the attack, and later reporting suggested it may have been an insider or accidental event, but the technical lessons stand regardless. Three lessons remain evergreen. First, never allow inbound remote access to an HMI without a jump host, MFA, and session recording — vendor convenience is not worth a poisoning event. Second, engineer safety-critical setpoints with hard limits enforced at the controller (not just the HMI) so that even a compromised operator screen cannot command a lethal dose. Third, physical and analytical instrumentation downstream of the control loop (in Oldsmar's case, pH monitoring) is often what actually saves you — invest in independent safety layers rather than assuming the control system will be trustworthy.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The week's most consequential story for OT teams is the demonstration of agentic-AI-driven ransomware via Langflow. Attackers chained LLM agents with real-time reasoning to autonomously execute a multi-stage intrusion, combining reconnaissance, exploitation, and lateral movement with minimal human input. For controls engineers this raises the ceiling on what mid-tier adversaries can attempt, and lowers the barrier for opportunistic attacks on internet-exposed HMIs, historians, and remote-access gateways. Expect a rise in automated scanning that not only finds exposed OT assets but also drafts and executes tailored exploit chains. On the identity and access front, an alleged Scattered Spider member was extradited to the US. That group has repeatedly demonstrated that social-engineering the IT help desk is the fastest route into an OT-adjacent environment, especially for MFA reset abuse against engineering workstations and jump hosts. Simultaneously, Google and the FBI disrupted the NetNut residential proxy network, which had been used by nation-state actors to obscure origin during OT reconnaissance. Both stories reinforce that the perimeter increasingly fails on identity, not firewalls. Medtronic disclosed a breach impacting 3.8 million people via a ShinyHunters intrusion into corporate IT. While this is a healthcare data breach rather than a device-safety event, it is a reminder that medical-device manufacturers, like their industrial-automation peers, sit on top of enterprise IT systems whose compromise can eventually cascade into product telemetry, remote-service platforms, and firmware update channels. Finally, critical remote-code-execution flaws in the Cursor AI code editor (the DuneSlide vulnerabilities) allow zero-click prompt injection to escape the sandbox and hit the host OS. Many engineering teams have quietly begun using AI coding assistants to draft PLC logic, HMI scripts, or Python for historian integration. Treat these tools as untrusted execution environments and keep them off engineering workstations that touch production networks.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The CISA KEV additions this month include several entries with direct OT relevance. CVE-2025-67038 in Lantronix EDS5000 device servers allows unauthenticated OS command injection as root via the username parameter — these devices are widely deployed as serial-to-Ethernet gateways in front of legacy PLCs and RTUs, and any internet-facing instance should be considered compromised until proven otherwise. CVE-2026-12569 in PTC Windchill and FlexPLM enables unauthenticated RCE and is particularly concerning because PLM systems hold the master engineering data (drawings, BOMs, controls specs) that would let an attacker plan a much more targeted follow-on attack. Three Ubiquiti UniFi OS bugs (CVE-2026-34908/34909/34910) covering command injection, path traversal, and access control are notable because UniFi gear has crept into industrial sites as cheap plant-network infrastructure — often outside the sanctioned IEC 62443 zone-and-conduit design. Combined with Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager path traversal (CVE-2026-20262, CVE-2026-20245), Arista EOS tunnel decapsulation flaws (CVE-2026-7473), and a Check Point Security Gateway IKEv1 auth bypass (CVE-2026-50751, ransomware-linked), the networking layer is under sustained pressure. Practical action for the long weekend: inventory any Lantronix, Ubiquiti, or Check Point devices touching the OT DMZ; verify SharePoint (CVE-2026-45659) and SimpleHelp (CVE-2026-48558) patch status since both are commonly used for engineering document sharing and vendor remote support; and confirm Splunk Enterprise (CVE-2026-20253) is patched if it is your historian-adjacent SIEM.Read more →
