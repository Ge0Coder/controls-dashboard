Today's Knowledge Bite

The original PID controller tuning rules published by Ziegler and Nichols in 1942 were derived experimentally on pneumatic controllers using a wax-cylinder chart recorder for the step response. The rules have survived 84 years and countless technology generations because they encode a fundamental relationship between process dead time and controller aggressiveness — not because pneumatic hardware had any special properties. Most modern autotuners still produce results within about 20% of what Ziegler-Nichols would give you for a first-order-plus-dead-time process.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The Watt centrifugal governor of 1788 — the flyball device most people picture when they hear "feedback control" — was not actually invented by James Watt; he adapted it from millstone-gap regulators already used in flour mills for at least a century. What Watt added was the linkage to a steam throttle, turning a passive mechanical regulator into an active control loop. James Clerk Maxwell's 1868 paper "On Governors," analysing why some of these devices oscillated, is generally regarded as the founding document of modern control theory — meaning the mathematical field we all use came out of an engineer complaining that his hardware was hunting.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues its slow march from marketing slides into real deployments. The premise is simple: use standard Ethernet, but with IEEE 802.1 TSN extensions (802.1AS for time sync, 802.1Qbv for scheduled traffic, 802.1CB for frame replication), so that deterministic control traffic and best-effort IT traffic can share the same physical network without the control loop ever missing a deadline. Combined with OPC UA PubSub, this promises a single converged network from sensor to cloud — the long-held dream of eliminating the fieldbus tower of Babel. In practice, adoption is still constrained by three things. First, PLC and drive vendors ship TSN support inconsistently; Siemens, B&R, Beckhoff, and Rockwell all have offerings but interoperability profiles (the OPC UA FLC group's work) are still stabilising. Second, TSN-capable managed switches remain 2-3x the cost of standard industrial Ethernet switches, and configuring the CNC (Centralized Network Configuration) model is genuinely harder than configuring PROFINET or EtherNet/IP. Third, most greenfield projects don't need microsecond-level determinism — a well-designed PROFINET IRT or EtherCAT network already handles motion control fine. The realistic near-term use case is not ripping out fieldbuses, but converging plant-floor cell networks with line-level MES/historian traffic on the same cable, which reduces cabinet wiring and switch count. If you're speccing a new line in 2026-2027, at minimum specify TSN-ready switches even if you don't enable the features yet — the incremental cost is small and future-proofs the panel.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) continues to be the standard most frequently cited in RFQs from asset owners this year, and it's worth re-reading if you're specifying a new system. The zone-and-conduit model it defines is the reference language that OEMs, integrators, and asset owners now use to argue about who owns which security control. If you aren't producing a zone-and-conduit diagram alongside your P&ID and network architecture drawings, you're behind the curve — and increasingly, insurers are asking for them during renewals. ISA/IEC 62443-4-2 (component-level security requirements) is where the real procurement leverage sits. It defines Security Levels 1-4 for individual components, and Rockwell, Siemens, Schneider, ABB, and Honeywell now certify specific product families to SL2 through the ISASecure program. When writing PLC or RTU specifications, requiring a specific SL rating is far more useful than vague language like "shall support cybersecurity best practices." The certification includes required capabilities like user authentication, session integrity, and audit logging — things you'd otherwise have to specify item-by-item. NIST is quietly finalising SP 800-82 Rev. 3 updates that better align with the CSF 2.0 Govern function, and CISA's Cross-Sector Cybersecurity Performance Goals (CPGs) now explicitly reference 62443 controls, giving US operators a clearer regulatory-to-standards mapping than existed even a year ago.Read more →

Incident of the Week

The most instructive recent incident for controls engineers remains the 2024 attack on a US water utility where a poorly segmented HMI running an outdated VNC server was exposed to the internet through a misconfigured firewall rule left in place after a remote support session. Attackers — later attributed to a hacktivist group — used default credentials to log in, changed setpoints on a chemical dosing pump, and posted screenshots on social media. Physical safety interlocks and operator vigilance prevented any real health impact, but the reputational damage was substantial and it triggered EPA enforcement action. What happened technically was almost boring: an integrator had opened port 5900 to allow after-hours troubleshooting; the firewall change request was never closed out; the VNC instance still had the vendor default password; and no one was reviewing edge firewall logs. The attackers found it via Shodan in under a day. Three lessons endure. First, every temporary firewall rule needs an automatic expiry, not a ticket that says "remove when done." Second, remote access should terminate at a jump host with MFA — never directly at an HMI or PLC. Third, if you don't have someone (or a service) actively looking at your internet-facing OT assets from the outside at least monthly, an attacker's Shodan search is your only monitoring. This incident cost the utility nothing in production but everything in trust, and every controls engineer should assume their own site has at least one forgotten rule like it.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The week opens with a mixed bag for OT-adjacent teams. SecurityWeek highlights newly disclosed vulnerabilities in industrial refrigeration systems — a reminder that cold-chain, food and pharma facilities running networked HVAC/R controllers remain soft targets often overlooked in vulnerability management programs. The same roundup notes DEF CON research into hacking a Boeing 737, underscoring that safety-critical embedded systems continue to attract scrutiny from the research community. Controls engineers running similar embedded RTOS platforms in process plants should assume any weaknesses demonstrated in aviation research will eventually be probed in industrial contexts. On the enterprise side, a large-scale Azure data theft campaign is affecting Fortune 500 companies including McDonald's, TCS, and Vodafone. While primarily an IT/cloud identity incident, the campaign matters to OT because many manufacturers now push historian, MES, and asset-management data into Azure via IoT Hub or Data Explorer. Any compromised service principal or federated identity that traverses the IT/OT boundary is worth reviewing this week. The RingCentral breach (1.6M records) and Beacon CRM incident (root cause: an AWS access key leaked in JavaScript build artifacts) reinforce that secrets hygiene in CI/CD pipelines remains the dominant failure mode for cloud-connected industrial software. Google Cloud has published a post-quantum cryptography roadmap targeting full readiness by 2029, with milestones in 2027 and 2028. For controls engineers, PQC is not an immediate concern for L1/L2 devices, but it will affect the certificate infrastructure underpinning OPC UA, MQTT-over-TLS, and remote access gateways in the coming procurement cycles. Start asking vendors about crypto agility now, not in 2028. On the tooling side, Wireshark 4.6.8 dropped over the weekend, fixing 28 vulnerabilities and 25 bugs — engineers running Wireshark on engineering workstations for protocol troubleshooting should update. Finally, the reported story that the LiteLLM package compromise was actually traced to Trivy (a container scanner) rather than LiteLLM itself is a useful reminder that supply-chain incident attribution is often wrong on the first pass; wait for confirmed IOCs before ripping out software.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA's KEV additions over the last two weeks are heavy on perimeter and management-plane devices that sit directly between corporate and OT networks. CVE-2026-20349 in Cisco ASA/FTD (unauthenticated remote DoS via heap inspection flaw) and CVE-2026-20316 in Cisco Firewall Management Center (hard-coded password allowing remote login) are both directly relevant to any site using Cisco as the IDMZ firewall. A DoS on your ASA in the middle of a production shift is an availability event even without data loss. Patch or apply mitigations urgently. Also notable: Progress LoadMaster (CVE-2026-8037, unauth command injection), Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator (CVE-2026-16812, OS command injection), Check Point SmartConsole (CVE-2026-16232, auth bypass to admin), and two N-able N-central authentication bypasses (CVE-2026-18556 and its incomplete-patch follow-up CVE-2026-18577). N-able is widely used by MSPs managing remote sites — if your OT remote support is outsourced, ask your provider directly whether they have patched. Fortinet FortiOS (CVE-2025-68686) and JetBrains TeamCity (CVE-2026-63077, unauth RCE via agent polling) round out the perimeter/build-system exposure. On the endpoint and application side, Microsoft Windows AFD.sys (CVE-2026-68820) is a local privilege escalation being exploited in the wild — relevant for engineering workstations and HMI hosts. SharePoint deserialization (CVE-2026-50522) and the WordPress chain (CVE-2026-60137 + CVE-2026-63030) matter mostly for corporate IT but often host plant documentation and P&IDs. Prioritise the Cisco, N-able, and LoadMaster items this week if any face your OT DMZ.Read more →
