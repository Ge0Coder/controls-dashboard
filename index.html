Today's Knowledge Bite

The KNX BCU (Bus Coupling Unit) key is only 4 bytes long and, by default in many installations, is left at 0x00000000. That means an attacker who reaches the bus can often authenticate trivially — and thanks to CVE-2023-4346, can also set the key to a value that locks legitimate integrators out of their own devices without physical reset access.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronics: Nicolas Minorsky derived the three-term control law in 1922 while trying to automatically steer the USS New Mexico, observing how a helmsman corrected course based not just on the current heading error but on how long it had persisted and how fast it was changing. His paper 'Directional Stability of Automatically Steered Bodies' effectively invented modern feedback control before op-amps, transistors, or digital computers existed. Every loop you tune today is a descendant of a naval officer watching a sailor at a wheel.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA FX (Field eXchange) is quietly maturing from spec into shipping product. Where classic OPC UA solved controller-to-supervisor communication, FX extends the same information model, security, and PubSub transport down to controller-to-controller and controller-to-device — the space historically owned by PROFINET, EtherNet/IP, and EtherCAT. Combined with Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) at the Ethernet layer, the promise is deterministic, vendor-neutral communication from sensor to cloud on a single converged network. For controls engineers the practical implication is significant: standardised device profiles mean a Siemens PLC and a Rockwell drive could exchange motion or safety data without a gateway, and asset information (nameplate, diagnostics, calibration) travels with the device rather than living in a separate engineering tool. The security model inherits OPC UA's certificate-based authentication and per-message signing/encryption, which is a step change from most fieldbuses. Adoption challenges remain real: TSN-capable switches are still a price premium, tooling for certificate lifecycle management at fieldbus scale is immature, and the installed base of legacy fieldbus won't disappear this decade. Most greenfield sites are piloting FX in a single cell rather than ripping out PROFINET — a reasonable posture until multi-vendor interop demos move beyond the Hannover Messe stage.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) continues to be the standard most frequently cited in procurement specs this year, and the ISA Global Cybersecurity Alliance has been pushing consistent zone-and-conduit templates to reduce the wide variability in how integrators actually apply it. If you're drafting a Cybersecurity Requirements Specification (CRS) for a new project, aligning your zone diagram with 62443-3-2 language up front saves enormous rework at FAT. On the NIST side, SP 800-82 Rev 3 remains the go-to overlay for mapping the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 onto ICS environments, and its guidance on virtualised OT and cloud-connected control is now the reference most auditors expect. Worth noting that CSF 2.0's added 'Govern' function is showing up in insurance questionnaires — expect underwriters to ask who owns OT cyber risk at board level. For those in building automation, the KEV addition of the KNX flaw is a good prompt to revisit ISO/IEC 27019 and the KNX Secure guidelines. Many sites still run KNX with default BCU keys, and the standards clearly call that out as non-compliant even if the auditor never opened the panel.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the 2023 Aliquippa, Pennsylvania water authority incident, in which a Unitronics Vision-series PLC controlling a booster station was defaced by an actor claiming affiliation with CyberAv3ngers. The attacker didn't exploit a sophisticated vulnerability — the PLC was internet-exposed on TCP/20256 with the default password '1111'. The HMI was replaced with an anti-Israel message and the station tripped to manual, prompting an emergency response. What makes this incident enduringly instructive is how mundane the root cause was: a small utility purchased an Israeli-made PLC, an integrator commissioned it with defaults, and no one segmented it from the internet. There was no zero-day, no supply chain compromise, no insider — just Shodan-visible controllers with vendor defaults. Three lessons for controls engineers: first, treat any PLC with a web or VNC interface as internet-hostile by default and put it behind a firewall or VPN even for 'temporary' remote commissioning; second, change vendor defaults during FAT, not 'later' — later never comes; third, keep an authoritative inventory of controllers with their firmware, exposed ports, and credential status, because you cannot defend what you cannot list.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The dominant story today is Microsoft's July Patch Tuesday, which SANS ISC dubbed 'The AI Apocalypse' — a staggering 622 Microsoft vulnerabilities (plus 427 in Chromium) with 62 rated critical and at least two under active exploitation. CISA has urged immediate patching of three SharePoint flaws being exploited in the wild, two as zero-days. For controls engineers, SharePoint often underpins document control, procedure management, and MES-adjacent portals in Level 3/3.5 of the Purdue model, so this is not just an IT problem. On the endpoint and boot-chain front, researchers disclosed that old Microsoft-signed UEFI shim bootloaders can be abused to bypass Secure Boot on any system regardless of OS. Engineering workstations, HMIs, and historians built on Windows are directly in scope — many OT assets ship with dated firmware and rarely see DBX updates. Separately, a 'Nightmare Eclipse' Windows zero-day dubbed LegacyHive was disclosed with a stripped PoC, and severe vulnerabilities were patched in Trend Micro, Tanium, ESET, and Tenable products, several of which are commonly deployed to monitor OT DMZs. SANS also flagged active scanning for MCP servers and AI assistant credentials, a reminder that as sites experiment with LLM-based copilots for alarm triage or procedure lookup, the credentials and tool-invocation endpoints are already being probed. Meanwhile, an unpatched Cursor IDE flaw allowing auto-execution of a malicious git.exe placed in a repo root is a direct hazard for developers and integrators who clone PLC/HMI code from vendor or customer repositories. CISA Vulnerability Digest

Two entries in the past 30 days are particularly relevant to plant floor teams. CVE-2023-4346 in the KNX protocol's Connection Authorization Option 1 was added on 15 July: an overly restrictive lockout mechanism lets an attacker purge devices and set the BCU key to a locked state, effectively bricking building automation deployments unless additional security options are enabled. If you run KNX for HVAC, lighting, or facility controls, verify that KNX Secure or BCU key protection is properly configured and segment KNX/IP routers from general networks. CVE-2025-67038 in Lantronix EDS5000 device servers (command injection as root via the username field) and CVE-2026-12569 in PTC Windchill/FlexPLM (unauthenticated RCE) both hit OT directly — serial-to-Ethernet gateways and PLM systems are pervasive in manufacturing. Ubiquiti UniFi OS also picked up two KEV entries (command injection and path traversal), relevant wherever UniFi has crept into plant networking. Non-OT but still notable: SonicWall SMA1000 (SSRF and code injection), Oracle E-Business Suite, and multiple SharePoint entries. Priority actions: patch or isolate Lantronix EDS5000 and PTC Windchill immediately, review KNX installations for secure-mode configuration, and audit any SharePoint instances reachable from OT engineering VLANs.Read more →
