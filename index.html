☀ Morning Briefing 2026-06-27 09:29 UTC

The headline item for OT this weekend is CISA's addition of PTC Windchill (CVE-2026-12569) to the KEV catalogue — an unauthenticated RCE in a PLM platform that sits at the heart of most manufacturing engineering workflows. If your site uses Windchill or FlexPLM, treat this as a Monday-morning emergency patch and verify it is not exposed beyond the engineering zone. Pair that with the Lantronix EDS5000 root-level command injection if you run serial gateways, and the Check Point IKEv1 auth bypass if vendor VPNs terminate on Check Point appliances. The pattern this week is clear: engineering and remote-access infrastructure, not field controllers, are where attackers are getting their wins.

Today's Knowledge Bite

The Lantronix EDS-series devices being patched this week trace their lineage back to the original Lantronix MSS100 from 1998 — one of the first commercial serial-to-Ethernet device servers. A surprising number of plants still run firmware derivatives only two or three major revisions removed from that lineage, which is part of why the same class of command-injection bug keeps reappearing decades later.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was effectively reverse-engineered from a helmsman. In the 1920s, Nicolas Minorsky watched US Navy helmsmen steer the USS New Mexico and noticed they didn't just respond to current heading error — they also anticipated based on how fast the error was changing and remembered the persistent drift from earlier. He formalised those three behaviours mathematically in 1922, and the proportional-integral-derivative structure that runs in virtually every loop in your plant today is, at its origin, a model of human intuition under load.

Tech Spotlight

This week's spotlight is on OPC UA FX (Field eXchange), the extension of OPC UA into the controller-to-controller and controller-to-field-device layer. Traditional OPC UA has long handled north–south traffic from PLC to SCADA or MES. FX, finalised in its first release by the OPC Foundation and now appearing in products from Siemens, Rockwell, Schneider, Beckhoff and B&R, standardises east–west exchange between controllers from different vendors using the same information model, security model, and — crucially — running over TSN-capable Ethernet for deterministic timing. The practical implication is significant: for the first time, you can engineer a multi-vendor cell where a Rockwell ControlLogix and a Siemens S7-1500 exchange synchronised process data without a gateway, without proprietary middleware, and with mutual certificate-based authentication built in. For controls engineers this could mean fewer protocol converters, simpler diagnostics, and a security model that finally treats peer controllers as authenticated endpoints rather than implicitly trusted neighbours. Adoption challenges remain real. TSN switch infrastructure is still more expensive than standard managed switches, certificate lifecycle management at the controller level is a genuinely new operational discipline for most plants, and conformance between vendors is still maturing. Expect the next two years to be the proving ground; greenfield automotive and battery plants are leading deployment.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443 continues its slow but steady consolidation as the de facto global OT security standard. The most consequential recent activity is around IEC 62443-2-1, the requirements for an OT cybersecurity management system, which has been revised to align more closely with ISO 27001's management-system structure. For asset owners this matters because it makes joint IT/OT certification audits genuinely feasible, rather than the parallel, duplicative exercises many organisations currently run. On the product side, IEC 62443-4-2 component-level certification is now being demanded in procurement specifications across European utilities and increasingly in North American pharma and water sectors. If you are specifying new PLCs, RTUs, or HMIs, asking for a current 62443-4-2 SL2 certificate (with the certified configuration documented) is becoming a reasonable baseline rather than an exotic ask. Be aware that "62443-ready" marketing claims are not the same as a certificate from an accredited body such as TÜV, exida, or ISASecure. In parallel, NIST SP 800-82 Revision 3 remains the most useful free reference for OT security architecture, and the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0's added Govern function maps cleanly onto 62443-2-1 governance requirements — a useful crosswalk if you are reporting to a board that thinks in NIST CSF terms but operates plants engineered to IEC.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's case study is the 2024 American Water Works cyber incident, the largest regulated US water utility to publicly confirm a breach. In October 2024 the company detected unauthorised activity in its IT environment, took its MyWater customer portal offline, and paused billing. Critically, the company stated that water and wastewater operations were not affected — but the incident forced a hard look at how cleanly IT and OT were actually separated. What is now better understood about the event is that the initial intrusion vector appears to have been a compromised third-party remote-access pathway used for corporate IT support, and that the decision to proactively isolate systems — including shutting down some interfaces between corporate and operational environments — was what kept OT untouched. In other words, the segmentation worked, but only because the response team trusted it enough to sever connections quickly. Three lessons stand out. First, your IT/OT boundary is only as good as your willingness to actually close it under pressure; practise the disconnect. Second, billing and customer-portal outages have regulatory and reputational consequences even when treatment plants run normally — incident response plans should explicitly cover "OT is fine but the business is degraded" scenarios. Third, third-party remote access remains the single most common initial vector across water, energy, and manufacturing incidents; inventory every vendor tunnel and require MFA plus session recording on all of them.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

This week's news cycle is dominated by IT and cloud-side stories, but several have downstream implications for OT. The Amazon Q vulnerability that enabled cloud credential theft through malicious repositories is a reminder that as more plants push historian and MES data into AWS for analytics, a compromise of developer AI tooling can cascade into the OT data path. AWS has patched and issued an advisory; engineers using Q in any pipeline that touches plant data should validate scope of access tokens. The widening Klue–Salesforce breach now affects roughly two dozen companies, illustrating how third-party SaaS compromises propagate through supplier ecosystems. Controls engineers may not own these CRMs, but the same pattern — single vendor compromise, dozens of downstream impacts — increasingly applies to remote-access vendors, EDR providers, and cloud-hosted SCADA platforms. The Five Eyes joint AI threat warning called out in SecurityWeek's roundup is also worth flagging: agencies are now formally cautioning about adversarial use of generative AI for crafting targeted ICS phishing and reconnaissance. On the technical side, SANS ISC's diary on CVE-2024-40766 (SonicWall) makes a point every controls engineer should internalise: patching the binary is not the same as fixing the configuration. The original SonicWall flaw was remediated months ago, but exposed devices remain compromised because legacy local accounts and weak password policies persisted across the upgrade. Many OT perimeter firewalls fall into exactly this pattern — patched but never reconfigured. The Linux Foundation's new Akrites project for coordinated open-source vulnerability disclosure is quietly significant. A large amount of embedded firmware in PLCs, RTUs, and gateways ships with Linux and open-source components; a more structured disclosure channel should eventually shorten the gap between upstream fix and vendor-released firmware update.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The most OT-relevant addition this week is CVE-2026-12569 in PTC Windchill and FlexPLM (added 25 June). Windchill is widely deployed as the PLM backbone in manufacturing — holding CAD, BOMs, and engineering change records that feed MES and ERP. An unauthenticated remote code execution against Windchill is a serious supply-chain and IP-theft risk, and in integrated environments it can serve as a pivot point into the manufacturing zone. Patch immediately and verify that Windchill is not directly reachable from the enterprise edge. Also highly relevant: CVE-2025-67038 in the Lantronix EDS5000 device server, a root-level OS command injection via the username field. EDS-series serial-to-Ethernet gateways are common in legacy plants connecting older RS-232/485 instrumentation to TCP networks. Treat any internet- or DMZ-exposed Lantronix device as compromised until proven otherwise, and audit network segmentation around serial gateways. The trio of Ubiquiti UniFi OS flaws (CVE-2026-34908/34909/34910) matter wherever UniFi has crept into OT support networks, guard shacks, or remote pumping stations. Round out the patch list with Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager (two CVEs), Cisco Unified CM SSRF, Check Point Security Gateway IKEv1 auth bypass (ransomware-linked), Splunk Enterprise missing-auth, and Arista EOS tunnel decapsulation. The Check Point IKEv1 issue is especially worth attention if your site uses Check Point gateways to terminate vendor remote-access VPNs into the OT DMZ.Read more →
