Today's Knowledge Bite

The KNX protocol, still deployed in millions of commercial buildings worldwide, was standardised in 1990 and originally had no authentication whatsoever — any device on the twisted-pair bus could send any telegram. KNX Secure wasn't ratified until 2017, meaning the vast majority of installed KNX networks operate on essentially the same trust model as a 35-year-old fieldbus. This is why a lockout-bypass CVE from 2023 is still worth adding to the KEV catalog in 2026.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronic computing by roughly a century — Nicolas Minorsky formalised the three-term algorithm in 1922 while analysing the automatic steering of the USS New Mexico, but proportional-integral feedback had been used in James Watt's centrifugal governor as early as 1788, and integral action appeared in temperature regulators for Dutch incubators in the 1620s. What we teach as a modern control law is actually an artifact of trying to keep battleships pointing straight, and Minorsky's insight was essentially watching a helmsman and realising a good one anticipates (derivative) and remembers (integral) as well as reacts (proportional).

Tech Spotlight

Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) combined with OPC UA over the field level (Part 14 / FLC) is finally moving from spec to silicon in 2026. The promise is a single deterministic Ethernet fabric carrying motion-control traffic, PLC-to-PLC messaging, and enterprise data on the same wires, replacing the current patchwork of PROFINET IRT, EtherCAT, Sercos III, and CC-Link IE. Siemens, Beckhoff, B&R, and Rockwell all now ship TSN-capable controllers and switches, and the OPC Foundation's FLC companion specs are stable enough for pilot deployments. The practical implication for controls engineers is that vendor lock-in at the network layer is starting to erode. In theory, a Beckhoff drive, a Siemens PLC, and a Rockwell HMI could share a TSN network and speak OPC UA PubSub with sub-millisecond determinism. In practice, the challenges are real: TSN configuration models (fully distributed vs. centralised via a CNC) are not fully interoperable between vendors, network engineering skills that used to be an IT specialty are now required on the plant floor, and troubleshooting a converged network with mixed criticality traffic requires new tools. Expect the first production-scale TSN lines in automotive and semiconductor before it filters down to process industries, where existing PROFIBUS and HART installations will remain for another decade at minimum.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) continues to be the most-requested standard in EPC contracts this year, driven partly by the EU NIS2 directive's push for demonstrable risk methodology in critical sectors. If you're specifying a new control system in 2026, expect owner-operators to require a documented zone-and-conduit diagram with target security levels (SL-T) per zone before FAT — this used to be a nice-to-have and is increasingly a contract deliverable. ISA/IEC 62443-4-2 (component security requirements) certification is also picking up momentum with PLC and RTU vendors. Siemens, Schneider, ABB, and Honeywell all now have certified product lines at SL 2 or higher, and procurement teams are starting to make certification a hard requirement rather than a tiebreaker. The practical catch: SL 2 certification does not mean the product is secure out of the box — it means the capability exists. Configuring firmware signing, role-based access, and audit logging is still the integrator's job, and default configurations often ship insecure for backwards compatibility. NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 has now been in force long enough that CISA's cross-sector performance goals (CPGs) are being aligned directly to its control families, giving smaller operators a simpler on-ramp than jumping straight to 62443.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the 2024 Frontier Software / Australian payroll incident's OT parallel: the Nortura (Norwegian meat processor) ransomware event, where production at multiple abattoirs was halted for over a week after an IT-side ransomware infection propagated through insufficiently segmented interfaces to plant historians and MES systems. The PLCs themselves were never touched, yet production stopped because the order-management and traceability systems that feed the lines were encrypted — and food-safety regulations prohibit running without traceability. The attack path was mundane: phishing email, credential theft, lateral movement over flat Windows domain, then encryption of everything the compromised service account could reach. The MES and historian servers sat in the same Active Directory forest as corporate IT, with no separate authentication boundary. Three lessons for controls engineers: first, 'the PLCs are fine' is not the same as 'production is fine' — modern plants cannot run without the L3 systems, so those systems need OT-grade availability planning. Second, a separate AD forest or at minimum a one-way trust for OT is worth the operational friction; shared domain admin between IT and OT means one phish equals plant shutdown. Third, test your manual/paper fallback for traceability and batch records — if you can't legally ship product without a working MES, that MES is a safety-critical system whether the standard calls it one or not.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

Cisco published a large patch bundle this week addressing critical flaws in SD-WAN, IOS XE, and Secure Firewall Management Center (FMC), including at least one with public proof-of-concept code. For plant networks running Cisco edge gear in the DMZ or industrial site routers, this is a straightforward call to check firmware inventories and prioritise patching of any FMC deployments — particularly given that CVE-2026-20316 (hard-coded password in FMC) was already added to CISA's KEV on 29 July. If your firewall management console lives on the IT side but has visibility into OT rules, treat it as an OT-adjacent asset. Active exploitation of JetBrains TeamCity (CVE-2026-63077) is now confirmed. While TeamCity is a developer tool, many automation shops use it (or similar CI systems) to build HMI applications, containerised historians, or firmware artefacts. A compromised build server is a supply-chain foothold into OT: attackers can inject code into signed installers before they ever reach the plant floor. SANS Internet Storm Center continues to highlight the collapsing timeline for attackers — a guest diary this week documents automated SSH actors moving from initial login to persistence in 22 seconds. For jump hosts, engineering workstations exposed via VPN, or Linux-based edge gateways, that means brute-force protections, key-only auth, and fail2ban-style throttling are no longer optional. Separately, a botnet is now actively scanning for diagnostic-tool URLs, a reminder that vendor diagnostic web endpoints (which frequently ship with default creds on OT devices) are being enumerated at scale. On the supply-chain front, the keyv/cacheable npm worm compromise is notable for the counterintuitive advice: don't revoke tokens first, because doing so can destroy forensic evidence and tip off the attacker. Any SCADA vendor or integrator using Node.js tooling in their build pipelines should be reviewing dependency trees today.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The most OT-relevant KEV addition this cycle is CVE-2023-4346, a KNX Protocol authorisation weakness allowing an attacker to purge devices and lock out the BCU key on unprotected installations. KNX is ubiquitous in commercial building automation — HVAC, lighting, access control — and the vulnerability being catalogued now (despite a 2023 CVE) suggests CISA is seeing real exploitation. Facilities and building-automation teams should verify KNX Secure is enabled and that IP-side KNXnet/IP interfaces are not exposed beyond the OT VLAN. Cisco FMC (CVE-2026-20316, hard-coded credentials) and Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator (CVE-2026-16812, OS command injection) are both perimeter/management-plane bugs that often bridge IT and OT zones. Fortinet added three entries in July — FortiOS symbolic link persistence bypass and two FortiSandbox command injections — reinforcing that Fortinet gear at the IDMZ remains a top target. N-able N-central got two consecutive KEV entries within a day, the second because the first patch was incomplete; MSPs managing remote sites should assume compromise until fully patched and audit for lateral movement into customer OT networks. Langflow (two entries) and repeated SharePoint deserialisation flaws round out the batch. If your plant intranet hosts SharePoint for procedures or drawings, ensure the July patches are applied — deserialisation RCE in a document server is a classic pivot point into engineering workstations.Read more →
