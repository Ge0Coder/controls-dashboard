Today's Knowledge Bite

The Modbus protocol, still the workhorse of countless field networks, was published by Modicon in 1979 — the same year the Sony Walkman launched. It was designed for serial links at a time when 'cybersecurity' wasn't a concept, which is precisely why every modern Modbus/TCP deployment inherits an authentication-free trust model that engineers still work around 46 years later.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller most of us tune daily traces its rigorous mathematical form to a 1922 paper by Nicolas Minorsky, who derived it not for a factory but to steer the USS New Mexico. Watching helmsmen, he realised they instinctively applied proportional correction, anticipated based on the rate of change (derivative), and remembered persistent offset (integral) — so he formalised what human pilots were already doing. Nearly every temperature loop, flow loop, and motion controller in the world is, in a sense, a mathematical model of an early-20th-century sailor's intuition.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues its slow march from spec sheets to production floors. The pitch is compelling: a single Ethernet fabric carrying both deterministic, sub-millisecond control traffic and best-effort IT traffic, with OPC UA PubSub providing the semantic layer on top. In principle, this collapses the historic separation between fieldbus (Profinet IRT, EtherCAT, Sercos III) and higher-level SCADA integration into one converged network, eliminating protocol gateways and giving you a common information model from sensor to cloud. In practice, adoption remains constrained by the ecosystem. TSN requires switches that implement IEEE 802.1Qbv (time-aware shaper), 802.1AS (precision time sync) and 802.1Qcc (stream reservation) — and getting these interoperating across vendors is still fragile. Siemens, Beckhoff, B&R and Rockwell all have offerings but their TSN profiles differ in subtle ways. If you're piloting this, budget for network engineering skills you probably don't currently have on the controls team, and expect to spend significant time on stream configuration and PTP grandmaster planning. The security story is also immature: TSN itself provides no confidentiality, and OPC UA's security relies on certificate infrastructure that many OT teams still struggle to operate. For greenfield high-performance motion or robotics cells it's worth trialling; for brownfield retrofits, Profinet or EtherNet/IP with a separate OPC UA overlay remains the pragmatic choice.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443 continues to be the reference framework operators are being asked about in audits and insurance questionnaires. The most practically important recent activity is around IEC 62443-2-1 (2024 edition), which restructured the security program requirements for asset owners into a more usable set of policies, procedures and controls aligned with the maturity-level concept in 62443-2-4. If you last read the 2010 version, the new one is substantially different — the old 'CSMS' terminology is gone, and requirements are now organised around security program elements (SPEs) that map more cleanly to what a plant actually does. On the product side, 62443-4-1 (secure development lifecycle) and 62443-4-2 (component security requirements) certifications are now table-stakes when specifying new PLCs, RTUs and network devices. When writing tender documents, ask vendors for their specific Security Level Capability (SL-C) claim per component and evidence of 4-1 certification of the development process — not just marketing statements about 'IEC 62443 compliant.' Those are two different things and vendors routinely conflate them. Separately, NIST SP 800-82r3 (Guide to OT Security) remains the most readable free reference for engineers new to the field, and it maps its recommendations to both NIST CSF 2.0 and IEC 62443 zones-and-conduits, making it useful as a bridge document when you need to explain OT constraints to an IT security team fluent only in CSF.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week, given the SonicWall KEVs, is the 2023 compromise of a US water utility via an internet-exposed Unitronics Vision PLC — the 'CyberAv3ngers' campaign. Attackers scanned for Unitronics devices reachable on TCP/20256 with the default password '1111', logged in, and defaced the HMI with a political message while stopping a booster pump serving a Pennsylvania municipality. Service was restored quickly by switching to manual operation, but the incident triggered a joint CISA/FBI/NSA/EPA advisory and became a reference case for water-sector cyber policy. The root causes were mundane: the PLC was directly exposed to the public internet with a factory default credential, and no network segmentation or VPN separated it from the wider control system. It happened because a small utility with limited IT resources had allowed a systems integrator to enable remote access 'temporarily' years earlier, and nobody had reviewed it since. Three lessons stand out for controls engineers. First, inventory your public IP exposure using Shodan or Censys against your own ASN and integrator ranges — you will likely find something you didn't expect. Second, treat vendor 'remote access enabled' as a configuration item requiring change control, not a support convenience. Third, factory default credentials on any device with a control function are a P1 finding; if your commissioning checklist doesn't include forced password rotation with evidence capture, fix the checklist today.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The IT security news cycle this week is dominated by AI-adjacent security stories rather than pure ICS incidents, but several items carry indirect implications for OT. Google shipped its sixth Chrome zero-day of 2026 (a V8 type-confusion flaw), a reminder that engineering workstations, HMIs with embedded browsers, and any web-based SCADA client bundling Chromium components need patching soon. VMware also pushed critical fixes for Workstation and Fusion allowing VM-to-host code execution — relevant to any engineer running virtualised historians, engineering VMs, or lab environments on their laptops. On the geopolitical/data side, Manchester Airports Group had roughly 550GB and data on 8.8 million people leaked after refusing a ransom payment, reportedly following exposure of admin API keys. While an IT breach rather than an OT one, aviation-adjacent operational teams should note the initial access vector: leaked credentials/keys, still the most common route into industrial estates. Review secret-management practices for engineering repositories and CI/CD systems that touch control-system code. Several funding rounds highlight where the market is moving: Catch ($5M) for guardrailed AI assistants, HiddenLayer ($100M) for AI runtime security, and Capsule Security's 'AI Circuit Breaker' to stop rogue agents pre-execution. Expect vendors to increasingly pitch 'agentic' features into SCADA, MES and historian products — controls engineers should push back hard on any AI feature that has write access to setpoints, recipes or alarm limits without hard-coded interlocks and audit trails. SANS ISC content this week was routine (Stormcasts, a Guildma/Astaroth phishing writeup, and a honeypot guest diary) with no ICS-specific advisories, but the Astaroth campaign underlines that Portuguese-language phishing lures continue to target LATAM industrial operators.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added a large batch of KEVs this week with several that matter directly to OT perimeters. SonicWall SMA1000 appliances — commonly used as remote-access gateways for vendor and engineer VPN into plants — picked up two entries: CVE-2026-83548 (unauthenticated SSRF) and CVE-2026-83549 (authenticated OS command injection). If you rely on SMA1000 for third-party vendor access into your DMZ or Purdue Level 3.5, treat this as an emergency patch and audit admin sessions and firmware. JFrog Artifactory also appears twice (CVE-2026-82329 default-config auth bypass to admin, and CVE-2026-66384 path traversal); Artifactory is increasingly used to host container images and firmware artefacts for edge devices, so a compromise here is a supply-chain compromise. PaperCut NG/MF (CVE-2026-82078 and CVE-2026-81578, chainable to unauthenticated RCE) is worth flagging because print servers are frequently forgotten IT assets sitting on flat networks with operations. The BerriAI LiteLLM (CVE-2026-59822) and Kestra OSS (CVE-2026-49869) entries reflect the new attack surface being introduced by AI/automation glue services — if your data-science or MES integration team has stood these up near the OT boundary, inventory them now. Rounding out the list: a Linux kernel IPv6 privilege escalation (CVE-2026-53362) affecting most distros, a Citrix NetScaler DoS (CVE-2026-8452), and revived old-but-still-exploited entries in ownCloud, Oracle WebLogic proxy, and Microsoft SQL Server. Practical action: prioritise the SonicWall SMA1000, JFrog Artifactory, and PaperCut items this weekend if any are exposed; then sweep engineering workstations for Chrome and VMware Workstation/Fusion updates.Read more →
