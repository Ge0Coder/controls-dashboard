Today's Knowledge Bite

The oldest industrial control loop still in widespread conceptual use — the PID controller — predates electricity in industry. Elmer Sperry's gyroscopic ship steering work in 1911 and Nicolas Minorsky's 1922 mathematical formalization of three-term control for the USS New Mexico defined the algorithm long before the first pneumatic PID controllers reached refineries in the 1930s. Every tuning rule you use today, including Ziegler-Nichols from 1942, descends from steering warships, not stirring tanks.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The familiar 4-20 mA current loop was not chosen because 4 mA is a nice round number — it was chosen because it is enough current to reliably power a two-wire field transmitter's electronics while leaving unambiguous headroom to detect a broken wire as 0 mA. The upper limit of 20 mA traces back to the intrinsic safety limits for hazardous areas, where keeping current and voltage below ignition thresholds for flammable atmospheres was non-negotiable. The entire modern process industry's analog signaling convention is, in effect, a compromise between powering a sensor over two wires and not blowing up a refinery.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues its slow but steady march from demo booths to real deployments. The pitch is simple: a single deterministic Ethernet fabric carrying everything from motion-control cycle data at sub-millisecond jitter up to MES-level analytics, all over standard IEEE 802.1 TSN hardware with OPC UA PubSub as the application layer. In theory, this replaces the current tangled mix of EtherCAT, PROFINET IRT, Sercos, and Ethernet/IP with one open, vendor-neutral stack — and brings strong, built-in security via OPC UA's certificate and signing model. In practice, adoption is still gated by three realities. First, controller and drive vendors have been cautious about committing silicon and firmware to TSN until the IEC/IEEE 60802 industrial profile stabilizes — and it only reached a workable baseline in the last year. Second, your network team has to actually configure TSN streams, gate control lists, and time synchronization (gPTP), which is a very different skillset from traditional managed-switch VLAN work. Third, the installed base of PROFINET and EtherNet/IP is enormous and works well enough that few sites will rip and replace. The realistic near-term pattern is greenfield lines — especially in automotive and semiconductor fabs — using TSN as the machine backbone, bridged into legacy fieldbus islands via gateways. Controls engineers should start learning gPTP and the basics of TSN scheduling now; it will be in your next machine spec whether you asked for it or not.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) and 62443-4-2 (component requirements) remain the two parts of the family most actively referenced in procurement specs this year. If you are writing an RFQ for a new skid or control system, requiring the vendor to document the Security Level Target (SL-T) per zone and to deliver 4-2 compliant components at a specified Security Level Capability (SL-C) is now a reasonable, enforceable ask — not aspirational language. Several major DCS vendors now publish 4-2 certification evidence for their controllers; take advantage of this when scoring bids. On the NIST side, the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 "Govern" function is quietly reshaping how OT programs report upward. The addition of Govern makes it harder for plants to treat cybersecurity as a purely engineering concern — board-level oversight, roles, and supply chain risk management are now first-class citizens of the framework. If your site cyber program maps to CSF, make sure your Govern-level artifacts (policies, risk tolerance statements, third-party SLAs with integrators) actually exist and are not just assumed to live "somewhere in corporate." Also worth tracking: ISA's TR84.00.09 on cybersecurity for safety instrumented systems continues to be the practical bridge between 62443 and IEC 61511. For any SIS modification or new SIF, demanding evidence that cybersecurity has been assessed as part of the functional safety lifecycle — not after it — is both best practice and increasingly expected by insurers.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's insider extortion case against the unnamed industrial firm is the obvious choice. According to the SecurityWeek report, a former core infrastructure engineer with privileged access abused that access to delete administrator accounts, forcibly reset hundreds of user passwords, and then demanded 20 bitcoin as ransom to leave remaining systems intact. He is now serving prison time, but the operational disruption to the company was substantial and the recovery almost certainly touched engineering workstations, historians, and the AD trust that OT depends on. The mechanics are mundane: legitimate credentials, legitimate tools, and intimate knowledge of where the single points of failure were. There is no exotic malware here and no zero-day — just an administrator who knew exactly which domain controllers to break and which backup accounts to delete first. For plant environments where the OT domain often trusts the IT domain, or where a single integrator holds the keys to every engineering workstation across multiple sites, this is a near-worst-case scenario. Three lessons stand out. First, enforce true separation of duties on your OT administrative accounts — no single human should be able to delete all break-glass accounts without a second approver or a tamper-evident audit trail. Second, keep offline, air-gapped backups of AD, PLC programs, HMI projects, and historian databases, and test restoration quarterly; online backups accessible to the same admin credentials are not backups. Third, treat offboarding of privileged engineers as an incident response exercise, not an HR task — revoke VPN, jump-host, vendor-portal, and cloud-tenant access on the same day, and audit for pre-planted scheduled tasks or secondary accounts.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The headline story for controls engineers this week is the sentencing of a former core infrastructure engineer who, after a workplace dispute, deleted admin accounts, bulk-reset passwords, and attempted to extort his industrial employer for 20 bitcoin. This is a textbook insider-threat case that resonates directly with plant floor realities: the people with the deepest access to engineering workstations, historians, and domain controllers are frequently the hardest to monitor. It is a timely reminder to review break-glass procedures, enforce separation of duties on engineering AD, and ensure PLC and HMI backups are stored offline and outside the reach of any single administrator. On the supply chain and firmware front, the "Midnight Mimosa" campaign hitting budget Android devices pre-installed with malware across 150+ countries is relevant beyond IT. Cheap Android tablets are increasingly used as cost-effective HMIs, maintenance terminals, and mobile dashboards on plant floors. If you have unmanaged consumer-grade Android hardware touching your OT network, now is the time to inventory it and confirm it is strictly segmented. Secondary stories worth noting: the Tensorlake npm SDK compromise reinforces that even niche developer dependencies pulled into engineering tooling or MES integrations can carry risk, and the ccTLD hijacks against .gh, .sl, and .as — which resulted in valid HTTPS certificates issued for Google domains — illustrate how trust anchors above your control can quietly fail. For OT teams relying on cloud-based remote access or vendor portals, certificate pinning and strict DNS resolver hygiene matter more than ever. Finally, SANS reminded practitioners this week that TLP is a sharing protocol, not a classification scheme. Many OT programs conflate the two in their incident response plans; if your IR playbook uses TLP:AMBER as a de facto confidentiality label for engineering drawings or network diagrams, you need a proper internal classification layer underneath it.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA's recent KEV additions skew heavily toward perimeter and remote-access infrastructure that typically fronts OT environments. Three Citrix NetScaler ADC/Gateway CVEs (CVE-2026-88779, 88772, 88771) were added within a two-week window, covering memory-corruption RCE, DoS, and unauthenticated command injection. NetScaler is extremely common as the reverse proxy for vendor remote-support portals and jump-host access into plant networks — if your DMZ relies on it, patching is urgent and session logs should be reviewed for anomalous admin activity. Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager (CVE-2026-76504) and Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator (CVE-2026-93952) both allow unauthenticated access to admin-equivalent functionality. For multi-site manufacturers using SD-WAN to stitch plants together, compromise of the orchestrator is effectively compromise of every site's WAN policy. F5 BIG-IP APM (CVE-2026-94127) rounds out the access-layer picture with an unauthenticated heap overflow. Fortinet FortiMail (CVE-2026-104286) is less OT-direct but matters because phishing into engineering mailboxes remains the top initial access vector. Also worth flagging: MikroTik RouterOS (CVE-2026-67279) is widely deployed in smaller water, wastewater, and remote telemetry sites as a budget edge router. The chained unauthenticated RCE path is exactly the sort of flaw that gets swept up in opportunistic scanning against ICS-adjacent IP ranges. If you have RouterOS anywhere in your remote site inventory, treat this as a priority patch and verify management interfaces are not reachable from the WAN.Read more →
