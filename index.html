Today's Knowledge Bite

The original Modbus protocol, published by Modicon in 1979, was designed to run over serial links so slow (typically 9600 baud) that its designers deliberately omitted authentication and checksummed only for line noise, not tampering. Nearly five decades later, the same frame format — now tunnelled over TCP/502 — still carries setpoints in refineries and water plants worldwide. It is arguably the most successful protocol in industrial history precisely because it refused to evolve.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronics: the first mathematically rigorous three-term controller was implemented pneumatically by Elmer Sperry and later refined by Nicolas Minorsky in 1922 for automatic ship steering on the USS New Mexico. Minorsky derived the derivative term by watching helmsmen anticipate the ship's swing — he essentially reverse-engineered human intuition into a differential equation. The tuning rules Ziegler and Nichols published two decades later were empirical shortcuts for engineers who couldn't afford Minorsky's mathematics, and they're still what most of us reach for on a Monday morning loop tune.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues its slow but real march from trade-show demos into production. The pitch is compelling: a single Ethernet fabric that carries deterministic, sub-millisecond motion traffic alongside best-effort IT data, using open standards (IEEE 802.1Qbv scheduling, 802.1AS time sync) instead of vendor-proprietary rings like Profinet IRT or EtherCAT. For controls engineers this promises real cell-to-enterprise convergence: your servo drives, safety controllers, and MES data all ride the same switches, secured by OPC UA's built-in certificate model. The practical reality is messier. TSN-capable switches from Cisco, Hirschmann, and Siemens are shipping, but interoperability across vendors is still fragile — the CC-Link Partner Association and OPC Foundation FLC group are actively hammering out profiles. Configuration tooling is immature; scheduling a TSN network still often requires vendor-specific engineering software rather than a portable YANG/NETCONF workflow. And most installed drives simply don't speak it yet. Expect greenfield automotive body shops and semiconductor fabs to lead adoption over the next 2-3 years while brownfield process plants stay on Profinet and EtherNet/IP well into the 2030s.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) remains the standard most frequently cited in RFQs this year, and it's worth revisiting how it interacts with the newer 62443-3-3 SR/CR requirements. The zone-and-conduit methodology in 3-2 is deceptively simple on a whiteboard but tends to fall apart when engineers discover that the OEM skid they just installed contains four PLCs, a Windows HMI, and an unmanaged switch — effectively a mini-zone that needs its own conduit definitions to any parent SIS or DCS. On the NIST side, the NIST SP 800-82r3 revision (published 2023) continues to be the most practical free reference for OT security programs and is now being cross-referenced heavily in insurance questionnaires. If you're building a case for capex on network segmentation or monitoring, framing the ask against 800-82r3's overlay of the Cybersecurity Framework tends to resonate better with finance and audit than pure 62443 language. Also worth tracking: ISA is progressing work on 62443-4-2 Edition 2, which will tighten component-level requirements around secure boot and cryptographic key storage — expect to see this reflected in device certifications from Siemens, Rockwell, and Schneider over the next 18 months.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the 2024 Arnold Clark-style pivot pattern seen at a European specialty chemicals producer (publicly disclosed in early 2025 by their insurer's incident report). The attackers gained initial access via a phishing-delivered infostealer on a corporate laptop, harvested VPN credentials, then used a compromised vCenter administrator account to enumerate VMs across the estate — including the process historian and batch server VMs that sat on a poorly segmented DMZ. They did not deploy ransomware on the OT VMs, but they did exfiltrate two years of batch records and recipe data before encrypting the corporate side. The attackers never touched a PLC. They didn't need to — the intellectual property and the extortion leverage lived in the historian and MES. Recovery took 11 days because the historian VM backups had been stored on the same vCenter cluster that was compromised. Three lessons for controls engineers: first, your historian and batch/MES servers are Tier 1 targets even when they look like IT boxes — segment their hypervisor management plane from corporate AD. Second, keep at least one backup copy on immutable or air-gapped media that cannot be reached from the hypervisor management network. Third, recipe and batch data has real extortion value; treat exfiltration, not just encryption, as a plausible impact scenario in your risk assessments.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The IT-side news dominates today's landscape, but several items have direct implications for plant floor engineers. A critical Ruby on Rails arbitrary file read flaw dubbed KindaRails2Shell is being actively exploited to extract secrets and achieve RCE — worth checking if any HMI dashboards, historian frontends, or MES web apps in your environment ride on Rails. ServiceNow patched three critical code injection bugs; many asset owners run ServiceNow for change management and CMDB, which often holds sensitive OT asset inventories that would be highly attractive to an attacker. On the endpoint front, a group calling itself Nightmare Eclipse released a Kaspersky Endpoint Security exploit called HardBreacher. Kaspersky says it has patched, but any OT-adjacent engineering workstations or jump hosts still running Kaspersky should be pushed to current builds urgently. Anthropic also warned Claude users of widespread infostealer infections and is force-logging users out — a reminder that AI tooling credentials are now valuable secondary loot alongside VPN and RDP tokens. The McKesson breach (284M records claimed by ShinyHunters) is a pharma supply-chain story worth watching if you work in life sciences manufacturing, as downstream disruption to ordering and logistics tends to propagate. Finally, SecurityWeek's analysis of the Hugging Face incident makes an important point for anyone piloting AI copilots or agents in engineering environments: autonomous agents should be treated as highly privileged identities with the same scrutiny you'd apply to a service account with domain admin.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA's KEV additions over the last week reinforce a familiar pattern: attackers are chaining unauthenticated bugs with follow-on RCE in widely deployed enterprise software. The two PaperCut NG/MF flaws (CVE-2026-82078 and CVE-2026-81578) can be chained for unauthenticated configuration tampering plus arbitrary Java bytecode execution — relevant because PaperCut is commonly deployed on corporate print servers that sit in the same enclave as engineering document control and drawing repositories. Patch immediately and verify print servers cannot reach Level 2/3 OT segments. Also notable for OT-adjacent environments: Broadcom VMware vCenter path traversal (CVE-2026-59310) enabling RCE — many sites run SCADA, historian, and HMI VMs on vCenter, so an unpatched vCenter is a direct route to mass compromise of the industrial estate. Citrix NetScaler ADC/Gateway (CVE-2026-8452) is a DoS but sits at the remote access edge often used for vendor support. Microsoft SharePoint authentication bypass (CVE-2026-55040) and the IKE double-free (CVE-2026-33824) round out a busy week for perimeter and identity infrastructure. Practical priorities: patch vCenter and PaperCut first if exposed to any OT network; audit remote-access paths through NetScaler; and confirm engineering workstations have the Microsoft August rollup applied to close the IKE and SharePoint issues.Read more →
