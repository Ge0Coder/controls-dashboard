Today's Knowledge Bite

The original Modbus protocol, published by Modicon in 1979, was designed to run over 1200-baud serial links and specified a maximum of 247 slave addresses — a number chosen not for any electrical reason, but because the 8-bit address field reserved values 248–255 for future use that never materialised. Nearly 50 years later, that same 247-node limit still shapes how engineers segment RTU networks on modern TCP-encapsulated Modbus, even though the underlying transport could easily address millions of devices.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronics entirely — Elmer Sperry's gyrocompass autopilot for ships, patented in 1911, implemented proportional, integral and derivative action mechanically using flywheels, springs and dashpots to steer battleships. Nicholas Minorsky's 1922 mathematical analysis of that same system, done for the USS New Mexico, is what gave us the formal PID equations we still tune today. The 'derivative kick' problem that plagues modern controls engineers has a direct physical analogue in Sperry's original hardware: a spring-loaded rate arm would slam hard against its stops whenever the helmsman changed setpoint too quickly.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues to move from marketing slideware into real deployments, and it is worth understanding what it actually delivers. Classic OPC UA gives you a rich, secure, vendor-neutral information model but no timing guarantees — packets share Ethernet with everything else and jitter can spike unpredictably. TSN is a set of IEEE 802.1 amendments (notably 802.1Qbv scheduled traffic, 802.1AS time synchronisation and 802.1CB frame replication) that lets a standard Ethernet switch reserve deterministic time slots for critical traffic. Combine the two, add the OPC UA PubSub extension, and you get publish/subscribe communication with sub-microsecond synchronisation and bounded latency — enough to replace EtherCAT or PROFINET IRT for motion control in principle. The practical picture is messier. TSN-capable switches from Cisco, Hirschmann, Moxa and Siemens are shipping, but configuration is still fragmented: the CNC (Centralised Network Configuration) model promised by IEC/IEEE 60802 is only now stabilising, and most vendors ship proprietary configuration tools. Interoperability between different vendors' TSN silicon at the scheduling level is improving but not yet plug-and-play. For controls engineers this means TSN is currently viable within a single-vendor cell or line, and increasingly viable at the plant backbone, but end-to-end deterministic OT/IT convergence — the original vision — still requires significant integration effort and careful bandwidth planning.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) continues to be the standard most controls engineers encounter first when a corporate security team asks for a zone-and-conduit diagram. A point often missed: 62443-3-2 does not tell you what security level to target — it tells you how to derive the target Security Level (SL-T) from a documented risk assessment of each zone. If your integrator hands you a system with 'SL-2 everywhere' without a matching risk assessment, that is a compliance red flag, not a deliverable. Also worth watching is the ongoing alignment between IEC 62443-4-2 (component technical security requirements) and the EU Cyber Resilience Act, which enters full application in December 2027. Vendors selling PLCs, RTUs, drives and HMIs into Europe will need to demonstrate secure-by-design practices that map closely to 62443-4-1 processes and 4-2 component requirements. Expect firmware update cadence, SBOM availability and vulnerability disclosure timelines from your major automation vendors to change noticeably over the next 18 months — and start asking for SBOMs in your next procurement cycle, because you will need them. On the NIST side, the CSF 2.0 'Govern' function is quietly becoming the anchor most OT security programmes hang their governance documentation on, particularly for asset owners who need to satisfy both IT auditors and process safety regulators with a single framework.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the January 2024 Muleshoe, Texas water tower overflow, which received renewed attention after CISA and Mandiant published deeper technical detail. Attackers linked to the CyberArmyofRussia_Reborn persona accessed the HMI of a small municipal water utility through an internet-exposed remote access interface protected only by a default password. They manipulated setpoints on the tank-level control loop, causing a physical overflow of tens of thousands of gallons before an operator noticed and reverted to manual control. No treatment chemicals were affected and no one was harmed, but the physical consequence was unambiguous. The root cause was mundane: a VNC-style remote access service exposed to the internet on a non-standard port, using a vendor-default credential that had never been changed since commissioning. The HMI in question had no session logging enabled, so the initial forensic timeline had to be reconstructed from SCADA historian data and tank telemetry. Three lessons remain sharp for controls engineers. First, 'security by obscurity' via non-standard ports is trivially defeated by Shodan-class scanning — assume anything reachable from the internet will be found within hours. Second, commissioning credentials must be treated as a formal handover artefact; a signed checklist confirming default passwords have been changed should be part of every FAT and SAT. Third, historian data is often your only forensic record after an OT incident, so retention policies and time synchronisation across HMI, PLC and historian deserve the same rigour you would apply to a safety instrumented system.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

Today's news cycle is light on pure ICS content, but several items have direct implications for plant floor and OT teams. SecurityWeek reports a novel 'Cryptographic Context Injection' technique that smuggles malicious instructions past Grok and Gemini safety filters by decrypting payloads only inside the model's trusted execution context. As more engineering teams pilot LLM copilots for HMI configuration, alarm rationalisation and code review, this class of attack matters — the guardrails you trust in an engineering assistant can be bypassed in ways that traditional prompt filtering will not catch. A second identity-related story is worth flagging: researchers describe iAuthFlow V2, a phishing toolkit that registers an attacker-controlled passkey on a compromised account, giving persistent access even after passwords are reset and sessions revoked. Many OT organisations are mid-way through passkey and FIDO2 rollouts for remote engineering access; the assumption that a password rotation clears an intruder is no longer safe. Any remote access platform, jump host, or vendor portal that supports passkey enrolment needs a monitored enrolment workflow and periodic audit of registered authenticators. On the malware front, banking trojans Manic, Grandoreiro and ToxicPanda 2.0 remain active in Latin America and Europe. While not ICS-targeted, these families frequently land on shared enterprise endpoints that also host engineering VPN clients or vendor remote-support tools — a classic pivot vector into segmented OT. SANS ISC's DOUBLECUP write-up on pseudo-steganographic PNG payloads is a useful reminder that image-hosted droppers continue to evade naive perimeter inspection. Finally, Anthropic's expansion of its Claude-based security scanning tooling and the launch of an open-source defender fund signal continued momentum for AI-assisted code and configuration review. For controls engineers this is a double-edged sword: the same tooling that could audit IEC 61131-3 logic or vendor-supplied Python scripts is also being weaponised against defenders through the prompt-injection techniques described above.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added a heavy batch of KEV entries over the past two weeks, several of which sit squarely in typical OT-adjacent infrastructure. CVE-2026-59310 in Broadcom VMware vCenter (path traversal to RCE) and CVE-2026-20349 in Cisco Secure Firewall ASA/FTD (heap inspection DoS) are the highest-priority items for most plants — vCenter frequently hosts historian, MES and virtualised HMI workloads, while ASA/FTD units commonly enforce the IT/OT DMZ boundary. Add CVE-2026-20316 (hard-coded credentials in Cisco FMC) and CVE-2025-68686 (FortiOS information disclosure bypassing a prior symlink-persistence patch) and you have a strong argument to review every perimeter and management appliance touching your Purdue Level 3.5. On the Windows side, CVE-2026-33824 (IKE Service Extensions double-free, remote code execution) is particularly concerning for engineering workstations and jump hosts that terminate site-to-site or vendor VPNs. CVE-2026-55040 (SharePoint authentication bypass) and CVE-2026-68820 (Windows AFD use-after-free, local EoP) round out the Microsoft exposure. Patch these on any Level 3 or DMZ Windows systems as soon as your change window permits, and verify IKE-based tunnels are not exposed to untrusted networks. The remaining KEV entries — Zimbra, TrueConf, MLflow, Ray, Langflow, Metabase, JetBrains TeamCity, N-able N-central, Progress LoadMaster, Apache Tomcat — are less likely to sit in a control network, but N-central and LoadMaster deserve attention if your integrator or MSSP uses them for remote monitoring.
