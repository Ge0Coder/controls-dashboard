Today's Knowledge Bite

The original Modbus protocol, designed by Modicon in 1979 for the 584 PLC, used a 7-byte address field in its serial form but only ever standardised slave IDs 1–247 — addresses 248–255 were reserved and never used in 47 years. The reservation exists because early Modicon engineers wanted headroom for broadcast and diagnostic functions that were ultimately implemented via function codes instead. To this day, scanning tools that probe the full 0–255 range will occasionally trigger odd behaviour in non-compliant gateways that use those reserved IDs internally.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller's integral term has a counter-intuitive origin: it was first implemented mechanically in 1922 by Nicolas Minorsky, not for chemical processes, but to automatically steer the USS New Mexico battleship. Minorsky derived the three-term control law by watching helmsmen and noticing that good ones did not just react to current heading error (proportional), they also accounted for how long the ship had been off course (integral) and how fast the error was changing (derivative). The chemical and process industries did not adopt PID widely until the 1940s — meaning the algorithm running in tens of millions of loops worldwide today was originally a model of human intuition, reverse-engineered from sailors at the wheel.

Tech Spotlight

Deterministic Ethernet via TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues its slow march into real production deployments, particularly paired with OPC UA PubSub. The pitch is straightforward: replace EtherCAT, PROFINET IRT, Sercos III and CC-Link IE with a single IEEE 802.1-standard converged network where motion-control traffic, vision data, and enterprise traffic share the same wire with guaranteed latency bounds. Siemens, B&R, Beckhoff, and Rockwell all now ship TSN-capable controllers and switches, and the OPC Foundation's FLC (Field Level Communications) initiative has matured the device profiles substantially. The practical reality on plant floors in 2026 is messier. TSN requires every switch in the bounded path to support 802.1Qbv (time-aware shaping) and 802.1AS (gPTP time sync), which means rip-and-replace of unmanaged or legacy managed switches. Configuration is still vendor-specific despite the standards — engineering a TSN schedule across mixed Cisco/Siemens/Hirschmann hardware remains painful. And from a security standpoint, gPTP itself is largely unauthenticated; an attacker who can inject onto a TSN segment can desynchronise the clock domain and indirectly cause scheduled traffic to be dropped. For greenfield motion-heavy applications it is increasingly the right answer; for brownfield, a parallel TSN cell connected via a gateway to the existing fieldbus is the pragmatic path.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) continues to be the standard most often misapplied in practice. The standard requires defining the System Under Consideration, partitioning it into zones and conduits, and performing a cybersecurity risk assessment per zone — but in audits we keep seeing zone diagrams that are really just network diagrams with coloured boxes. A proper 3-2 zone is defined by a common security level target (SL-T), not by VLAN membership; a safety PLC and a process PLC on the same VLAN can and often should be in different zones with a documented conduit between them. IEC 62443-2-1 (the security program requirements for asset owners) is in the late stages of revision, with the 2024 edition replacing the 2010 original and aligning more closely with ISO 27001 control structures. If your organisation built its OT cyber program on the old 2-1, the gap analysis is worth doing now rather than at audit time — the new edition has substantially expanded requirements around supply chain, patch management governance, and incident response that go beyond what most asset owners currently document. NIST SP 800-82r3 (Guide to OT Security, finalised late 2023) remains the most accessible free reference for engineers who need to translate IEC 62443 into operational practice, and the NIST CSF 2.0 mappings to 800-82r3 published last year are genuinely useful for executive reporting.Read more →

Incident of the Week

The incident worth dwelling on this week is the Polish water utility breach itself. According to Poland's ABW security agency, attackers compromised ICS at five separate water treatment plants and gained the ability to modify equipment operational parameters. Public reporting suggests the initial access vector was internet-exposed engineering interfaces and weak or default credentials on HMI and remote-access systems — the same pattern seen in the 2023 Aliquippa, Pennsylvania attack on a Unitronics Vision PLC. No public health impact has been reported, suggesting either operator detection, conservative parameter changes, or downstream chemical-dosing safeguards that absorbed the attack. Three lessons stand out. First, small water utilities remain catastrophically under-defended, and the threshold for "interesting target" has dropped to anything internet-reachable with a recognisable HMI screen. If you support municipal customers, an asset inventory pass focused specifically on what is reachable from the public internet — not what should be, but what actually is — will almost always find something. Second, parameter-modification access is the threat model now, not just data exfiltration; any control you have that detects unexpected setpoint or recipe changes (alarm on write, golden-baseline comparison, or simple change-management logging) is worth its weight in gold. Third, physical/process safeguards — chlorine dosing limits, mechanical overpressure relief, manually-set hard limits in the field — are the last line of defence and should be assumed to be the only line that matters when the HMI is compromised.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The headline story for plant-floor engineers this weekend is Poland's internal security agency confirming that hackers breached ICS at five water treatment facilities and gained the ability to modify operational parameters — not just read data, but actually change setpoints affecting public water supply. No mass-poisoning event occurred, but the incident underscores that the wave of opportunistic attacks on small, under-resourced water utilities (which began with CyberAv3ngers and Volt Typhoon-style probing in 2023-2024) has now produced confirmed parameter-level access in an EU member state. Expect renewed regulator pressure on small water systems across Europe and North America. CISA published a heavy slate of vendor advisories this week, with ABB B&R dominating: Automation Studio (TLS/OPC-UA certificate validation weakness allowing server impersonation during ANSL or OPC-UA handshakes), Automation Runtime (denial-of-service against the runtime itself, CVE-2025-11044 and others), and B&R PVI (sensitive logging data exposure, mitigated by the fact logging is off by default). The Automation Studio issue is the most consequential of the three because man-in-the-middle against engineering-station-to-controller traffic is a direct path to malicious project downloads. Hitachi Energy PCM600 (the substation IED configuration tool used widely in transmission utilities) has an integrity-impacting flaw, and Johnson Controls CEM AC2000 access control has a CVSS 8.7 local privilege escalation that turns any badge-system workstation user into an admin. On the periphery but worth flagging: a train hacker arrest (likely related to the Polish radio-stop attacks of 2023-24 era), a new PamDOORa Linux backdoor, and a US government push toward a 72-hour patch cycle for federal systems — a cadence OT operators should not pretend they can match, but should at least track for IT-adjacent assets like jump hosts and historians. SANS also disclosed a second universal Linux LPE in two weeks ("Dirty Frag"), following Copy Fail (CVE-2026-31431, now in KEV). Any Linux-based HMI, historian, or edge gateway is in scope. Finally, the Canvas LMS outage and Trellix/RansomHouse breach are reminders that managed security and SaaS providers remain prime ransomware targets — relevant if your site relies on any cloud-managed OT asset inventory, EDR, or remote-access broker.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added two items to KEV this week that matter to OT environments. CVE-2026-0300 in Palo Alto PAN-OS is an unauthenticated out-of-bounds write in the User-ID Captive Portal that yields root RCE on PA-Series and VM firewalls — if your IT/OT boundary firewall is a Palo Alto with Captive Portal exposed, treat this as a drop-everything patch. CVE-2026-6973 in Ivanti EPMM (authenticated admin RCE) is lower urgency for OT but relevant where EPMM manages ruggedised tablets or mobile HMIs on the plant network. The other notable KEV trend is the steady accumulation of remote-management tool exploits: ConnectWise ScreenConnect (CVE-2024-1708), SimpleHelp (CVE-2024-57726/57728), and Kentico Xperience. These tools are commonly deployed by OEMs and integrators for remote support of PLCs, drives, and SCADA — often without the asset owner's full visibility. Now is a good time to inventory which third parties hold remote-access tooling into your environment and confirm they have patched. CVE-2026-31431 (Linux kernel "Copy Fail" LPE) added 1 May should drive a sweep of any Linux HMI, historian, or virtualisation host, with the new "Dirty Frag" flaw likely heading to KEV next.Read more →
