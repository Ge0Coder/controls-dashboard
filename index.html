Today's Knowledge Bite

The TDengine vulnerability flagged this week is a reminder that many plants have quietly replaced traditional historians like PI or Wonderware with open-source time-series databases — InfluxDB, TimescaleDB, TDengine, QuestDB — often installed by data science teams without OT security review. These databases frequently expose REST or SQL-like interfaces on default ports with weak or no authentication, and they now hold the same process data that used to sit behind hardened historian architectures.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was formalised not in a university lab but on a US Navy destroyer in the 1920s, when Nicolas Minorsky watched helmsmen steer the USS New Mexico and realised they were unconsciously applying proportional, integral, and derivative corrections based on heading error, accumulated drift, and rate of swing. His 1922 paper 'Directional Stability of Automatically Steered Bodies' derived the three-term control law from human behaviour, not from theory. Every PID loop tuned today — in a refinery, a 3D printer, or a drone — traces its lineage to a sailor's hands on a wheel.

Tech Spotlight

This week's spotlight is on time-sensitive networking (TSN) as a transport for OPC UA, sometimes marketed as OPC UA over TSN or OPC UA FX (Field eXchange). The idea is to unify what used to be separate worlds: deterministic, cycle-accurate fieldbus traffic (Profinet IRT, EtherCAT, Sercos) and best-effort IT-style traffic (MQTT, HTTPS, video) on the same standard Ethernet infrastructure. TSN provides the deterministic scheduling at layer 2 (IEEE 802.1Qbv time-aware shaper, 802.1AS time sync), while OPC UA FX provides the vendor-neutral information model on top. The practical implication is that a controls engineer can, in principle, run a servo drive control loop and an MES data feed over the same switch without one starving the other, and without proprietary ASICs in every device. That collapses cable trays, simplifies spare parts, and makes cross-vendor controller-to-controller communication realistic. Siemens, Rockwell, Beckhoff, B&R, and Schneider have all shown interoperability demos. Adoption is still slow for three reasons: TSN-capable switches remain expensive and configuration tooling is immature (no one has really nailed the engineering workflow for scheduling); most installed drives and I/O do not support TSN, so brownfield migration is a forklift job; and the OPC UA FX specifications for controller-to-controller and controller-to-device are still stabilising. Expect meaningful deployments in greenfield automotive, semiconductor, and packaging first — process industries will follow only when the OEMs bake it into standard product lines.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2, the standard covering security risk assessment for system design, remains the single most under-used part of the 62443 family in day-to-day engineering. It defines the concept of zones and conduits and requires a documented cybersecurity risk assessment that drives the target security level (SL-T) for each zone. In practice most sites still segment by 'what was easy to cable' rather than by 62443-3-2 risk logic, then retrofit security controls to match. Worth revisiting is 62443-2-4, which specifies security requirements for service providers — the integrators, OEM commissioning teams, and remote-support vendors who touch your systems. Given this week's ConnectWise ScreenConnect KEV entry, 62443-2-4 gives you the contractual language to require your integrators to demonstrate patch cadence, MFA, session logging, and least-privilege for their remote access tooling. If your integrator agreements do not reference 2-4, you are carrying risk that should sit with them. NIST is quietly progressing the next revision of SP 800-82 (Guide to OT Security), which now aligns more explicitly with the CSF 2.0 'Govern' function added last year. The practical takeaway: OT security governance is no longer a subsection of IT governance in the federal reference model — it is a peer with its own risk appetite, roles, and reporting lines. Private-sector auditors are starting to expect the same structure.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's spotlight is the 2023 Aliquippa, Pennsylvania water authority incident, which remains one of the clearest teaching cases for small utility operators. Attackers attributed to the Iran-linked CyberAv3ngers group compromised a Unitronics Vision series PLC controlling a booster pump station by simply connecting to it over the internet — the device was exposed on TCP 20256 with the factory default password '1111'. They defaced the HMI screen with a political message and took the pump offline; operators reverted to manual control and no water quality impact occurred, but the disruption was real. What made this incident so instructive was how mundane the failure was. There was no zero-day, no sophisticated malware, no insider — just an internet-exposed PLC with default credentials, discoverable via Shodan in seconds. The Unitronics devices were installed by a system integrator who left remote access enabled for their own convenience, and the utility did not know the exposure existed. CISA issued an advisory within days and later reported dozens of similar Unitronics compromises across US water utilities. Three lessons for controls engineers: first, inventory every device with an IP address and independently verify none are internet-reachable — do not trust the integrator's assurance, run your own external scan. Second, change every default credential on every device, including the ones you 'never log into' like managed switches, UPSs, and OT-side firewalls. Third, treat your integrator's remote access as a supply chain risk covered by contract (see 62443-2-4 above) — you own the consequence, so you must own the requirement.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The most consequential item for OT teams this week is the confirmed in-the-wild exploitation of Microsoft SharePoint flaw CVE-2026-65660, now on CISA's KEV list with a federal patch deadline of September 28. Many plants use SharePoint as the document backbone for procedures, HAZOP records, drawings, and change management — often bridging IT and OT domains — so a code-injection foothold there can quickly become a pivot into engineering workstations. If your site still runs on-premise SharePoint, treat this as a weekend patch even if you sit outside the federal mandate. Buried in SecurityWeek's 'In Other News' roundup is a detail worth pulling to the front: a newly reported flaw in TDengine, the open-source time-series database increasingly used for industrial telemetry and historian-adjacent workloads, threatens uptime for plants that have adopted it. The same column flags a fresh water utility exposure incident and Docker botnets scanning for AI API keys — a reminder that any internet-exposed containers you spun up for pilot analytics projects are being actively harvested. On the broader threat landscape, a new Windows botnet dubbed x47.c is notable because it uses xAI's Grok API to select from predefined command actions, effectively outsourcing its command-and-control decision logic to a public LLM. This is the operational template we expected: adversaries no longer need bespoke C2 infrastructure when they can hide traffic inside legitimate AI API calls. Expect this pattern to complicate egress monitoring on engineering networks that permit general internet access for OEM support tools. Geopolitically, the US and China have agreed to establish an AI safety communication channel and continue trade and military talks. CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added a heavy batch of KEV entries over the past two weeks, and several are directly relevant to OT perimeter and remote-access architecture. CVE-2026-85102 in Check Point Security Gateways allows unauthenticated remote code execution via site-to-site or remote-access VPN when certificate validation is improper — this is the exact device class many sites use to tunnel vendor support or corporate-to-plant traffic. Pair that with CVE-2026-93616 (Check Point management server path traversal) and any Check Point-based OT DMZ needs immediate patching and log review. The MikroTik RouterOS flaw (CVE-2026-67279) and Zyxel GS1900 switch buffer overflow (CVE-2026-7273) matter because both product lines show up frequently in small remote sites, pump stations, and cost-sensitive plant edges where enterprise-grade Cisco gear was skipped. The Zyxel bug is LAN-based and unauthenticated, so a single compromised HMI on that VLAN can pivot to switch OS command execution. Also patch Cisco ISE (CVE-2026-76460) and Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator (CVE-2026-93952) if you use them for OT segmentation policy or SD-WAN into remote assets. Finally, the ConnectWise ScreenConnect entry (CVE-2026-84869) deserves attention because ScreenConnect is a common vendor remote-support tool quietly installed on engineering workstations. The flaw allows file transfer and execution during an active session without host confirmation — meaning a compromised integrator account can drop payloads onto your PLC programming laptop without the operator seeing a prompt. Inventory where ScreenConnect (and similar tools like TeamViewer, AnyDesk) are installed and confirm they are patched and MFA-gated.Read more →
