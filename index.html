Today's Knowledge Bite

The RADIUS protocol, still ubiquitous for authenticating engineers into OT network gear, dates from 1991 and was originally designed by Livingston Enterprises for dial-up modem pools. Its use of MD5 for message integrity — the root cause of the Blast-RADIUS forgery class of bugs — was already considered weak by 2005, yet RADIUS over UDP/1812 remains the default AAA mechanism on virtually every industrial managed switch shipping today. The fix (mandatory Message-Authenticator attribute) has existed in RFC form since 2003 but is only now being enforced by vendors.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller's integral term has a surprising naval origin: Nicolas Minorsky derived the three-term control law in 1922 by watching helmsmen steer the USS New Mexico, observing that good helmsmen didn't just correct for current heading error (proportional) but also for how long the ship had been off course (integral) and how fast the heading was changing (derivative). His paper, "Directional Stability of Automatically Steered Bodies," predated the term "PID" by decades and was essentially ignored by industry until the 1940s. Every tuning rule you use today — Ziegler-Nichols included — descends from a sailor's intuition about a battleship's rudder.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) is finally moving from trade-show demos into pilot deployments, and it's worth understanding because it directly challenges the assumption that deterministic control traffic requires a separate fieldbus. TSN is a set of IEEE 802.1 amendments — primarily 802.1AS for time sync, 802.1Qbv for scheduled traffic, and 802.1CB for frame replication — that turn standard Ethernet into a deterministic transport with bounded latency in the microsecond range. Layered with OPC UA's PubSub model, the goal is one converged network carrying everything from MES queries to motion-control updates. The practical implication for controls engineers is that the historical IT/OT cable split could collapse into a single switched fabric where QoS is enforced by time slots rather than VLAN priorities. That's powerful for greenfield builds but creates real headaches for brownfield: TSN requires every switch in the path to support the relevant 802.1 features and share a common PTP grandmaster, and mixed-vendor interoperability is still patchy despite the Avnu Alliance's certification work. Adoption challenges today include limited PLC support (Beckhoff, Siemens and B&R are furthest along), the difficulty of engineering schedules across large topologies, and a genuine shortage of engineers who understand both 802.1Qbv configuration and OPC UA information modelling. Expect to see TSN first in machine-builder cells and robotics, not plant-wide backbones, for at least the next three to five years.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) and 62443-4-2 (technical security requirements for components) continue to be the standards most actively cited in procurement language this year, particularly for renewable energy and water sector projects driven by EU NIS2 and US EPA cyber requirements. Notably, the KACO inverter advisory today is the kind of issue 62443-4-2 component-level requirements are explicitly designed to prevent — CR 1.5 (authenticator management) prohibits credentials derived from public identifiers like serial numbers. Engineers writing tender specs should be requiring 62443-4-2 SL-2 certification from inverter and RTU vendors at minimum. On the framework side, NIST SP 800-82 Rev 3 remains the most practical reference document for OT security architecture, and its mapping to the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (which added the Govern function) is increasingly being used by US utilities to satisfy NERC CIP audit narratives. ISA also continues work on the ISA/IEC 62443-2-2 (security program ratings) document, which aims to give asset owners a defensible scoring methodology for their own security programs rather than just their suppliers — useful if your management asks "how secure are we, really?" Worth noting for substation engineers: IEC 62351 (security for IEC 61850, 60870-5 and DNP3) is gaining real traction now that Hitachi Energy, Siemens and SEL are shipping native support in newer relays and RTUs. The Hitachi RTU500 advisory today is a good prompt to check whether your fleet supports 62351-5 secure authentication for DNP3 and whether it's actually enabled.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's worth-revisiting incident is the 2021 Oldsmar water treatment plant near-miss in Florida, which has resurfaced in CISA training material because the root-cause pattern keeps recurring. An attacker (or, per FBI's later assessment, possibly an operator error misidentified as an attack) used a TeamViewer session left running on a shared HMI workstation to briefly change the sodium hydroxide setpoint from 100 ppm to 11,100 ppm. An on-shift operator noticed the cursor moving and reverted the change within minutes; downstream pH alarms and limit clamps would have caught it regardless. The attack chain was prosaic: shared credentials across multiple plants, an unsupported Windows 7 host, direct internet exposure of remote-access software, and no MFA. What makes this incident worth reflecting on five years later is that nearly identical exposures still exist across small water and wastewater utilities — and recent advisories around remote-access tools, hard-coded credentials (today's NAVTOR and KACO bulletins) and VPN bypasses (this month's KEV additions) show the underlying pattern is unchanged. Three lessons remain sharp: first, engineering safeguards (pH limits, dosing pump capacity, contact-time chemistry) saved Oldsmar — defence in depth at the process layer matters as much as the network layer. Second, audit your remote-access tools quarterly; uninstall what's not actively used and put what remains behind MFA and a jump host. Third, shared HMI credentials are still the single most common finding in OT assessments — individual named accounts with RADIUS or LDAP auth, plus session recording, eliminate an entire class of risk.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

It's a heavy advisory day for plant floor engineers. CISA published seven ICS advisories spanning Schneider Electric (Modicon Network Managed Switches affected by the RADIUS protocol forgery flaw, and EcoStruxure Panel Server with an authentication weakness), Siemens-distributed KACO Blueplanet inverters (credentials derivable from serial number — a recurring renewables-sector design sin), Hitachi Energy MACH HiDraw (buffer overflow leading to DoS or RCE), Hitachi Energy RTU500 (availability-impacting bugs in a widely deployed substation gateway), and NAVTOR NavBox in the maritime sector with hard-coded credentials. The RADIUS issue (Blast-RADIUS family) is particularly worth attention because RADIUS is commonly used for engineer/operator authentication into managed switches, firewalls, and jumphosts across OT networks — mitigations typically require enabling Message-Authenticator attributes end-to-end. On the IT side that bleeds into OT, Microsoft's June Patch Tuesday shipped fixes for 204 vulnerabilities including a previously zero-day Exchange Server bug (CVE-2026-42897), and Splunk and Palo Alto Networks both released patches for severe flaws. Given Splunk is widely deployed for OT SOC use cases and Palo Alto firewalls frequently form the IT/OT boundary, both warrant prompt review through your management-of-change process. The FBI's seizure of 13 China-linked fake consulting websites targeting cleared US workers is a reminder that social engineering against engineers — particularly those with access to critical infrastructure designs — remains a primary recruitment vector. Combined with SecurityWeek's reporting that infostealers are now the dominant credential-theft channel feeding ransomware crews, the practical implication is that engineering laptops with VPN credentials, vendor portal logins, and stored RDP sessions are high-value targets even when they never touch the plant network directly. Finally, the SANS ISC continues tracking the TeamPCP supply chain campaign, which has now drawn formal US government attention. Supply-chain compromise of security tooling — the original SANS paper was titled "When the Security Scanner Became the Weapon" — should reinforce caution about what's installed on engineering workstations and historian servers.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The last 30 days of KEV additions skew heavily toward network edge and VPN devices that often form the IT/OT boundary. Check Point Security Gateway (CVE-2026-50751, IKEv1 authentication bypass, ransomware-linked) and Palo Alto PAN-OS (CVE-2026-0257, VPN authentication bypass) are both pre-auth flaws that let attackers establish unauthorised VPN sessions — exactly the foothold needed to reach engineering DMZs. Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager (CVE-2026-20245) and Arista EOS (CVE-2026-7473) round out the network gear additions; if your WAN to remote sites runs through any of these, patch windows need to be scheduled now. Also notable is CVE-2022-0492 (Linux kernel cgroups v1 privilege escalation) being added — a reminder that container escape primitives stay weaponised for years, relevant if you run containerised historians, MQTT brokers, or edge gateways. SolarWinds Serv-U (CVE-2026-28318) and Oracle WebLogic (CVE-2024-21182) remain common in industrial back-office stacks for file transfer and MES integration respectively. The supply-chain entries for Nx Console and TanStack npm packages are less directly OT-relevant but matter if your IT/OT integration teams or SI partners use modern JavaScript tooling. Practical action: prioritise the Check Point and Palo Alto VPN bugs first (active exploitation, perimeter exposure), then the Cisco/Arista network gear, then internal application servers.
