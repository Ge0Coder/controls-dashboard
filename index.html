Today's Knowledge Bite

The original Modbus protocol, published by Modicon in 1979, was designed to run over an RS-232 serial link at 9600 baud with no authentication because the physical cable was the security boundary. Nearly fifty years later, the same protocol frames — unchanged — now ride on TCP/IP across routed networks and the public internet, which is why Shodan still finds tens of thousands of exposed Modbus/TCP devices on port 502. The protocol didn't fail; the assumption about where the wire ended did.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was formalized not by a mathematician but by Nicolas Minorsky in 1922, while analyzing how helmsmen steered the USS New Mexico. He noticed experienced helmsmen didn't just correct based on current heading error — they anticipated using the rate of change and compensated for persistent bias from wind and current. He translated that intuition into the three-term equation, essentially publishing a formal description of what skilled humans had been doing by feel for centuries. Every loop you tune today is a mathematical fossil of a sailor's wrist.

Tech Spotlight

Deterministic Ethernet via Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) is quietly moving from spec sheets into real deployments, particularly for motion control and safety-critical cell-level networks. TSN is a set of IEEE 802.1 extensions (notably 802.1AS for time sync, 802.1Qbv for scheduled traffic, and 802.1CB for frame replication) that turn ordinary Ethernet into a network capable of microsecond-level determinism — something previously only achievable with EtherCAT, PROFINET IRT, or SERCOS III. For controls engineers, the practical appeal is convergence: a single physical network carrying hard real-time motion traffic, standard PROFINET/EtherNet-IP, video, and even OT security telemetry, without one stepping on another. OPC UA over TSN (the FLC working group) is the clearest path to vendor-neutral deterministic communication between PLCs from different vendors — a long-standing pain point on multi-OEM lines. Adoption is still bumpy. TSN requires end-to-end support — every switch, NIC, and endpoint must be TSN-aware, and the configuration models (fully distributed, centralized, or hybrid) are still shaking out. Most shipping TSN installs today are vendor-locked within a single ecosystem (B&R, Siemens, Rockwell), and interoperability remains more promise than practice. If you're specifying new infrastructure for a greenfield line, insist on TSN-capable hardware even if you don't turn the features on yet — the retrofit cost later is punishing.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) continues to see increasing adoption pressure as a contractual requirement, especially from European operators responding to NIS2 enforcement that ramped up this year. The standard's zone-and-conduit model is becoming the lingua franca for RFPs, and engineering firms that can produce a defensible 3-2 risk assessment are winning work over those still doing informal segmentation diagrams. If your organization hasn't formally documented zones and conduits for a brownfield site, that's increasingly the first thing auditors ask for. On the US side, NIST SP 800-82 Revision 3 remains the practical reference for OT security program design, and its alignment with the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 makes it easier to map OT controls into enterprise-wide reporting. ISA/IEC 62443-4-2 (component-level technical requirements) is also increasingly cited by PLC and RTU vendors in their security certifications — Siemens, Schneider, and Rockwell are all now shipping products with 62443-4-2 SL2 certification as a baseline marketing claim. Treat these certifications as a starting point, not a finish line: SL2 assumes a benign insider environment, which is rarely a safe assumption for a shared engineering workstation.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the 2023 Aliquippa water authority incident in Pennsylvania, where attackers affiliated with the CyberAv3ngers group compromised an internet-exposed Unitronics Vision series PLC controlling a booster pump station. The attackers didn't disrupt water quality — they defaced the HMI with a political message — but they had full logic-level access to a device managing public water infrastructure. The root cause was mundane: the PLC was exposed to the internet on port 20256 (Unitronics' PCOM protocol) with the default password '1111'. No VPN, no firewall rule, no segmentation — just a device shipped with defaults and plugged into a modem. The attackers used Shodan-style scanning to find it along with dozens of similar units at other utilities. The lessons are evergreen and worth repeating in every design review: never expose a PLC's native protocol port to the internet, change default credentials before commissioning (and verify in FAT), inventory what's actually reachable from outside your firewall (not what you think is reachable), and segment vendor remote access behind a hardened jump host with MFA. The Aliquippa case was embarrassingly simple to prevent — which is precisely why it keeps happening.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The headline event this week is PHP2Shell (CVE-2026-109618/109619), a critical pre-auth flaw in PHP that attackers began probing on October 17 — days before patches landed. Mass exploitation kicked off almost immediately after disclosure, with SANS raising InfoCon to yellow and vendors scrambling to assess exposure. For OT environments, PHP tends to lurk in historian web frontends, MES dashboards, vendor support portals, and the countless small Linux appliances that run plant floor web interfaces. Any externally reachable PHP service should be considered compromised until proven otherwise. Oracle's October CPU is unusually large at over 1,200 patches, nearly 100 critical. Fusion Middleware, WebLogic, and the MySQL stack received the heaviest attention — all of which commonly underpin manufacturing analytics, LIMS, and ERP-to-MES integration layers. Splunk and SolarWinds also pushed updates addressing code execution and privilege escalation, both widely deployed in OT SOC and network management roles. Cisco released fixes for 34 vulnerabilities across its product line, including critical issues enabling command execution and auth bypass. Given Cisco's dominance in industrial networking (IE switches, ISA3000, Catalyst in Purdue Level 3), controls teams should check whether their devices are on the affected list before next week's maintenance window. A quieter but notable item: SANS published a YARA rule for orphaned VBA projects embedded in DWG (AutoCAD) files. Weaponized CAD drawings are a plausible delivery vector into engineering workstations, which typically have broad access to the plant network and poor endpoint controls. Worth sharing with your engineering document control process.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added Siemens Software Center (CVE-2026-110936) to the KEV catalog on October 20 — an uncontrolled search path (DLL hijack) flaw letting an authenticated local attacker gain code execution via a planted DLL. Siemens Software Center is installed on virtually every engineering workstation running TIA Portal, PCS 7, or NX, making this a high-value foothold for lateral movement into the engineering domain. Patch via the latest Software Center release and audit workstation directories writable by standard users. Also highly relevant for OT: BeyondTrust Remote Support (CVE-2026-102764) auth bypass, Fortinet path traversal across FortiOS/FortiProxy (CVE-2025-61624), Palo Alto PAN-OS root RCE (CVE-2026-0322), Ivanti Connect Secure auth bypass (CVE-2026-104935), and Cisco Secure Email Gateway path traversal (CVE-2026-76519). These are the exact perimeter and remote-access products sitting between the enterprise and the DMZ — the hinge points for every ransomware incident that has crossed into OT in the past two years. Prioritize the Palo Alto and Ivanti fixes if you use either for IT/OT segmentation. The backfill of older CVEs (BIND 2015, Struts 2016, ProFTPD 2015) is a reminder that CISA still sees exploitation of ancient flaws — exactly the vintage of software that ends up on neglected plant servers. If you have an asset you haven't patched since commissioning, assume it's on someone's target list.Read more →
