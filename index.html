Today's Knowledge Bite

The Lantronix-style serial device server is arguably the most under-appreciated attack surface in OT. A single 1U box can bridge dozens of RS-232/485 controllers — drives, weighbridges, analysers, legacy PLCs — into TCP/IP, and because the serial side has no concept of authentication, full compromise of the gateway is effectively physical-bus access to every device hanging off it. Many of these gateways have been in service for 15+ years on firmware that predates TLS 1.2.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronic computing by decades — Elmer Sperry's gyrocompass autopilots in the 1910s and Nicolas Minorsky's 1922 analysis of USS New Mexico's steering used proportional, integral, and derivative action in pneumatic and mechanical form long before anyone wrote a line of ladder logic. Minorsky derived the algorithm not from theory but by watching helmsmen, noticing they corrected not just for current heading error but for how long it had persisted and how fast it was changing. Almost every tuning rule you use today is a descendant of that observation of human behaviour on a battleship's bridge.

Tech Spotlight

Today's spotlight is on OPC UA FX (Field eXchange), the extension of OPC UA standardised by the OPC Foundation together with the major automation vendors to push UA down to the controller-to-controller and controller-to-device layer. Traditionally OPC UA lived between SCADA and PLCs; OPC UA FX aims to replace the patchwork of EtherNet/IP, PROFINET, and Modbus traffic between controllers with a single information-model-driven, secure protocol, running over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) for deterministic timing. For controls engineers the practical implication is significant: instead of mapping tags by address and praying the I/O tree stays aligned, devices advertise themselves with semantic information models, so an engineering tool can discover a drive and know it is a drive, with standardised objects for speed, torque, and faults. Combined with TSN, you can in principle mix safety, motion, and best-effort traffic on one converged network without the dedicated fieldbus cabling. Adoption is still early. TSN switches are available from Cisco, Hirschmann, Siemens, and others, but interoperability across vendors remains uneven, and few controllers ship native FX endpoints today. Expect pilot deployments in greenfield automotive and semiconductor lines first, with brownfield migration likely 5+ years out. The honest near-term value is the information model — even without TSN, semantic discovery dramatically reduces commissioning time.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) and 62443-4-2 (technical security requirements for components) continue to be the most-cited standards in current OT procurement language, and they are increasingly being referenced by national regulators. The EU Cyber Resilience Act, which begins applying its main obligations in late 2027, leans heavily on 62443-4-2 as the de facto evidence base for industrial components, so vendors are racing to get controllers, drives, and gateways certified to at least Security Level 2. In parallel, the NIST IoT guidance update reopened this week for public review extends the NIST IR 8259 baseline and SP 800-213 federal profile. While not OT-specific, it is converging with 62443-4-2 on the same themes: device identity, secure update, configurability, and logging. If you procure edge devices or IIoT gateways, asking vendors to map their product against both standards is becoming a reasonable expectation rather than an unusual one. For day-to-day work, the most useful thing a controls engineer can do this quarter is read 62443-3-3 (system security requirements and security levels) and use its seven foundational requirements — identification, use control, system integrity, data confidentiality, restricted data flow, timely response, and resource availability — as a checklist when reviewing a new skid, MES integration, or remote-access design. It scales down to small projects far better than its length suggests.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's reflection is on the 2024 Arkansas City, Kansas water treatment incident, which became public knowledge in stages and is still informing OT defensive practice in 2026. Attackers reached the facility's SCADA environment via a remote access pathway that had been intended for vendor support, forcing the utility to switch to fully manual operations for several days while incident responders rebuilt the affected systems. No contaminated water was distributed, but production capacity dropped and chemical dosing had to be controlled by operators on the floor with handheld instruments. The technical root cause was familiar: a remote access service exposed to the internet, weak authentication, and flat network architecture that allowed lateral movement from a business-side foothold straight into the control LAN. The operational root cause was more interesting — the facility had documented its remote access in a way that emphasised availability for vendors rather than restriction, and there was no routine review of who could reach what. Three lessons remain durable for controls engineers. First, every remote access path needs an owner, an expiry date, and a quarterly review; vendor VPNs that have been live for five years are liabilities, not assets. Second, manual fallback procedures need to be exercised, not just documented — the Arkansas City operators coped because they had recent muscle memory for manual operation. Third, segmentation between business IT and the control LAN is still the single highest-leverage control; a properly enforced DMZ with brokered jump hosts would likely have stopped this intrusion well short of the SCADA HMIs.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The most plant-relevant story today is the disclosure that CVE-2026-20245, a Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager command-injection flaw, was exploited in the wild for months before patches were available. This is the seventh Cisco SD-WAN bug exploited in 2026 and matters to any site using SD-WAN to interconnect remote facilities, substations, or pumping stations to corporate or cloud SCADA. If your IT team manages SD-WAN on your behalf, confirm patch status and review logs for unusual vManage activity going back at least 90 days. NIST has reopened its IoT security guidance (the NIST IR 8259/SP 800-213 family update) for public comment. Although framed for federal IoT, the device-level baseline — identifiable inventory, configurable security, signed firmware updates, data protection, and cyber state awareness — is increasingly being lifted into private-sector OT procurement specs. Controls engineers writing PLC, RTU, or edge-gateway requirements should track this draft because it will shape what vendors ship over the next two years. On the supply-chain side, a 25-year-old vulnerability in curl was patched. Curl is embedded in vast amounts of HMI, gateway, and historian firmware; while none of the 18 fixes are rated critical, this is a good reminder to ask vendors for an SBOM and confirm which curl version ships in your edge devices. Microsoft and partners also disrupted the Amadey and StealC malware C2 infrastructure — both info-stealers commonly used to harvest engineering workstation credentials that later enable OT intrusions. Finally, SecurityWeek's piece on AI agent traps is worth a skim if your organisation is piloting LLM-driven assistants for operations, alarm triage, or maintenance documentation. The takeaway is that any data the agent reads — a PDF datasheet, a wiki page, a vendor manual — can carry hidden instructions that hijack the agent's behaviour. Keep AI assistants out of any path that can write to controllers or change setpoints.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The standout KEV addition for OT is CVE-2025-67038 in the Lantronix EDS5000 device server: a command injection in the username field that executes as root with no authentication. EDS-family serial-to-Ethernet gateways are widely deployed to bring legacy Modbus RTU, DNP3, and proprietary serial protocols onto IP networks, often sitting deep inside Purdue Level 1/2. If you have these devices reachable from any engineering VLAN, treat this as urgent: pull them off routable networks, restrict management to a jump host, and check Lantronix advisories for firmware. Compromise here yields a root foothold adjacent to your PLCs. Three Ubiquiti UniFi OS flaws (CVE-2026-34908/34909/34910) were also added — command injection, path traversal, and improper access control. UniFi gear has crept into many smaller plants and remote sites as cheap wireless and switching infrastructure; if it carries OT traffic, patch promptly and ensure management interfaces are not exposed to the OT VLAN. Round this out with the Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN entries (CVE-2026-20245 and 20262), Check Point IKEv1 auth bypass (CVE-2026-50751, ransomware-linked), and Ivanti Sentry root RCE (CVE-2026-10520) — all perimeter/edge devices that frequently form the IT/OT boundary. For practical triage: inventory any Lantronix, Ubiquiti, Cisco SD-WAN, Check Point, and Ivanti devices that touch OT, confirm none expose management to plant networks, and schedule patch windows. Where patching requires a process outage, compensate with strict ACLs and out-of-band management until the next planned shutdown.Read more →
