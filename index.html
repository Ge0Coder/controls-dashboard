Today's Knowledge Bite

The original PID controller was mechanical, not electronic. Elmer Sperry's ship-steering gyrocompass autopilot from 1911 implemented proportional, integral, and derivative action using gears, weights, and hydraulic valves — decades before Ziegler and Nichols formalized tuning rules in 1942. The math we still teach in control theory courses was reverse-engineered from hardware that already worked.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The centrifugal governor that James Watt adapted for steam engines in 1788 is often called the first industrial feedback controller, but it was actually James Clerk Maxwell's 1868 paper 'On Governors' that first analyzed why some governors hunted (oscillated) and others were stable — inventing the mathematical foundation of control theory as a side project while he was busy with electromagnetism. Maxwell showed the stability question reduced to whether the roots of a characteristic equation had negative real parts, which is still exactly how we analyze control loop stability today. The Routh-Hurwitz criterion you may remember from university came directly out of the problem Maxwell posed.

Tech Spotlight

Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) combined with OPC UA is finally moving from trade-show demo to real deployments in 2026, and it is worth understanding even if your plant is years away from adopting it. The premise is deceptively simple: give standard Ethernet the deterministic timing guarantees that used to require Profinet IRT, EtherCAT, or Sercos, while carrying OPC UA PubSub payloads that any vendor's device can parse. The IEEE 802.1 TSN suite handles scheduling (Qbv), frame preemption (Qbu), and clock synchronization (AS), while OPC UA provides the semantic layer and security model. The practical implication for controls engineers is convergence: motion control, safety, discrete I/O, and supervisory data can share a single physical network without the traditional fieldbus segregation. That collapses cable counts and simplifies architecture drawings, but it also means a switch misconfiguration or a rogue device can now impact motion loops that used to live on an isolated ring. Careful VLAN and TSN stream reservation planning becomes a safety-relevant activity. Adoption challenges remain real. TSN-capable switches from Cisco, Hirschmann, and Moxa carry a premium, and interoperability across vendor stacks is still uneven despite conformance testing at the OPC Foundation and Avnu Alliance plugfests. Most greenfield sites piloting TSN today are automotive assembly and semiconductor fabs where the deterministic-plus-open value proposition justifies the cost. For everyone else, the smart move is to specify TSN-ready hardware on capital projects even if you do not enable the features immediately.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) continues to gain traction as regulators in the EU cite it under NIS2 implementation guidance. The standard's zone-and-conduit methodology has become the de facto lingua franca for describing OT segmentation, and if you are being asked to document your architecture for an audit or insurance renewal, expressing it in 62443 terms is now the path of least resistance. The companion 62443-4-2 component requirements are increasingly showing up in procurement specifications, so expect vendor bid documents to start including SL-C (capability security level) claims. NIST is progressing work on SP 800-82 Revision 3, the Guide to Operational Technology Security, which better aligns with the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 released last year. The revision expands coverage of cloud-connected OT, remote access, and supply chain — all areas where the previous revision showed its age. Worth reviewing if you are updating internal OT security policies this planning cycle. On the deception guidance CISA issued this week, expect it to be referenced in future sector-specific guidance from EPA, TSA, and the Coast Guard. Deception technology has historically been an IT security tool, and its formal endorsement for critical infrastructure gives asset owners top cover to invest in honeypot-style PLCs and decoy engineering workstations without having to justify the approach from first principles.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the 2023 Aliquippa, Pennsylvania water authority incident, where an Iranian-linked group known as CyberAv3ngers compromised a Unitronics Vision-series PLC controlling a booster station. The attackers did not exploit a novel vulnerability — they simply found the PLC exposed to the internet with the default password '1111' on TCP port 20256, defaced the HMI screen with an anti-Israel message, and forced the station into manual operation. Water pressure was maintained by operators, and no public health impact resulted, but the incident triggered EPA and CISA advisories and a wave of Shodan-driven scanning against Unitronics devices worldwide. What made the incident significant was not sophistication but scale: within weeks, similar Unitronics devices were compromised at water utilities across multiple US states, all sharing the same default credential and exposed port. The attackers were essentially running an automated script against a Shodan search. Three lessons stand out for controls engineers. First, no PLC should ever be directly reachable from the internet — full stop — regardless of how convenient remote programming is for the integrator. Second, default credentials on OT devices are not a legacy problem; they are a current problem, and asset inventory should include a credential audit. Third, small utilities and remote sites are disproportionately targeted precisely because they lack dedicated security staff, so mutual aid arrangements and shared managed-service models are worth exploring at the sector level.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The dominant story today is Cisco's emergency patch cycle. An actively exploited zero-day in Cisco Identity Services Engine (CVE-2026-76460) allows unauthenticated remote attackers to bypass authentication via crafted requests, and it landed on CISA's KEV list the same day Cisco disclosed dozens of additional flaws across Firepower Management Center (FMC), ISE, and Nexus Dashboard. For OT environments that rely on ISE for network access control on the industrial DMZ, or Nexus switches in the plant core, this is a drop-everything patching event. Root access and RCE on these devices effectively hands attackers the keys to network segmentation. CISA also published new Cyber Decoy Guidance for critical infrastructure operators, positioning deception technology as a complement to Zero Trust. For plant floor engineers, this is worth reading: well-placed decoy PLCs, fake HMIs, and honeytokens in engineering workstations can detect lateral movement long before it reaches real controllers, and unlike IDS signatures they generate near-zero false positives because no legitimate process should ever touch them. On the AI front, two stories deserve attention. Irregular researchers demonstrated that agentic AI systems can retrain their own underlying models mid-task, potentially leaking secrets and erasing safety refusals. Separately, Spanish regulators received what is reportedly the first documented agentic-AI data breach, where an AI agent autonomously chained a login, vulnerability discovery, and data exfiltration. These are not abstract concerns for OT: many vendors are pushing AI copilots into engineering tools and historians, and the trust boundary around what an autonomous agent can touch inside a control network needs to be nailed down now, not later. Rounding out the week, Apple patched 261 vulnerabilities across its OS lineup, MikroTik RouterOS picked up two KEV entries, and SANS ISC flagged unusual scanning against hospitality applications. Nothing plant-specific, but engineering laptops running macOS and edge routers running RouterOS in remote sites are both in scope.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added 20 vulnerabilities to the KEV catalog in the last 30 days, with an unusually heavy concentration on network infrastructure that sits between IT and OT. The Cisco entries are the most urgent: CVE-2026-76460 (ISE authentication bypass, actively exploited), CVE-2026-76461 (Secure Email Gateway SQL injection to root), and CVE-2026-20079 (FMC authentication bypass) collectively threaten the enforcement points many sites rely on to isolate control networks. If your industrial DMZ authenticates through ISE or your perimeter firewalls are managed via FMC, patch on an emergency change window and audit for indicators of compromise before assuming you were not touched. Two MikroTik RouterOS flaws (CVE-2026-86060 privilege escalation and CVE-2026-67277 kernel memory disclosure) matter for anyone using MikroTik at remote pump stations, substations, or wellheads — a common choice for low-cost cellular backhaul. Citrix NetScaler (CVE-2026-19490) and a Fortinet FortiOS heap overflow (CVE-2025-25249) round out the perimeter picture. On the endpoint side, two Windows local privilege escalations (CVE-2026-81963 and CVE-2026-85880) are relevant to engineering workstations and jump hosts. The ConnectWise ScreenConnect flaw (CVE-2026-84869) deserves specific mention for OT: many integrators and OEMs use ScreenConnect for remote support into customer sites. An attacker who compromises an integrator's instance can push and execute files into active sessions without host confirmation — exactly the supply-chain remote-access vector that has driven several recent ICS incidents.Read more →
