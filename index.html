Today's Knowledge Bite

The STUN protocol abused by the new ClingSTUN backdoor was originally designed in 2003 to help VoIP phones discover their public IP behind NAT — it was never intended as a secure channel and includes no built-in authentication or encryption. That same NAT-traversal capability is exactly what makes it attractive for malware wanting to punch out through plant firewalls that permit UDP 3478 for legitimate softphones. Many OT firewall rulesets still allow STUN wholesale because voice teams asked for it a decade ago and nobody re-reviewed the rule.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was formalised not in a lab but on warships: Nicolas Minorsky worked out the mathematics of three-term control in 1922 while analysing automatic steering for the USS New Mexico, observing how an experienced helmsman anticipated the ship's inertia rather than just reacting to current heading error. His insight that the helmsman was effectively adding a derivative term predates most industrial applications by decades. Every time you tune a derivative gain to tame overshoot on a slow thermal loop, you're applying the same mental model a 1920s quartermaster used to keep a battleship on course.

Tech Spotlight

Deterministic Ethernet via OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) is quietly moving from pilot to production in 2026, with Siemens, Rockwell, Beckhoff, and B&R all shipping controllers that can act as TSN talkers and listeners using the OPC UA FX (Field eXchange) companion specification. The promise is real: a single Ethernet fabric carrying both bounded-latency controller-to-controller traffic (replacing Profinet IRT or EtherNet/IP CIP Motion) and best-effort engineering and historian traffic, all with vendor-independent semantics. For controls engineers, the practical implication is that your next greenfield line could use standard managed switches with TSN features (IEEE 802.1Qbv scheduled traffic, 802.1AS time sync) instead of proprietary motion backbones. That means lower switch costs, easier integration with IT monitoring, and the ability to span cells without protocol gateways. The catch is that TSN requires end-to-end configuration — every switch and endpoint in the path must support and be configured for the same schedule, and misconfiguration manifests as subtle jitter rather than obvious failure. Adoption challenges remain significant: tooling for TSN stream reservation is still vendor-specific despite the standards, troubleshooting requires time-aware packet capture (regular Wireshark doesn't tell you if a frame missed its gate), and most installed-base switches aren't TSN-capable. Expect TSN to arrive first in semiconductor, automotive body-in-white, and packaging lines where motion determinism justifies the learning curve.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) and 62443-4-2 (component security requirements) continue to be the practical anchor points for OT security programs, and we're seeing more procurement specifications explicitly require 62443-4-2 Security Level 2 certification for new PLCs, drives, and HMIs. If you're specifying equipment for a 2027 capital project, ask vendors not just whether they claim compliance but which specific SL and which certification body (TÜV, exida, etc.) issued the certificate — self-attestation is common and not equivalent. On the U.S. side, NIST SP 800-82 Rev 3 (Guide to OT Security) remains the de facto reference for aligning OT practices with the broader Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, and CISA's Cross-Sector Cybersecurity Performance Goals (CPGs) now map explicitly to 62443 controls, giving engineers a defensible way to translate between regulator-speak and engineering reality. The pending healthcare cybersecurity legislation in the U.S. Senate is worth watching as a template — similar sector-specific mandates for water and critical manufacturing have been discussed and would likely lean heavily on these same reference frameworks. For those working internationally, note that the EU NIS2 directive's national transpositions are now in enforcement mode, and the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) obligations for manufacturers of "products with digital elements" — which explicitly includes industrial controllers — start biting from late 2027. Vendors will be pushing SBOMs and vulnerability-handling processes to their customers; expect to receive (and need to act on) a lot more coordinated vulnerability disclosures over the next 18 months.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful incident to revisit this week is the 2021 Oldsmar, Florida water treatment intrusion, where an attacker used a shared TeamViewer session on an HMI to briefly raise the sodium hydroxide setpoint from 100 ppm to 11,100 ppm. An alert operator noticed the mouse cursor moving on its own and reverted the change before any physical consequence occurred; downstream pH alarms and residence time would likely have caught it anyway, but the near-miss drove home how thin the margin can be. The root causes were depressingly mundane: a flat network with no segmentation between the business LAN and the SCADA HMI, shared credentials for TeamViewer across multiple operators, Windows 7 end-of-life machines directly accessible, and no multifactor authentication on remote access. There was no exotic ICS malware involved — just a legitimate remote-access tool used the way the attacker found it configured. Three lessons remain directly actionable in 2026: first, inventory and consolidate remote-access tooling (the recent SANS ISC note on ScreenConnect abuse echoes exactly this pattern), funneling all vendor and off-hours access through a hardened jump host with MFA and session recording. Second, treat setpoint-change authorization as a safety control, not just a security one — rate limits, dual-approval for safety-critical parameters, and independent instrumented alarms will catch both malicious and fat-finger changes. Third, assume operators will be your last line of defense and train them to recognise and report anomalous HMI behaviour promptly.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The notable item for OT-adjacent IT today is ClingSTUN, a Linux backdoor abusing the STUN protocol for command-and-control while bundling exploits for self-propagation across dozens of known Linux vulnerabilities. Because many industrial gateways, historian servers, thin-client jump hosts, and edge compute nodes now run Linux variants, controls engineers should treat this as a reminder to verify that patch cadence on Linux-based OT infrastructure keeps pace with the enterprise. STUN traffic (UDP 3478) is often permitted outbound for VoIP/WebRTC and is a plausible covert channel through firewalls that aren't doing deep application inspection. SecurityWeek also reports active exploitation of an AI-discovered Rejetto HFS flaw (CVE-2026-61500) enabling admin takeover and RCE. HFS is a lightweight HTTP file server occasionally used by integrators to shuttle firmware, PLC programs, or recipe files between offices and plants — if you find it on your network, retire it in favour of an authenticated managed share. The arrest of the alleged ShinyHunters leader in Jordan is operationally interesting; expect short-term disruption to that extortion ecosystem but no lasting dent in overall ransomware pressure on manufacturers. On the policy side, the U.S. Senate passed a bipartisan bill to strengthen healthcare cybersecurity, with breach costs averaging $10M. While healthcare-specific, the pattern of sector-specific legislation is likely to spill into critical manufacturing and water utilities over the next year, so plant engineers should watch for new reporting and baseline-control obligations. SANS ISC continues to highlight ScreenConnect abuse by threat actors — a reminder to audit remote-support tools installed by vendors and integrators on engineering workstations. Finally, Google temporarily narrowing its OSS bug bounty under a flood of AI-generated junk reports is a sign of the times: automated vulnerability discovery is accelerating on both sides, and defenders need better triage tooling rather than more raw findings.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The last two weeks of KEV additions are dominated by perimeter and remote-access infrastructure that commonly sits in front of OT DMZs: Citrix NetScaler (three CVEs including unauthenticated RCE in CVE-2026-88771/88772 and a DoS in CVE-2026-88779), Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager (CVE-2026-76504, unauthenticated admin access via URI hex-encoding mishandling), F5 BIG-IP APM (CVE-2026-94127, unauthenticated heap overflow RCE when OAuth profiles are in use), and Check Point gateways and management servers (CVE-2026-85102 and CVE-2026-93616). If any of these front-end your industrial zone, Purdue Level 3.5 DMZ, or vendor remote-access path, patching is urgent — these are precisely the devices adversaries pivot through to reach engineering workstations and jump hosts. Two additions are directly relevant to field infrastructure: MikroTik RouterOS (CVE-2026-67279) is widely deployed as cheap industrial routing in remote sites and SCADA backhauls, and Zyxel GS1900 switches (CVE-2026-7273) show up on plant floors as low-cost unmanaged-tier equipment. Both allow unauthenticated command execution; inventory your edge gear and prioritise firmware updates or network isolation. Two Linux kernel entries (CVE-2025-39964 and CVE-2026-53266) matter for Linux-based HMIs, edge gateways, and virtualization hosts running historians. Practical remediation: treat the Citrix, F5, Cisco, and Check Point CVEs as emergency-change candidates this week; snapshot configs, apply vendor hotfixes, and rotate any credentials or session tokens that may have been exposed. For MikroTik and Zyxel, if patching requires an outage window, apply ACLs restricting management-plane access to a dedicated jump host in the meantime.Read more →
