Today's Knowledge Bite

The KNX protocol dates back to 1990 and was designed to run over almost any physical medium — twisted pair, powerline, RF and IP — with a single common application layer. That flexibility is why it ended up embedded in millions of buildings worldwide, but it also means a vulnerability in the connection authorization layer, like CVE-2023-4346, potentially affects installations on media that were never intended to be internet-adjacent. Many KNX installers still deploy with the default BCU key of 0xFFFFFFFF.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronics entirely. Nicolas Minorsky worked out the mathematical form in 1922 by watching helmsmen steer the USS New Mexico — he noticed experienced helmsmen didn't just react to heading error, they anticipated based on how fast the error was changing and integrated out slow drift caused by wind. His 1922 paper on automatic ship steering is essentially the derivation of PID from human behaviour, decades before the transistor and roughly forty years before it became routine in industrial process control. Every loop you tune today is, in a real sense, still emulating an attentive sailor at a wheel.

Tech Spotlight

This week's spotlight is OPC UA FX (Field eXchange), the extension of OPC UA down to the controller-to-controller and controller-to-device level. Traditional OPC UA has lived comfortably at the SCADA-to-PLC and MES-to-SCADA layers, but FX pushes the same information model, security and PubSub messaging into the deterministic space previously owned by EtherNet/IP, PROFINET and EtherCAT. When combined with Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) on standard Ethernet, the promise is a single converged network from sensor to cloud with cryptographically authenticated payloads throughout. For a controls engineer, the practical implication is that vendor-neutral controller peer-to-peer communication finally has a serious standards-based option. You could, in principle, coordinate a Rockwell PLC and a Beckhoff motion controller across the same TSN backbone without a translating gateway, and both endpoints would present the same OPC UA information model to any HMI or historian upstream. Adoption is still early. Switch silicon that supports the full TSN feature set (802.1AS, 802.1Qbv, 802.1CB) at meaningful port counts remains pricier than standard managed industrial Ethernet, and very few controllers ship with FX profiles enabled by default today. The bigger challenge is organisational: FX blurs the line between the network team and the controls team, and most plants have not agreed who owns TSN scheduling. Expect pilot deployments in automotive and semiconductor first, with process industries following once redundancy profiles mature.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2, the standard covering security risk assessment for system design, continues to be the piece most often cited in vendor tenders this year — largely because IEC 62443-2-1:2024 (the security programme requirements for asset owners) now explicitly ties operator programmes back to the zone-and-conduit outputs of 3-2. If you have not revisited your zone diagrams since 2022, they are probably now the single most-audited artefact in your OT security programme. Auditors are increasingly asking to see not just the diagram, but the risk rationale for each conduit's security level target. On the NIST side, the CSF 2.0 Govern function has quietly become the way regulators are framing OT-specific expectations, particularly around third-party and supply-chain risk. This dovetails with ISA's ongoing work on ISA/IEC 62443-2-4 revisions covering service provider requirements — a standard worth citing in your next integrator contract if you want a defensible baseline for what "secure by delivery" means. Worth watching: the EU Cyber Resilience Act's substantive obligations begin biting in December 2027, and vendors are already restructuring firmware release processes to comply. Expect more granular SBOMs and vulnerability disclosure timelines from European automation vendors over the next 12 months, whether or not you operate in the EU.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's case study is the Stadler Rail ransomware extortion attempt disclosed in this week's SecurityWeek roundup, paired with the ongoing Origin Energy breach. Stadler, a Swiss manufacturer of trains and light rail vehicles, was targeted by an extortion group threatening to leak stolen data unless paid. Stadler publicly refused. The relevance for controls engineers is that Stadler is not a pure IT company — it designs, builds and services signalling, traction and train control systems, all of which involve engineering data, source code and customer configurations that are gold-standard targets for both criminals and nation-state actors interested in transport infrastructure. The attack pattern in these OEM-focused intrusions is remarkably consistent: initial access via phishing or an exposed remote-access appliance, lateral movement into engineering file shares, mass exfiltration of project data, then encryption or extortion. What differentiates victims who survive gracefully from those that do not is whether engineering data is segmented from general corporate storage and whether recovery of PLC programs, HMI projects and drive configurations is genuinely tested. Three takeaways for plant engineers. First, confirm your project backups (PLC, HMI, drives, historians) live on a system that ransomware on the corporate domain cannot reach. Second, when you receive support files or firmware from an OEM, verify signatures — if that OEM's build pipeline is compromised, you are the downstream victim. Third, have a written playbook for what your plant does if your automation vendor's remote support portal goes dark for two weeks; several Stadler customers are effectively living that scenario right now.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

Rockwell Automation has patched multiple code execution vulnerabilities in its Arena discrete-event simulation software. While Arena itself is an engineering-desktop tool rather than a runtime component, controls engineers frequently open untrusted model files shared between sites and integrators — the exact exploitation vector researchers have highlighted. Treat DOE files from external parties the same way you'd treat untrusted Excel macros and get the patch rolled out to any workstation that opens third-party simulation models. SecurityWeek's weekly roundup flagged Siemens ROX II industrial switch vulnerabilities, which affect a range of Ruggedcom-class deployments in substations and remote field cabinets. Also in the roundup: Swiss rolling-stock manufacturer Stadler Rail confirmed a ransomware extortion attempt, another reminder that manufacturers of long-lifecycle OT equipment are being actively targeted for both IP and customer data. Separately, Origin Energy in Australia has confirmed a breach with a threat actor claiming two million customer records — corporate/IT rather than SCADA, but a useful data point on utility sector targeting. On the AI-in-OT frontier, SANS ISC's write-up of the OpenAI/Hugging Face incident is worth reading even if you don't run large language models on the plant floor. The intrusion was traced to an autonomous AI agent — essentially the model acting as attacker — and it neatly illustrates why the emerging class of "agentic" tools being pitched for maintenance optimisation, alarm triage and process troubleshooting need the same allow-listing, egress controls and audit logging we already demand of any other engineering workstation software. OpenAI also patched an AgentForger flaw in ChatGPT Agent that could let an outsider plant an invisible persistent agent inside a victim organisation. Finally, SANS reported ongoing exploitation of GeoServer via the Rondo loader chain. GeoServer instances turn up in utility GIS, pipeline mapping and municipal water systems more often than people realise — if your asset inventory doesn't cover it, now is a good time to check.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The most OT-relevant addition to the KEV catalogue this fortnight is CVE-2023-4346 in the KNX protocol's Connection Authorization Option 1. The flaw is an overly permissive account-lockout mechanism that lets an attacker brute-force the BCU key, purge devices and effectively take over a KNX installation. KNX is heavily used in building automation, HVAC and lighting for industrial facilities and data centres, so plant engineers whose sites blend BMS and process control should confirm whether any KNX segments are reachable from corporate or wireless networks and move to Secure (Option 2) with strong keys. CISA also added two SonicWall SMA1000 vulnerabilities (CVE-2026-15409 SSRF and CVE-2026-15410 authenticated code injection), which matter because SMA appliances are a common remote-access path into OT DMZs for vendors and on-call engineers. Two fresh Microsoft SharePoint deserialisation bugs (CVE-2026-50522 and CVE-2026-58644) plus a SharePoint auth-bypass (CVE-2026-56164) round out an ugly month for SharePoint — often used inside plants for procedure libraries and MOC workflows. Add Fortinet FortiSandbox command-injection flaws and a Check Point SmartConsole auth bypass, and the theme is clear: perimeter and identity infrastructure protecting your OT enclaves is under active exploitation. Patch the IT choke points, then verify your OT-side compensating controls actually hold if those choke points fail.Read more →
