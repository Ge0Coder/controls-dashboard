Today's Knowledge Bite

The original Modbus protocol, released by Modicon in 1979, was designed to run over RS-232 with an assumed maximum of 247 slave devices — a limit that came directly from the 8-bit address field minus reserved values. Nearly fifty years later, Modbus TCP still carries that same 247-device ceiling in its unit-identifier field, even though Ethernet has no such physical constraint. It's a perfect example of how a hardware-era design decision can outlive its original justification by decades.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronics by decades — the first mathematical formulation is generally credited to Nicolas Minorsky in 1922, who derived it while watching helmsmen steer the USS New Mexico. He noticed that experienced helmsmen didn't just react to the current heading error, but also to how fast the error was changing and to the accumulated drift — proportional, derivative, and integral action, discovered empirically at the ship's wheel. Every tuning session you do today is essentially reverse-engineering what a good sailor did instinctively a century ago.

Tech Spotlight

Today's spotlight is on Ethernet-APL (Advanced Physical Layer), which is quietly reshaping process instrumentation. APL brings two-wire, power-and-data, intrinsically safe Ethernet to field devices in hazardous areas at 10 Mbit/s over up to 1,000 metres — replacing 4-20 mA and HART with native TCP/IP right down to the transmitter. That means an OPC-UA or PROFINET session can terminate at the sensor itself, no gateway required. For controls engineers, the practical implication is significant: rich diagnostic data (multivariable readings, valve signatures, corrosion coupons) becomes accessible without protocol translation, and commissioning tools like FDI packages work end-to-end. It also collapses the traditional split between fieldbus specialists and network engineers — your instrument tech now needs to understand VLANs and PTP. Adoption challenges are real. APL switches remain expensive versus traditional I/O, the installed base of HART is enormous, and cybersecurity implications are non-trivial — every transmitter is now an IP endpoint with its own attack surface. Most greenfield projects in refining and pharma are piloting APL on non-critical loops first, and brownfield migration is likely to take a decade or more.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) continues to gain traction as the de-facto method regulators point to when asking 'how did you decide your zones and conduits?' Increasingly, EPCs are being contractually required to deliver a 62443-3-2 risk assessment as part of FEED deliverables, not just as an operational activity post-handover. If you're on a capital project, push to get the zone and conduit diagram baked into the P&ID review cycle rather than tacked on afterwards — retrofitting network segmentation into a commissioned plant is painful and expensive. Worth watching: ISA/IEC 62443-4-2 certification for components (PLCs, RTUs, gateways) is now being specified by name in several major operator standards, including recent updates from Shell and Aramco. Vendors like Siemens, Rockwell, and Schneider have been racing to get flagship product lines certified at Security Level 2. When evaluating new controllers, ask for the certificate and the Security Target document — the ST tells you which security functions were actually tested versus merely claimed. On the NIST side, the CSF 2.0 'Govern' function is starting to influence how OT security programs are structured, particularly around vendor risk management — very relevant given today's news about N-able and integrator remote access.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's case study is the 2023 Aliquippa, Pennsylvania water authority incident, where the Cyber Av3ngers group (linked to Iran's IRGC) compromised a Unitronics Vision-series PLC controlling a booster station. The attackers exploited the fact that the PLC was directly reachable from the internet on TCP/20256 with the default password '1111'. They defaced the HMI with an anti-Israel message and forced operators into manual control — no water quality impact, but a very public humiliation. How it happened: the integrator had exposed the PLC for remote programming convenience, the default password was never changed, and the utility had no asset inventory that flagged the exposure. CISA subsequently issued an advisory noting the same attackers had hit multiple water utilities using identical tradecraft. Lessons for controls engineers: first, Shodan and Censys will find your PLC before you do — do the search yourself, monthly. Second, default credentials on safety- or process-critical devices are not an IT failure, they're an engineering failure, and they should fail your commissioning checklist. Third, 'we're too small to be a target' is not a threat model; ideologically motivated actors will hit any exposed device regardless of the operator's size or strategic importance.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The dominant story this morning is the MikroTik 'MikroTrick' vulnerability chain. MikroTik has patched already-exploited flaws allowing SSH authentication bypass and configuration overwrite, and SANS ISC is telling defenders to assume compromise on any unpatched device — attackers have been observed creating new accounts to persist across patching. MikroTik hardware is common at remote sites, telemetry backhauls, and cellular gateways in water, oil & gas, and renewables, so this is squarely a controls-engineering concern rather than just an IT problem. N-able has patched a critical zero-day in N-central, the RMM platform frequently used by integrators and OEM support teams to reach plant assets. Administrators are told to hunt for unrecognised user accounts. If your automation vendor or integrator uses N-central for remote support into your DMZ or jump hosts, this warrants a direct conversation today about their patch status and account audit. On the espionage front, a new North Korean Linux toolkit embedding a backdoor inside HAProxy is targeting South Korean automotive and media firms for long-term surveillance. The tradecraft — living inside a load balancer that's usually treated as trusted infrastructure — is a reminder that Linux appliances in the industrial DMZ (historians, MES front-ends, reverse proxies) deserve the same integrity monitoring as Windows servers. Rounding out the day: 'Nightmare Eclipse' drops chained PoCs abusing CrowdStrike, Nvidia, and Avast drivers for SYSTEM-level privilege escalation — relevant if you run any of these on engineering workstations. CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added a substantial batch of KEVs over the past two weeks. Of most direct OT concern are the SonicWall SMA1000 flaws (CVE-2026-83548 SSRF and CVE-2026-83549 authenticated command injection) — SMA1000s are commonly deployed as secure remote access gateways into industrial networks, and chaining these gives an unauthenticated attacker a path to RCE. Also notable is the Citrix NetScaler ADC/Gateway memory buffer flaw (CVE-2026-8452), affecting another remote-access chokepoint many enterprises use in front of OT jump servers. Two PaperCut NG/MF vulnerabilities (CVE-2026-81578 and CVE-2026-82078) are chainable to unauthenticated RCE — PaperCut sits on many corporate print servers that bridge into plant office networks. JFrog Artifactory has two entries (auth bypass CVE-2026-82329 and a path traversal CVE-2026-66384) that matter for anyone consuming container images or firmware artefacts through Artifactory in their build pipeline. Round it out with the Chromium V8 type confusion (CVE-2026-85046) — patch every browser on every engineering workstation and HMI that has one embedded. Practical action: prioritise external-facing remote access (SonicWall, Citrix), then RMM/support tooling (N-able, MikroTik edge), then internal supply-chain infrastructure (Artifactory, PaperCut).
