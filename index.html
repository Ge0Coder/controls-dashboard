Today's Knowledge Bite

The Modbus protocol, still ubiquitous on plant floors after 47 years, was designed in 1979 by Modicon for a world where serial cabling was point-to-point and physically secured by the building itself. That's why it has no authentication — the network 'was' the perimeter. Every modern Modbus/TCP deployment is essentially trusting that original 1979 assumption still holds, which is why segmentation, not protocol upgrades, is usually the only realistic defense.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller's derivative term has a quietly fascinating origin: it was first implemented mechanically by Elmer Sperry around 1911 for ship steering, where helmsmen had intuitively been applying 'anticipation' to counter a ship's inertia long before mathematicians formalized it. Nicolas Minorsky later analyzed helmsman behavior on the USS New Mexico in 1922 and explicitly modeled what they were doing — proportional response to error, integral correction for steady bias, and derivative anticipation of where the bow was heading. In other words, PID wasn't invented; it was reverse-engineered from skilled human operators, which is why it still feels intuitive when you tune it by hand.

Tech Spotlight

This week's spotlight is on MQTT Sparkplug B, which is quietly becoming the de facto data layer for Unified Namespace (UNS) architectures. Sparkplug adds a strict topic namespace, stateful session awareness (birth/death certificates), and a defined payload schema on top of plain MQTT — solving the three problems that made raw MQTT awkward in OT: ambiguous topic structures, stale data, and undocumented payloads. The result is a publish/subscribe data fabric where every device's state is discoverable and current, which fits modern event-driven SCADA, MES integration, and cloud historian ingestion far better than poll-based OPC architectures. The practical implication is that you can decouple your edge devices (PLCs via gateways like Ignition Edge, HighByte, or native Sparkplug-capable controllers) from your consumers (SCADA, analytics, ERP). Add a broker like HiveMQ or EMQX in the middle and any new consumer can subscribe without touching the PLC. The adoption challenge is cultural more than technical: traditional controls teams are wary of brokers as a single point of failure, and IT teams want to own the broker — which raises governance questions about who is responsible when a broker outage stops production. Brokers also need real HA clustering and TLS with client certs to be defensible, and most Sparkplug deployments still rely on shared usernames and unencrypted links inside the plant. Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) continues to be the standard most often misapplied during greenfield projects. The 2020 edition formalized the zone-and-conduit methodology, but many integrators still treat the SL-T (target security level) assignment as a paperwork exercise rather than a design constraint. A correctly executed 3-2 assessment dictates which conduits need authentication, encryption, or unidirectional gateways — and crucially, where they don't, which saves money. If you're scoping a 2026 capital project, insist that the SL-T per zone is fixed before P&IDs are frozen, because retrofitting an SL-3 conduit later typically requires hardware changes (managed switches, firewalls, certificate infrastructure). On the NIST side, the CSF 2.0 'Govern' function is increasingly being cited in insurance underwriting questionnaires for industrial sites. Cyber insurers now ask whether your OT environment has documented governance ownership distinct from IT — a question many plants cannot answer cleanly. Expect this to flow into premium calculations through 2026. ISA's TR84.00.09 (cybersecurity related to the safety lifecycle) is also worth revisiting if you operate SIS. The technical report explicitly addresses the overlap between IEC 61511 and IEC 62443, which auditors are starting to probe in earnest following several recent incidents where SIS networks were found bridged to BPCS networks without compensating controls.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's case study is the 2024 Arnold Clark / Halliburton-pattern incident as it continues to inform OT response playbooks: a major North American oilfield services firm experienced an IT-originated ransomware event that forced manual operation of field services for nearly two weeks. While the OT systems themselves were not directly encrypted, the loss of IT-side scheduling, billing, telemetry aggregation, and remote support effectively halted normal operations. Investigators later attributed initial access to a compromised VPN credential combined with insufficient MFA enforcement on remote engineering access. The mechanism was unremarkable — stolen credentials, lateral movement through flat segments of the corporate network, and eventual deployment of ransomware to file servers and virtualization hosts. What made it OT-relevant was the dependency mapping: the OT environment 'survived' technically but could not operate commercially because every supporting business process lived on the IT side. Three lessons for controls engineers: first, document which IT services your OT actually depends on (AD, DNS, NTP, license servers, historians replicating upward) and test operating without them; second, ensure your remote engineering access path has MFA, session recording, and a kill-switch independent of IT — VPN-only access is no longer defensible; third, your business continuity plan should distinguish between 'OT can run' and 'the business can sell what OT produces.' The two are not the same, and most plants only discover the gap mid-incident.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

CISA released a cluster of six ICS advisories today, five of which directly affect ABB and Hitachi Energy gear common in plant environments. ABB B&R Automation Runtime, Automation Studio, and B&R industrial PCs all received fixes spanning browser-session takeover, outdated third-party components, and a network-exploitable cluster enabling RCE, DoS, and DNS cache poisoning. The B&R PC advisory is the most operationally significant because the affected devices often sit on engineering or operator workstations within the control LAN, where a single compromised node can pivot to controllers. Engineers running B&R toolchains should pull the new release notes today and schedule patching during the next maintenance window. ABB's Terra AC Wallbox advisory matters for sites running EV charging infrastructure on the same network as building or facility systems — heap pollution leading to firmware modification is a worst-case outcome for any embedded device. Hitachi Energy's GMS600 substation product is flagged for the lingering OpenSSL CVE-2022-4304 (the 'timing oracle' Bleichenbacher variant); exploitation is impractical in most field LANs but matters where TLS traffic crosses untrusted boundaries. Kieback & Peter DDC building controllers (DDC4002/4100/4200/4400 series) have a browser-takeover bug — relevant for facilities and BMS integrators. On the IT side that bleeds into OT: Trend Micro patched an Apex One on-prem zero-day (CVE-2026-34926) being actively exploited, and Cisco issued a critical fix for Secure Workload REST API auth bypass granting Site Admin. The Grafana breach via the TanStack supply-chain compromise is a reminder that unrotated tokens after a third-party incident remain the #1 cause of downstream takeover — relevant for anyone using Grafana dashboards on top of historian data. The FBI's takedown of 'First VPN', a service used by ransomware crews for recon and intrusion staging, is good news but doesn't change the threat model; the operators will reconstitute under new infrastructure within weeks.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added several entries to the KEV catalog this week with direct OT relevance. CVE-2026-34926 (Trend Micro Apex One on-prem) is being exploited in the wild — many industrial sites run Apex One on engineering workstations and jump hosts, so verify your Apex One server is patched and audit agent integrity. CVE-2026-20182 (Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager auth bypass) is critical for any plant using SD-WAN to backhaul site traffic; an unauthenticated admin takeover of the Manager is a full-fabric compromise. CVE-2026-0300 (PAN-OS Captive Portal unauthenticated RCE as root) likewise demands immediate attention if you expose Palo Alto User-ID Authentication Portal anywhere near plant boundaries. Notably, CISA also re-added a batch of very old Microsoft and Adobe bugs (CVE-2008-4250 MS08-067, 2009-1537, 2010-0249/0806) to the KEV — these are still being seen in active campaigns, which is a stark indicator of how much unpatched legacy Windows still exists on plant floors. If you have Windows XP/7/Server 2003/2008 islands running HMIs or historians, assume hostile actors know exactly where to look.
