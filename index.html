Today's Knowledge Bite

The humble 4-20 mA current loop, still ubiquitous in process plants, was standardised in the 1950s partly because a broken wire produces 0 mA — a value outside the valid signal range — making loop failure instantly and unambiguously detectable. Voltage-based signalling cannot distinguish a dead sensor from a legitimate zero reading. This single design choice, pre-dating modern diagnostics by decades, is why 4-20 mA has outlived dozens of 'successor' standards.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller's integral term was effectively invented aboard ships, not in factories. Nicholas Minorsky formalised three-term control in 1922 while analysing automatic steering for the USS New Mexico, after observing how experienced helmsmen anticipated the ship's response — applying rudder not just to current heading error, but to its rate of change and its accumulated history. His paper predated the Taylor Instrument and Foxboro commercial PID controllers of the 1930s by more than a decade, and the mathematics he wrote down for a 32,000-tonne battleship is identical to what runs in every PLC function block today.

Tech Spotlight

OPC-UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues its slow march from trade-show demo to production reality. The idea is simple: combine OPC-UA's rich, secure, vendor-neutral information model with Ethernet TSN's deterministic, bounded-latency delivery, so you can run both motion control and MES-level data over the same cable. The IEEE 802.1 TSN sub-standards (Qbv for time-aware shaping, Qbu for frame preemption, AS for time sync) have been stable for years, and all major PLC vendors — Siemens, Rockwell, Beckhoff, B&R, Schneider — now ship controllers with TSN-capable ports. The practical catch is that TSN requires every switch in the path to participate; one legacy unmanaged switch breaks determinism. That means greenfield or major-retrofit projects only, and typically a full switch refresh. Configuration tooling is also fragmented — each vendor's engineering suite handles TSN stream reservation differently, and interoperability across brands still requires careful testing despite the IEC/IEEE 60802 industrial profile. For most brownfield plants, the pragmatic path remains PROFINET IRT or EtherCAT for motion and standard OPC-UA pub/sub for everything else, with TSN earmarked for the next capital project.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443 continues to be the reference OT security framework, and the recent industry push is around 62443-4-2 (component requirements) certification for PLCs, drives, and HMIs. Several major vendors now ship controllers with SL2 or SL3 certification out of the box, which materially simplifies the asset-owner's compliance story under 62443-2-1. If you are specifying new hardware, insist on the certification letter and the security capability statement — not just a marketing claim of being 'IEC 62443 compliant,' which is meaningless without specifying which part and which security level. On the US side, NIST SP 800-82 Revision 3 remains the authoritative guide for ICS security and now aligns closely with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, which added the 'Govern' function. For plants with corporate parents subject to SEC cyber disclosure rules, the Govern function is where OT risk needs to be visible to the board — a shift that is pushing controls engineers into conversations they never used to have. ISA/IEC are also progressing work on 62443-3-2 risk assessment methodology refinements and on a dedicated profile for safety instrumented systems, bridging the long-standing gap between IEC 61511 functional safety and 62443 cybersecurity.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful one to revisit this week is the 2023 Aliquippa, Pennsylvania water authority incident, where the CyberAv3ngers group (linked to Iran's IRGC) compromised a Unitronics Vision series PLC controlling a booster station. The attack succeeded not through a sophisticated exploit but because the PLC was exposed directly to the internet on its default port 20256 with the factory-default password '1111.' Operators arrived to find the HMI defaced with an anti-Israel message; fortunately the attackers chose vandalism over process manipulation, and manual operation prevented any service disruption. The mechanics are embarrassingly simple: Shodan-style scanning for Unitronics PCOM protocol on 20256, default credential login, and HMI screen overwrite. No zero-day, no supply chain compromise, no insider. Lessons for controls engineers remain painfully relevant three years on: first, no PLC should ever have a routable public IP — if a vendor or integrator insists it is necessary, they are wrong, and a properly configured VPN or jump host costs almost nothing. Second, change every default credential on commissioning, document it, and audit it annually; the Unitronics default was published in the manual. Third, assume reconnaissance is continuous — your small municipal or remote-site PLC is just as interesting to a nation-state as a refinery DCS, because it offers a cheap political statement.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The most consequential story for OT operators this week is the US government's disruption of Chinese state-sponsored scanning and exploitation infrastructure, including MicroScan and FishHub, used by Flax Typhoon and related APTs to enumerate and compromise critical infrastructure networks worldwide. These tools have been used for reconnaissance against exposed OT-adjacent systems — VPN concentrators, firewalls, and remote access appliances — so expect retaliatory activity and a short-lived lull in scanning noise before new infrastructure comes online. Review your perimeter logs for the past 90 days for any artefacts tied to the published indicators. Anthropic announced it is tapping 11 firms specifically for OT security collaboration while also launching an OSS vulnerability scanner that forwards model-generated bug reports directly to open source maintainers. The OT angle is notable: large AI vendors are now openly recruiting ICS specialists, suggesting LLM-assisted vulnerability discovery against protocol stacks like Modbus, OPC-UA, and vendor runtimes is imminent. Expect a wave of low-quality, AI-generated CVE claims against industrial software — maintainers of libraries like libmodbus, open62541, and various HMI toolkits will likely be flooded. On the commodity threat side, SANS ISC continues documenting the abuse of legitimate RMM tools (ScreenConnect and now others) as initial-access and persistence mechanisms. This matters directly to plant engineers because contractor laptops, vendor support workstations, and jump hosts frequently run these tools with permissive firewall exceptions into Level 3 and sometimes Level 2. If you have not inventoried RMM agents on your OT network recently, do it this week. Also worth noting: a pre-installed firmware malware campaign dubbed Midnight Mimosa is hitting budget Android devices across 150+ countries. CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added a notable cluster of edge-device vulnerabilities to KEV over the past two weeks that are highly relevant to OT perimeters. Three Citrix NetScaler ADC/Gateway flaws (CVE-2026-88771, 88772, and 88779) cover unauthenticated RCE, memory-corruption RCE, and DoS respectively — NetScaler is a common remote-access gateway for engineering vendors and integrators, so patching is urgent. Add to that Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager (CVE-2026-76504) allowing unauthenticated admin access via URI encoding tricks, Fortinet FortiMail path traversal (CVE-2026-104286), MikroTik RouterOS unauthenticated session abuse (CVE-2026-67279) which is commonly found at small remote sites and substations, and Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator (CVE-2026-93952). F5 BIG-IP APM also received an unauthenticated heap overflow (CVE-2026-94127). For OT specifically, the MikroTik and Citrix entries are the ones to action first — MikroTik because it is pervasive in cellular-backhauled remote sites and often unmanaged by central IT, and Citrix because it typically sits in front of jump servers used by control-system vendors. The older additions (BIND 2015, Struts 2016, ProFTPD 2015) suggest CISA is seeing active exploitation against long-neglected Linux-based appliances and historian servers that still run these components bundled inside vendor appliances. Check vendor bills-of-materials and historian OS images; these libraries hide in many places.Read more →
