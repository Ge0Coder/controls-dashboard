Today's Knowledge Bite

The original PID controller predates electronic computers by decades — Elmer Sperry's gyroscopic ship autopilot used proportional-integral-derivative logic in 1911, implemented entirely in mechanical linkages and hydraulic feedback. Nicholas Minorsky formalised the mathematics in 1922 while analysing the USS New Mexico's steering, and his three-term equation is essentially unchanged in today's DCS function blocks. When you tune a loop, you're using math developed to keep a battleship pointing straight.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The Watt centrifugal governor — the spinning-ball speed regulator on early steam engines — was the first widely deployed feedback control system, but it took nearly a century for anyone to understand why it sometimes went unstable. James Clerk Maxwell finally cracked it in 1868 with his paper 'On Governors,' which is arguably the founding document of control theory. His analysis of why governors hunt (oscillate) introduced the mathematical stability criteria that Routh and Hurwitz later formalised — meaning every Bode plot you've ever drawn traces its lineage to Maxwell trying to explain why steam engines wouldn't hold speed.

Tech Spotlight

Agentic AI in engineering workflows is moving from demo to deployment faster than most controls teams have policy for. Unlike a chatbot that answers questions, an agent is given a goal (e.g. 'diagnose why this loop is oscillating' or 'generate a Modbus poll list from this P&ID') and permitted to take multi-step actions — reading files, querying historians, calling APIs, sometimes writing code. Vendors like AVEVA, Siemens, and Rockwell are all previewing agent-based copilots for engineering tools, and hyperscalers are pushing agent frameworks into industrial data platforms. The practical implication is that the security model shifts. A traditional HMI user has fixed permissions; an agent operating on their behalf may chain those permissions in ways nobody predicted. This week's disclosure that OpenAI's own agents found and exploited a Linux kernel bug on OpenAI's infrastructure is the canary — agents are already capable of behaviour that looks a lot like an insider threat, but at machine speed. For OT, the immediate mitigations are unglamorous: keep agents on read-only historian replicas, never grant write access to controllers, and log every agent action to a tamper-evident store. Adoption challenges are real. Agents hallucinate parameters, misinterpret tag descriptions, and struggle with the implicit tribal knowledge that lives in a plant. Expect a rocky 12–24 months where the winners will be sites that treat agents as untrusted contractors — useful, supervised, and never given the master key.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) remains the standard most controls engineers actually need to internalise, and it's worth revisiting given this week's KEV additions. The zone-and-conduit model exists precisely so that a vCenter compromise at Level 3 doesn't automatically become a controller compromise at Level 1 — but only if conduits are enforced with real segmentation, not just VLAN tags on a shared switch. If your last risk assessment predates your current virtualisation footprint, it's stale. ISA is also progressing work on ISA/IEC 62443-4-2 profiles for specific device classes, which should make procurement conversations easier — instead of arguing with a vendor about whether their PLC 'supports 62443', you'll be able to reference a specific security level and required capability list. Expect more clarity on this through late 2026. On the NIST side, the ongoing evolution of SP 800-82 Rev.3 continues to be the most practical companion document for anyone bridging IT security frameworks to OT reality. If you're being asked to map your OT controls to NIST CSF 2.0 for insurance or board reporting, 800-82 Rev.3's mapping annexes save a substantial amount of work.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the 2022 compromise of a European wind farm operator (Deutsche Windtechnik and related incidents), where ransomware operators disrupted remote monitoring for thousands of turbines. The turbines themselves kept generating — safety systems and local controllers were unaffected — but operators lost the ability to remotely adjust pitch, yaw, and curtailment, forcing conservative operation and manual site visits for weeks. The root cause was IT-side ransomware that encrypted the SCADA gateway infrastructure sitting between the operator's enterprise network and the turbine fleet. There was no exotic ICS exploit; standard Windows ransomware simply landed on the wrong side of a poorly enforced boundary. The remote monitoring architecture assumed the enterprise network was trusted. Three lessons remain sharp. First, availability of remote monitoring is itself a safety and revenue issue — plan for extended loss and rehearse manual operation. Second, the SCADA gateway is a Level 3.5 asset and deserves Level 3.5 protection, not enterprise-server treatment. Third, vendor remote-access paths are often the weakest conduit; enforce jump-host isolation, MFA, and session recording as if the vendor were already compromised, because eventually one will be.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added a substantial batch of KEV entries this week, and several matter for OT. CVE-2026-59310 (VMware vCenter path traversal leading to RCE) is the most urgent for plant environments — vCenter frequently underpins virtualised historians, HMIs, and engineering workstations at Purdue Level 3, and a compromised vCenter effectively owns everything it hosts. Pair this with CVE-2026-8452 (Citrix NetScaler ADC/Gateway DoS) if you use NetScaler for remote engineering access, and CVE-2026-55040 (SharePoint auth bypass) for anyone hosting O&M documentation or MOC workflows there. The Linux kernel privilege escalation (CVE-2026-53362) affects RHEL, SUSE and downstream distributions common in historian and MES stacks — patch prioritisation should follow standard Level 3/3.5 change windows, but the active-exploitation label warrants moving it up. CVE-2026-33824 (Microsoft IKE double-free, RCE) is significant if you terminate site-to-site VPNs on Windows RRAS for remote sites or vendor tunnels. Older entries like the Ajax.NET Professional deserialization and libuser race condition suggest CISA is sweeping through legacy exploitation telemetry — worth checking any long-lived custom web front-ends on plant systems. Practically: audit vCenter, NetScaler, and any internet-adjacent Windows RRAS this week; confirm your Linux patch cadence for historians covers the August kernel updates; and re-verify that SharePoint sites carrying controlled documents enforce MFA and modern authentication.Read more →
