Today's Knowledge Bite

The IEC 61850 MMS protocol cited in today's ABB advisory traces its lineage back to MAP/TOP — General Motors' Manufacturing Automation Protocol from the mid-1980s — via the MMS standard ISO 9506. That means the message framing your modern substation IED uses to talk to a SCADA front-end shares DNA with code originally written to coordinate robots on a Detroit assembly line. It's also why MMS feels strangely object-oriented compared to Modbus: it was designed for an era that expected industrial networks to look like distributed CORBA.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronics: Elmer Sperry's gyrocompass autopilots for the US Navy in 1911 already implemented proportional-integral-derivative action mechanically, using flyweights and dashpots to stabilise ship heading. Nicholas Minorsky formalised the math in 1922 after watching helmsmen — noting that good helmsmen don't just react to current error, they anticipate based on rate of change and accumulate a sense of persistent bias. So the algorithm in your loop controller today is essentially the codified instinct of an early-20th-century sailor, and the 'derivative kick' problem you tune around is the same one Sperry's mechanical engineers solved with viscous damping over a century ago.

Tech Spotlight

Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) carrying OPC UA is finally moving from trade-show demo to real deployment, and it's worth understanding before your next greenfield project. The premise is simple: standard Ethernet has always been non-deterministic, so vendors built parallel fieldbuses (PROFINET IRT, EtherCAT, Sercos) to guarantee microsecond-level cycle times. TSN — a set of IEEE 802.1 amendments including 802.1AS time sync, 802.1Qbv scheduled traffic, and 802.1CB frame replication — bakes determinism into ordinary switched Ethernet, so a single converged network can carry motion control, process I/O, video, and IT traffic without collision. For controls engineers the practical implication is that the 'one cable to the machine' vision is becoming achievable, with OPC UA pub/sub providing the semantic layer and TSN providing the timing. Siemens, B&R, Beckhoff, Rockwell, and Schneider have all shipped TSN-capable hardware, and the OPC Foundation's FLC (Field Level Communications) initiative defines the profiles. Adoption challenges remain real, however: TSN switch configuration is genuinely harder than VLAN-and-go, the ecosystem of TSN-aware diagnostic tools is thin, and many existing devices need firmware-or-silicon refreshes to participate. Most sites adopting TSN today are doing so on isolated cells, not as a plant backbone — and that's probably the right pace for now.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443 continues to be the centre of gravity for OT cybersecurity, and the part most relevant to today's ABB advisory cluster is 62443-4-1 (secure product development lifecycle) and 62443-4-2 (technical security requirements for components). The Symphony Plus PostgreSQL exposure is a textbook 4-1 supply-chain concern: a control system inherits CVEs from a bundled open-source database, and the vendor's SDLC must include monitoring and patching of those upstream components. When evaluating advisories like today's, ask your vendor for their 62443-4-1 certification status and their documented process for embedded third-party component patching — it tells you more about future risk than any single CVE score. On the asset-owner side, 62443-2-1 (security program requirements) was substantially revised in 2024 and is the document that should be driving your patch-management policy. The revised standard puts more weight on risk-based patching cadence and compensating controls — useful ammunition when you need to justify to management why an unpatched OPTIMAX server behind segmentation and MFA is acceptable until the next outage window. NIST SP 800-82 Rev 3 remains the best free companion reference, particularly its updated guidance on remote access architectures, which dovetails with this week's ScreenConnect and SimpleHelp KEV entries.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week, given the FBI's cargo-theft alert, is the 2023 Port of Nagoya ransomware incident in Japan. The Lockbit affiliate that hit the Nagoya United Terminal System took down container-handling operations at Japan's busiest port for roughly three days, halting Toyota parts logistics among other flows. The intrusion vector was reportedly a vulnerable VPN appliance, and the impact wasn't a clever OT exploit — it was encryption of the Windows-based terminal management system that schedules crane and truck movements. Physical equipment was never directly attacked, yet the port stopped. The lessons map directly to this week's news. First, the 'OT' system that takes you down is often a logistics or scheduling layer running on plain Windows, not a PLC — so your asset inventory and segmentation strategy must include those Level 3/3.5 systems, not just the controllers. Second, internet-facing remote-access appliances remain the single most common entry point; the SonicWall and SD-WAN Manager advisories above are the 2026 edition of the same problem. Third, recovery time depends almost entirely on whether you have tested offline backups of the orchestration databases — at Nagoya, the team restored from tape, which worked, but only because someone had insisted on keeping the tape regime alive.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

Today's headline event for plant-floor engineers is an unusually large bundle of CISA ICS advisories targeting ABB's portfolio. Affected products span ABB Ability OPTIMAX (auth bypass via Azure AD SSO integration), AWIN GW100 gateways (unauthenticated reboot and configuration disclosure), the Edgenius Management Portal (unauthenticated RCE via crafted node messages), Ability Symphony Plus Engineering (downstream PostgreSQL ≤13.11 flaws), PCM600 (path traversal leading to code execution, affecting versions 1.5 through 2.13), and System 800xA / Symphony Plus IEC 61850 MMS client implementations. Collectively these touch power generation, transmission protection engineering, and broad DCS estates — meaning if you run ABB anywhere from substation automation to combined-cycle controls, expect to be patching or compensating-controlling this quarter. On the broader threat surface, SecurityWeek reports two trends that matter to OT: the FBI is warning of a sharp rise in hacker-enabled cargo theft, where threat actors compromise freight brokers and carriers to redirect physical shipments — directly relevant to logistics, rail, and warehouse-control teams. Separately, a 'mini Shai-Hulud' supply-chain worm has hit roughly 1,800 victims via SAP, Lightning, and Intercom npm packages with combined ~10M monthly downloads, a reminder that MES/ERP integration layers and any node-based HMI tooling inherit upstream package risk. The AI-and-exploitation theme is hardening into something operational. Anthropic launched Claude Security in response to what it calls the 'Mythos' era of near-instant exploitation, and SecurityWeek frames cybercrime as now industrialised — time-to-exploit measured in hours, not weeks. For controls engineers, the practical consequence is that the comfortable 30–90 day OT patch window is shrinking; vendor advisories like today's ABB batch will be weaponised faster than your next maintenance outage. Finally, SonicWall is urging immediate firewall patching for bugs allowing security-control bypass and crash conditions. Many sites still use SonicWall as the IT/OT boundary or remote-access concentrator, so this one belongs on the IT team's desk before the weekend.Read more →
