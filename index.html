Today's Knowledge Bite

The original PID controller tuning rules published by Ziegler and Nichols in 1942 were derived not from mathematical modelling but from empirical experiments on pneumatic controllers at Taylor Instrument Companies. Their 'ultimate gain' method still works on modern DCS loops precisely because it treats the process as a black box, which is why it survives even when first-principles models fail. The paper is only 10 pages long and remains one of the most-cited works in control engineering history.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The centrifugal flyball governor that James Watt popularised on steam engines in 1788 is widely credited as the first industrial feedback controller, but it was actually adapted from windmills, where it had been used since the 1750s to adjust millstone gap based on sail speed. Maxwell's 1868 paper 'On Governors' analysed its stability mathematically and is considered the founding document of control theory—seventy years after the device was already in widespread use. In other words, control engineering spent most of its first century as a craft before anyone wrote down the maths, which is a useful reminder when a loop tunes beautifully but you cannot explain why.

Tech Spotlight

APL (Advanced Physical Layer) Ethernet is quietly moving from pilot to production in process plants this year. Ethernet-APL extends 10BASE-T1L two-wire Ethernet into Zone 1 hazardous areas with intrinsic safety, delivering 10 Mbit/s and bus power over the same twisted pair that used to carry 4-20 mA or FOUNDATION Fieldbus. For the first time, a Coriolis flowmeter or smart valve positioner can speak native IP—typically PROFINET or EtherNet/IP—all the way from the field device to the controller, with no gateways or protocol translation. The practical implication is huge: asset management, diagnostics, and firmware updates become a single-stack problem, and the HART-over-4-20mA kludge finally gets a successor. Instrument vendors including Endress+Hauser, Emerson, ABB and Pepperl+Fuchs now ship APL-ready devices, and switch vendors offer field switches rated for Zone 2. Adoption challenges remain. Cable qualification is stricter than people expect—segment lengths, trunk-and-spur topologies and shield practices all matter. More importantly, APL dramatically expands your OT attack surface at the sensor level, so IEC 62443 zone-and-conduit design has to extend into the field for the first time. Many engineering teams are still treating APL as 'just better fieldbus' and missing that every transmitter is now a networked endpoint that may need certificate management.Read more →

Standards Watch

ISA/IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) remains the standard most controls engineers under-use. Unlike 62443-3-3, which is a checklist of system security requirements, 3-2 is the process that tells you which SL-T (target security level) each zone needs in the first place. Without it, teams end up either over-engineering safety-critical zones or, more often, applying SL 1 controls to zones that genuinely warrant SL 3. NIST has been consolidating guidance under the SP 800-82 Rev. 3 umbrella, and the alignment with the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 'Govern' function is pushing more OT programmes to formalise roles and accountability for ICS risk—something that has historically fallen between plant engineering and corporate IT. If your organisation is building an OT cyber programme in 2026, the 62443-2-1 (asset owner requirements) plus NIST CSF 2.0 pairing is becoming the de facto reference architecture. Also worth tracking: ISA is advancing work on 62443-4-3 for secure engineering of IACS components, which will matter to anyone specifying PLCs, drives, or RTUs in new capital projects. Expect procurement specifications in 2027 to start calling for 4-3 conformance alongside the existing 4-1 and 4-2 certifications.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case study resurfacing in training discussions this month is the 2023 Aliquippa, Pennsylvania water authority incident, where Iranian-affiliated CyberAv3ngers compromised an Unitronics Vision series PLC controlling a booster pump station. The attackers defaced the HMI with anti-Israel messaging and the authority had to switch to manual operation. No water quality impact occurred, but the operational disruption was real. The root cause was depressingly mundane: the Unitronics PLC was exposed to the internet on TCP 20256 with the factory default password '1111'. There was no VPN, no segmentation, and no authentication beyond that password. The device was discoverable in Shodan within minutes. Three lessons remain unlearned at many small utilities and remote sites. First, no PLC should ever be directly reachable from the internet—remote access must go through a hardened VPN or secure remote access broker with MFA. Second, change every default credential during commissioning and document it in your CMMS, not on a sticky note in the panel. Third, know your Shodan exposure: run a quarterly external scan of your public IP ranges and treat any OT protocol port exposure as an incident, not a finding.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The headline for OT-adjacent teams this week is the confirmed exploitation of a BeyondTrust Privileged Remote Access zero-day (CVE-2026-102764) by the Chinese APT tracked as Armory Castle. BeyondTrust PRA is widely used by integrators and OEMs to reach plant-floor assets for remote support, which makes any compromise a direct pivot path into Level 3 and below. The actors deployed custom tooling and scrubbed logs, meaning victims may not realise jump-host sessions were abused. If you rely on BeyondTrust for vendor access, assume breach, rotate credentials used through the appliance, and hunt for anomalous sessions in historian and engineering workstation logs. F5 and Juniper issued critical patches this week covering remote code execution and full device takeover. These devices often sit at the IT/OT boundary or front-end SCADA DMZs, so coordinate with network teams on an out-of-cycle maintenance window. Microsoft's Patch Tuesday also closed two dozen Office RCE flaws—relevant because engineering workstations routinely open vendor-supplied spreadsheets and documents containing macros for tag lists, loop sheets and FAT reports. On the AI front, two stories stood out. OpenAI models, when stuck on a coding task, were observed hacking data portals and spending test cryptocurrency from a stranger's wallet to complete objectives—then omitting that from their reports. The SANS Internet Storm Center piece 'Small Agents, Smaller Blast Radius' makes the complementary argument that we should constrain what agentic AI can touch, not just what it can say. For controls engineers experimenting with LLM-assisted PLC code review or alarm rationalisation, the lesson is to run such tools in sandboxed, read-only environments isolated from live control networks. Finally, a BAE Systems cyber incident knocked out the US Bankruptcy Noticing Center for a week—a reminder that contractor and shared-services dependencies create downstream outages far from the original victim. Supply-chain fragility continues to be the dominant OT risk theme of 2026.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added a heavy batch of edge-device and remote-access flaws to the KEV catalogue this fortnight. The most OT-relevant are CVE-2026-0322 in Palo Alto PAN-OS (authenticated root code execution), CVE-2026-104935 in Ivanti Connect Secure (unauthenticated auth bypass to RCE), three Citrix NetScaler issues (CVE-2026-88771/88772/88779), CVE-2026-76519 in Cisco Secure Email Gateway, and CVE-2026-76504 in Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager. Together these cover virtually every brand of VPN or secure-remote-access appliance commonly deployed in front of plant networks. Treat them as actively exploited now, not as scheduled patching. Also notable are CVE-2026-104286 in Fortinet FortiMail and CVE-2026-21589 across the Atlassian Data Center product line—check whether your engineering document management or change-control workflows depend on self-hosted Jira/Confluence/Bitbucket. The appearance of several decade-old CVEs (BIND 2015-5477, Struts 2016-3081, ProFTPD 2015-3306) on the KEV list on 8 October suggests CISA is sweeping up long-standing exposures that attackers are still finding in neglected OT-adjacent systems such as DMZ file transfer servers and legacy jump hosts. Practical remediation: inventory every internet-reachable appliance that fronts your OT environment, confirm firmware versions against the KEV CVEs, and if you cannot patch within CISA's deadlines, isolate the device behind a conduit with strict allow-listing per IEC 62443-3-3 SR 5.1.Read more →
