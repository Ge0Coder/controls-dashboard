Today's Knowledge Bite

The FragAttacks vulnerabilities resurfacing in this week's SCALANCE advisory were originally disclosed in 2020, yet they affect the fundamental 802.11 frame aggregation design — meaning every Wi-Fi chipset ever made is theoretically susceptible. What is surprising is that the practical attack requires the victim to be tricked into connecting to an attacker-controlled server, so on an air-gapped shop-floor wireless network with no internet egress, the real-world exploitability drops dramatically. It is a useful case study in why raw CVSS scores mislead in OT contexts.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues to edge from demo booths into real deployments, and 2026 is shaping up to be the year where several large automotive and semiconductor fabs commit to it for new lines. The promise is simple: a single converged Ethernet network carrying deterministic motion control traffic, standard OPC UA PubSub telemetry, and regular IT traffic — replacing the historical split between fieldbus (Profinet IRT, EtherCAT, Sercos) and the supervisory network. TSN provides the bounded latency and zero jitter via IEEE 802.1Qbv time-aware shaping, while OPC UA PubSub provides the vendor-neutral data model on top.

The practical implication for controls engineers is that your network switches become safety-critical assets in a way they never were before. A misconfigured TSN schedule or a switch that fails to honour gate control lists will cause drive faults, not just slow SCADA updates. You will need PTP (IEEE 1588) time synchronisation engineered end-to-end, and your cybersecurity posture has to account for the fact that an attacker on the converged network can potentially disrupt motion control by flooding non-scheduled queues or tampering with PTP.

Adoption challenges remain real. TSN-capable switches from Cisco, Hirschmann, Siemens and Moxa are available but pricey, and the configuration tooling (CNC — Centralised Network Configuration) is still maturing and mostly vendor-specific despite IEEE 802.1Qcc. Multi-vendor interoperability at line speed is improving but not turnkey. For brownfield sites, the realistic path is TSN islands for new cells bridged to legacy Profinet, not a rip-and-replace.

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (Security Risk Assessment for System Design) and 62443-4-2 (Component Requirements) remain the two parts most directly relevant to day-to-day engineering work, and the ISA99 committee has been actively working on a second edition of 4-2 to better address cloud-connected components, wireless, and — importantly — virtualised PLCs and software-defined control. If you are specifying new equipment, 4-2 Security Level 2 is quietly becoming the de facto procurement baseline in European utilities and pharma, with SL3 required for safety-adjacent components.

On the regulatory side, the EU Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) enforcement dates are now firmly on the horizon: the main obligations on manufacturers apply from December 2027, but vulnerability reporting obligations kick in earlier, in September 2026. For OT vendors this is a bigger deal than NIS2 because CRA attaches cybersecurity requirements directly to products placed on the EU market, with CE-marking implications. Controls engineers should expect vendors to start shipping SBOMs and signed firmware as standard, and to update procurement specs to require CRA conformity declarations.

NIST also quietly updated the OT overlay in SP 800-82 Rev 3 guidance addenda, with more concrete mappings to IEC 62443 zones and conduits. If your site uses NIST CSF 2.0 as its top-level framework but engineers work in 62443 terms, the mapping tables are genuinely useful for bridging the auditor-engineer conversation.

Incident of the Week

This week's spotlight is the Lotus Wiper campaign against Venezuela's energy sector, disclosed this week by multiple researchers. The malware was deployed in the days prior to US military action in the region and specifically targets recovery mechanisms — it deletes shadow copies, corrupts boot records, overwrites physical drives in random-order chunks to defeat partial recovery, and systematically wipes files by extension. Early reporting suggests initial access was via stolen VPN credentials into an engineering contractor's remote access, followed by lateral movement through a flat Windows domain that spanned both corporate and generation-plant networks.

What makes this relevant rather than just geopolitical news is the architecture that enabled it. The affected utilities reportedly had domain trusts extending into the OT environment, with engineering workstations dual-homed between IT and control networks, and historian servers reachable from the corporate domain via SMB. Once the wiper executed on the domain controllers, it propagated via Group Policy and scheduled tasks — the same playbook as NotPetya in 2017 and the Ukrainian attacks of 2022 and 2023.

Lessons for controls engineers: first, a separate OT Active Directory forest (or no AD in OT at all) is worth the operational pain — shared domains are a single point of catastrophic failure. Second, remote access for contractors must terminate in a jump host with MFA and session recording, not route directly into the control network. Third, test your recovery from bare metal, not just from backups — Lotus Wiper specifically targets the recovery path, and many sites discover during an incident that their gold images, engineering project files, and PLC backups all live on the same file server that just got wiped.

ICS / OT News Digest

CISA issued a notably heavy batch of ICS advisories this week, dominated by Siemens products that are staples on many plant floors. Industrial Edge Management carries an authorization bypass that lets an unauthenticated remote attacker reach connected Edge Devices via the remote connection feature — a direct path from enterprise into edge compute. SINEC NMS has a similar authorization bypass allowing arbitrary user password resets, RUGGEDCOM CROSSBOW Station Access Controller has an RCE/DoS flaw, and SCALANCE W-700 802.11n wireless access points pick up a bundle of older Wi-Fi stack CVEs (the FragAttacks family). If you run Siemens networking or Edge in production, this is a coordinated patch cycle worth scheduling rather than treating as individual tickets.

Two third-party OT devices round out the advisories. SenseLive X3050, a widely deployed Modbus/IIoT gateway, has eleven CVEs in V1.523 that collectively enable full device takeover — if you have these at remote pumping, metering, or solar sites, treat them as compromised-until-patched and verify they are not reachable from the internet. Silex SD-330AC industrial Wi-Fi bridges and their AMC Manager controller have RCE, DoS, and unauthenticated configuration change flaws; these devices commonly backhaul machine data in factories and the AMC Manager's unauthenticated config change is particularly nasty because it can persist attacker settings across reboots.

On the threat side, SecurityWeek reports a new wiper dubbed Lotus Wiper deployed against Venezuela's energy sector just before US military intervention there — it targets recovery mechanisms, overwrites drives, and systematically deletes files. The pattern mirrors earlier wipers used against Ukrainian energy (HermeticWiper, Industroyer2) and is another reminder that geopolitical escalation routinely spills into OT. The UK's cyber chief separately warned British operators that Russia, Iran, and China now drive the most serious attacks and urged readiness for scale attacks tied to any international conflict.

Also worth noting: a Mirai variant is actively exploiting a year-old command injection flaw in discontinued D-Link routers, a reminder that consumer-grade gear used as cellular or remote-site routers in OT still gets weaponised long after EOL. And the SBOM debate heated up again, with researchers arguing SBOMs without a governance and VEX intelligence layer are failing to stop supply chain attacks — a relevant point as NIS2 and CRA drive SBOM requirements into European OT procurement.

CISA Vulnerability Digest

KEV additions over the last 30 days have been enterprise-IT heavy but several entries matter to OT engineers because these systems usually sit in the DMZ or management plane adjacent to the control network. Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager picked up three KEV entries (CVE-2026-20122, 20128, 20133) covering privileged API abuse, credential exposure, and sensitive information disclosure. SD-WAN is increasingly the WAN fabric between corporate and plant sites; a compromised SD-WAN Manager is effectively a skeleton key to every remote facility. Patch and audit admin accounts immediately.

Today's addition is CVE-2026-33825 in Microsoft Defender, a local privilege escalation — relevant on engineering workstations and HMI servers running Defender. Also notable: Fortinet FortiClient EMS SQL injection (CVE-2026-21643), Ivanti EPMM unauthenticated RCE (CVE-2026-1340), and Apache ActiveMQ code injection (CVE-2026-34197). ActiveMQ in particular shows up in historian and MES stacks more often than people realise. The PaperCut flaw (CVE-2023-27351) is flagged as actively used in ransomware — printing infrastructure is frequently the pivot point from IT into OT during ransomware sweeps.

Practical priorities: patch any internet-reachable SD-WAN Manager, FortiClient EMS, and Ivanti EPMM this week; inventory ActiveMQ instances in your historian/MES chain; and confirm Defender and SharePoint patch levels on engineering workstations. The re-appearance of very old Office and VBA CVEs (2009, 2012) on the KEV list underlines that unpatched engineering laptops opening vendor-supplied spreadsheets remain a real initial-access vector.
