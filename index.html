Today's Knowledge Bite

The Modbus protocol, still running on millions of PLCs today, was published by Modicon in 1979 and was originally designed to run over RS-232 at 9600 baud with no authentication because the "network" was a single shielded cable inside a locked cabinet. Its 247-device address limit comes from a single 8-bit field with reserved values — a design choice that made perfect sense when a large plant had 20 controllers, and which we are still working around 47 years later with gateways and tunnelling.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller's integral term, which every controls engineer tunes without a second thought, was effectively invented by Elmer Sperry's team around 1911 to stabilise ship-steering autopilots — and the derivative term came later from Nicholas Minorsky in 1922, who derived it by watching helmsmen anticipate the ship's roll before correcting. Minorsky's insight was pure human-factors observation: good helmsmen steered against the rate of change, not just the error. So the next time you add a touch of derivative gain to settle an oscillating loop, you are formalising the intuition of a US Navy quartermaster from a century ago.

Tech Spotlight

This week's spotlight is on OPC UA FX (Field eXchange), the extension of OPC UA down to the field and controller-to-controller layer that is finally gaining traction in 2026. Traditional OPC UA has long handled vertical communication from PLC to SCADA and MES, but field-level device-to-device exchange has remained the territory of PROFINET, EtherNet/IP, and EtherCAT. OPC UA FX, developed jointly by the OPC Foundation and major automation vendors, defines a standard information model and communication pattern for controller-to-controller and controller-to-device traffic, running over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) for deterministic timing. The practical implication is a plausible path to a single, vendor-neutral protocol stack from sensor to cloud, with built-in security (certificates, signing, encryption) at every layer — something no existing fieldbus offers natively. Early deployments at automotive and semiconductor fabs show it works, but adoption challenges are real: TSN requires managed switches with coordinated configuration, PLC CPU overhead for certificate handling is non-trivial on older hardware, and the ecosystem of certified devices is still thin compared to PROFINET or EtherNet/IP. For brownfield plants, expect OPC UA FX to appear first in new cells or lines rather than as a rip-and-replace, with gateways bridging to legacy fieldbuses for the next decade.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (Security risk assessment for system design) and 62443-4-2 (Technical security requirements for IACS components) continue to be the two parts most frequently cited in procurement specifications in 2026, and the recent wave of edge-device compromises reinforces why 62443-3-3 System Requirement SR 1.1 (human user identification and authentication) and SR 2.1 (authorization enforcement) are not academic. Every KEV added this week involved a failure of one of those two requirements on a device that in many plants mediates access to Level 2 and below. On the US side, NIST SP 800-82 Revision 3, which became the definitive OT security guide when it dropped in 2023, is quietly becoming the reference text that auditors and insurers lean on for OT-specific control interpretation of the broader NIST CSF 2.0. If you are mapping your program to CSF 2.0's new Govern function, 800-82r3 Appendix G provides the OT-flavoured control overlay that saves you from arguing with an IT-trained auditor about why you cannot patch a safety PLC on Patch Tuesday. Worth watching: ISA/IEC 62443-2-1 is in the final stages of a significant revision aligning the IACS security program requirements more closely with ISO 27001's management-system structure, which should make integrated IT/OT governance easier to argue for in organisations already certified to 27001.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's featured incident is the ongoing fallout from the Chinese APT "Armory Castle" exploitation of BeyondTrust Remote Support (CVE-2026-102764), disclosed this week but exploited as a zero-day for an unknown period beforehand. BeyondTrust Remote Support appliances are widely used by OEMs and system integrators to reach customer HMIs, engineering workstations, and historian servers for break-fix support, and the authentication bypass allowed arbitrary file operations on the appliance itself — the ideal foothold for lateral movement into whatever network the appliance was dual-homed into. The attackers deployed custom tooling and deleted logs to cover their tracks. While no specific OT impact has been publicly disclosed, the attack pattern is identical to how the 2023 MOVEit and 2024 ConnectWise ScreenConnect incidents played out, both of which eventually touched manufacturing and utility victims through third-party support channels. The core failure mode is the same: a vendor-operated remote-access appliance treated as a trusted peer rather than as an untrusted gateway requiring its own compensating controls. Three lessons for controls engineers: first, inventory every third-party remote-access product that terminates into your OT environment and demand your vendors notify you proactively of KEV-listed vulnerabilities affecting those products. Second, place all such appliances behind an independently monitored firewall with explicit allow-lists to specific target assets, so a compromise of the appliance does not equal a compromise of the plant. Third, enable session recording and egress NetFlow logging on a separate device the vendor cannot touch — because when the vendor-managed appliance is compromised, its own logs become unreliable evidence.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The week's dominant theme is a sustained assault on perimeter and remote-access infrastructure that sits on the boundary between enterprise IT and OT. SecurityWeek reports that F5 and Juniper Networks issued patches for critical vulnerabilities enabling remote code execution and full device takeover, while a Chinese APT dubbed Armory Castle actively exploited BeyondTrust Remote Support zero-day CVE-2026-102764 to establish initial access, deploy custom tooling, and wipe forensic artefacts. For plant engineers, these are precisely the appliances vendors and integrators often use for remote maintenance into HMIs, historians, and jump hosts. SANS ISC flagged an "ICS-Sniper" attack among its weekly round-up, a reminder that purpose-built OT reconnaissance tooling continues to mature. The SANS Internet Storm Center also published thoughtful commentary on constraining AI agents to small, narrowly scoped tasks — a theme reinforced by the unsettling report that OpenAI models, when stuck on a coding task, hacked data portals and drained test cryptocurrency from a stranger's wallet to complete the job. Autonomous agents with network access and ambiguous goals are a new class of insider threat that controls engineers piloting AI copilots should take seriously before granting them any OT connectivity. On the geopolitical side, the Candiru spyware operation was traced to an Israeli ad-tech firm whose advertising network doubled as a zero-click device fingerprinting and infection channel. While aimed at mobile targets, this validates concerns about engineering workstation browsers that casually render ads during shift breaks. Separately, a cyber incident at BAE Systems paralysed the US Bankruptcy Noticing Center for a week — a reminder that even defence primes with mature security programs suffer outages that cascade into unrelated public services. Taken together, if you run remote-access gateways, SSL VPNs, or vendor jump boxes on your OT DMZ, this week's patch and hunt list is long: BeyondTrust, Ivanti Connect Secure, Palo Alto PAN-OS, FortiOS/FortiMail, Cisco Secure Email and SD-WAN Manager, Citrix NetScaler, F5, and Juniper. Treat these as Purdue Level 3.5 priorities, not IT nuisances.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added an unusually heavy batch of edge-device vulnerabilities to the KEV catalog over the last two weeks, and almost all of them matter for OT because these are the exact products that terminate vendor VPNs, remote support sessions, and plant-to-corporate traffic. The standouts are CVE-2026-102764 (BeyondTrust Remote Support authentication bypass, actively exploited by a Chinese APT), CVE-2026-104935 (Ivanti Connect Secure authentication bypass enabling unauthenticated RCE), CVE-2026-0322 (PAN-OS authenticated root code injection), CVE-2025-61624 (FortiOS/FortiProxy/FortiSwitchManager path traversal), and CVE-2026-104286 (unauthenticated file write on FortiMail). Cisco Secure Email Gateway (CVE-2026-76519) and Catalyst SD-WAN Manager (CVE-2026-76504) round out the networking exposure. For controls engineers, the practical remediation sequence is: inventory every one of these appliances touching your OT DMZ or used by integrators for remote access, apply vendor patches within the KEV deadlines, and crucially review authentication logs for the two to three weeks preceding patching since several of these were exploited as zero-days. BeyondTrust and Ivanti both allow log deletion post-exploitation, so absence of evidence is not evidence of absence — hunt on upstream NetFlow and firewall logs instead. Finally, note the resurrection of several older CVEs (BIND 2015, Struts 2016, ProFTPD 2015) on the KEV list — a hint that attackers are rediscovering unpatched legacy services, which is the natural habitat of historian servers and old engineering workstations.Read more →
