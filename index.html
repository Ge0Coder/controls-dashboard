Today's Knowledge Bite

The Siemens S7 protocol still in wide use today derives from a design lineage that predates modern authentication concepts — the original S7comm has no cryptographic protection, which is why S7CommPlus was introduced. Yet even S7CommPlus's anti-replay integrity check was reverse-engineered from PCAPs within a few years of release. This is a large part of why AI-assisted attackers can now generate valid PLC payloads so quickly: the protocol semantics are well-documented in the public research literature.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was formalised not in a research lab but on a US Navy destroyer. Nicholas Minorsky derived the three-term control law in 1922 by watching helmsmen steer the USS New Mexico and noting that good ones didn't just respond to heading error — they anticipated based on how fast the error was changing and integrated out persistent drift. Nearly every temperature loop, flow loop, and motion axis you tune today is a direct descendant of a mathematical description of human steering behaviour, which is why PID feels intuitive to us: it was reverse-engineered from us.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) is finally moving from vendor demos into pilot deployments, and it is worth understanding even if you are not deploying it this year. The idea is simple in concept: combine OPC UA's rich, secure, model-based data exchange with deterministic Ethernet timing, so that the same wire can carry high-priority motion-control traffic and non-critical MES data without the former ever being late. Achieving this requires TSN-capable switches (IEEE 802.1Qbv scheduled traffic, 802.1AS time sync) end-to-end, and OPC UA PubSub rather than the traditional client/server model. For controls engineers, the practical implication is a potential collapse of the traditional fieldbus/Ethernet split: instead of Profinet IRT here, EtherCAT there, and a separate MES Ethernet on top, you get a single converged network. That simplifies cabling and cabinet design but shifts complexity into network engineering and time-synchronisation domains that most controls teams are not yet staffed for. Current adoption challenges include immature multi-vendor interoperability (TSN profile fragmentation is real), sparse tooling for diagnostics when a scheduled window is missed, and the fact that most installed I/O still doesn't speak PubSub. Expect it in new greenfield semiconductor and automotive lines first, and prepare by getting your network team certified in TSN fundamentals before your first project lands on their desk.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443 continues to be the reference point for anyone responding to advisories like today's Siemens PLC alert. Part 4-2 (component-level security requirements) is what a customer should be pointing to when asking a PLC vendor about authenticated firmware, secure boot, and role-based access — and part 3-3 (system security requirements) is where the SL-T (target security level) discussion for a given zone lives. If your risk assessment for a Siemens-heavy zone still shows SL-T 1, the AI-assisted threat landscape is a strong argument for revisiting that number. NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 remains the pragmatic companion for US operators, particularly its guidance on network segmentation and remote access — both directly relevant to reducing exposure to the current wave of vCenter and VPN-adjacent CVEs. It's also worth watching ISA's work on the ISA/IEC 62443-2-1 revision, which is sharpening the OT security program management requirements in ways that will affect how audits treat your patch management, MOC, and incident response processes. If you haven't mapped your existing plant security procedures against a 62443-2-1 gap analysis in the last 18 months, that's a worthwhile Q4 exercise.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's spotlight is the unfolding Cl0p / PTC Windchill campaign, in which the ransomware group has now publicly named over 40 victims, including several with heavy industrial footprints. The initial intrusion vector, based on public reporting, was a Windchill vulnerability exploited before broad patch adoption; from there, attackers exfiltrated PLM data — CAD models, BOMs, engineering change orders, and in some cases process documentation linked to shop-floor systems. What makes this incident notable is not the ransomware payload but the target: Windchill sits at the exact seam between engineering IT and OT, and its contents are often the crown jewels of a manufacturer's process knowledge. Even without any encryption event on plant-floor systems, the disclosure of proprietary control narratives, custom function blocks, or safety calculations can be operationally damaging for years. Three lessons for controls engineers. First, PLM and engineering document stores need to be treated as OT-adjacent assets in your risk register, not as generic IT. Second, ensure that anything Windchill exports to plant systems (recipes, part programs, tool data) is validated on the receiving side — a compromised PLM is a plausible supply-chain path into the DCS. Third, review who at your site has Windchill admin rights and whether those accounts have MFA and privileged access monitoring; historically, PLM admin accounts have been under-scrutinised compared to AD domain admins.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The top story for controls engineers today is a joint NSA/CISA advisory revealing that threat actors are using AI tooling to accelerate reconnaissance, payload crafting, and exploitation against Siemens PLCs deployed in US critical infrastructure. Reports indicate attackers are using LLMs to parse S7 protocol traffic, generate valid STEP 7/TIA Portal artifacts, and produce ladder-logic modifications that survive cursory operator review. The advisory carries technical detail and mitigations, and it should be treated as a call to immediately audit exposed Siemens assets, review firmware baselines, and verify that engineering workstation access is tightly controlled. In parallel, the Cl0p ransomware group has publicly named more than 40 victims of its PTC Windchill campaign, including Shell, Philips, Fiserv, Zebra, and Mindray. Windchill sits squarely in the engineering IT/OT boundary: it holds PLM data, CAD, BOMs, and often integrates with MES and shop-floor systems. Compromise there means potential exposure of proprietary process designs, control narratives, and supplier data — a nightmare for any manufacturer relying on Windchill as the system of record. CISA also issued an urgent push on newly exploited Microsoft, VMware, and Apple flaws, several of which landed on the KEV list this week. For OT sites, the VMware vCenter path traversal (CVE-2026-59310) is particularly concerning because vCenter almost universally underpins virtualised historians, HMIs, and jump servers in modern plant DMZs. The Microsoft IKE double-free (CVE-2026-33824) is likewise relevant anywhere site-to-site VPNs terminate on Windows. On the geopolitical front, the US indicted 17 Iranian nationals tied to the Mabna Institute, with $10M rewards on five of them — a reminder that state-linked actors continue to invest heavily in long-term access campaigns against Western targets, including those with industrial footprints.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

This week's KEV additions include several items with direct OT relevance. CVE-2026-59310 (VMware vCenter path traversal to RCE) is the standout — vCenter is the invisible backbone under most virtualised OT stacks, and an attacker with vCenter access can silently clone, snapshot, or modify HMI, historian, and engineering VMs. Patch immediately; if you cannot, restrict vCenter management interfaces to a hardened jump host and enforce MFA. CVE-2026-33824 (Windows IKE double-free) matters wherever your remote-access or site-to-site VPN terminates on Windows Server. Also worth flagging: CVE-2026-65400 (macOS Screen Sharing auth bypass) — some OEMs and integrators use Mac workstations for HMI development and remote support; the older VNC-derived screen sharing is unauthenticated in the exploit path. CVE-2026-55040 (SharePoint auth bypass) and the earlier CVE-2026-50522 (SharePoint deserialization RCE) are relevant because plant SharePoints frequently hold P&IDs, control narratives, and MOC documents. Finally, the Cisco ASA/FTD DoS (CVE-2026-20349) and Cisco FMC hard-coded password (CVE-2026-20316) should be prioritised in any site where these devices sit at the IT/OT boundary — a firewall reload during a production run is not a small event.Read more →
