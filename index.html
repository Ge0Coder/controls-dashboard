Today's Knowledge Bite

The Modbus protocol, still ubiquitous on plant floors 47 years after its 1979 release, was originally designed for Modicon's 484 PLC to run over a single RS-232 link at 9600 baud. Its function codes were never designed for authentication because the physical serial cable was the security boundary. Every modern Modbus/TCP deployment inherits that trust assumption — which is why a $30 USB-to-Ethernet adapter and a laptop can still write coils on production networks that haven't been properly segmented.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronics: Nicolas Minorsky worked out the mathematics of three-term control in 1922 while trying to automate the steering of the USS New Mexico, observing how a helmsman used not just current heading error but its rate of change and its accumulated history. His paper described what we now call proportional, derivative, and integral action in terms of a human operator's intuition. Nearly every loop running in your plant today is a direct descendant of a naval helmsman's instinct, formalised on paper a century ago.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues its slow march from spec to shop floor. The promise is compelling: a single Ethernet fabric carrying deterministic control traffic, engineering data, and IT payloads with bounded latency measured in microseconds, replacing the tangle of Profinet, EtherCAT, EtherNet/IP, and Modbus segments many plants still run in parallel. The OPC Foundation's Field Level Communications (FLC) initiative — backed by Siemens, Rockwell, ABB, Schneider, and B&R — is now producing interoperable controller-to-controller and controller-to-device profiles. Adoption reality is more sober. TSN requires switches that support IEEE 802.1Qbv scheduling, and mixed-vendor deployments still trip over configuration models (CNC vs. distributed, NETCONF/YANG maturity). Most greenfield projects choosing OPC UA/TSN today are doing so for specific cells — robotics, motion, semiconductor tools — rather than as a plant-wide backbone. Brownfield migration is nearly always a gateway story, not a rip-and-replace. For controls engineers, the practical near-term implication is that your next network design review should ask whether the switches you're specifying are TSN-capable even if you don't turn the features on yet. Managed switches with 802.1AS (time sync) and Qbv support are only modestly more expensive than standard industrial Ethernet gear, and the option value in 5–7 years is significant. Also worth tracking: how your DCS/PLC vendor plans to expose UA companion specs (PA-DIM, Machinery, Robotics) — that's where the interoperability payoff actually lands.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) remains the standard most controls engineers actually need to internalise, because it's the one that dictates how you partition zones and conduits and justify SL-T (target security level) assignments. If you haven't formally documented zones for your current plant, the exercise is more valuable than the certificate at the end — it forces conversations about which HMIs really need to talk to which controllers, and where your safety instrumented systems sit relative to the BPCS. ISA/IEC 62443-2-1, updated in 2024, tightened the requirements for the asset owner's security program, including explicit expectations around patch management, backup/recovery, and incident response for OT. Auditors are increasingly asking for evidence that these programs exist as OT-specific documents rather than IT policies with 'and OT' appended. If your organisation is heading toward IEC 62443 certification or aligning with the EU NIS2 directive, expect the 2-1 requirements to be the ones most frequently found wanting. On the NIST side, SP 800-82r3 (Guide to OT Security) remains the most practical free reference in English, and it now cross-maps cleanly to the CSF 2.0 Govern function — useful when translating between engineering and executive audiences.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the 2023 Aliquippa, Pennsylvania water authority incident, where an Iran-linked group known as CyberAv3ngers compromised a Unitronics Vision-series PLC controlling a booster station. The attackers gained access because the PLC was directly exposed to the internet with the default password '1111' on TCP port 20256 — Unitronics' PCOM protocol. They defaced the HMI with an anti-Israel message but did not manipulate the process; operators reverted to manual control and there was no impact on water quality. The incident happened because a small municipal utility procured integrated PLC/HMI hardware, an integrator commissioned it with defaults, and nobody performed a post-commissioning security review. It wasn't sophisticated — Shodan-class reconnaissance and a well-known default credential were sufficient. CISA and the FBI issued a joint advisory shortly after, and Unitronics devices remained a hunted target for months. Three lessons stand out. First, exposure of PLC protocols to the public internet is still shockingly common in smaller utilities and OEM skid deployments — an external attack surface review costs almost nothing. Second, default credentials on PLCs, HMIs, and network gear must be part of your commissioning checklist with sign-off, not an assumed integrator practice. Third, the fact that Aliquippa had a manual fallback is what turned a serious incident into a nuisance; every automated process should have a documented, practised manual mode of operation.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

Today's news cycle is heavy on IT and enterprise security stories rather than direct ICS incidents, but several items have downstream implications for plant environments. ReliaQuest confirmed a ShinyHunters intrusion originating from a phishing attack against an employee — a reminder that identity remains the softest attack surface, including for the managed detection providers many industrial firms now rely on. The Spring Framework has now received over 200 patches this year, an order of magnitude more than in 2024 or 2025; if your MES, historian, or vendor-supplied middleware runs on Spring, expect a heavier-than-usual patch cadence from your vendors. The Uber decision from the Dutch DPA — an €825M GDPR fine for automated driver account suspensions — is worth watching for anyone deploying AI-driven decisioning in industrial workflows. Regulators are increasingly willing to hit automated systems that produce material effects on individuals without adequate human review, and that logic will eventually reach worker-monitoring, safety, and quality systems on the shop floor. On the criminal side, an eight-year sentence for ATM jackpotting is a rare public reminder that physical/logical hybrid attacks against embedded devices are still commercially viable for criminal groups. The same tradecraft — direct-to-device serial or USB attacks, cash-out via manipulated firmware — maps closely to attack patterns against HMIs and RTUs in unattended remote sites. SANS ISC also continues its useful series on Entra/Graph login auditing, relevant to controls teams whose engineering workstations increasingly authenticate through cloud identity. CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA's KEV additions over the past two weeks lean heavily toward perimeter and virtualization infrastructure that OT teams depend on. Cisco Secure Firewall ASA/FTD (CVE-2026-20349) and Secure Firewall Management Center (CVE-2026-20316, hard-coded credentials) are widely deployed at the IT/OT boundary; a DoS reload on your DMZ firewall during a production window is a real operational risk, and hard-coded creds on FMC deserve immediate attention. Broadcom VMware vCenter (CVE-2026-59310, path traversal to RCE) is equally serious given how much OT virtualisation — historians, engineering workstations, jump hosts — sits on vSphere. On the Microsoft side, the IKE Service Extensions double-free (CVE-2026-33824) is remotely exploitable and affects any Windows host terminating IPsec, including many site-to-site VPNs bridging remote plants. SharePoint auth bypass (CVE-2026-55040) matters if you use SharePoint for document control, batch records, or SOPs. The Progress LoadMaster command injection (CVE-2026-8037) and both N-able N-central auth bypasses (CVE-2026-18556 and its incomplete-patch sibling -18577) should be prioritised by anyone using those products for remote management of plant IT. Practically: pull the KEV list, cross-reference against your asset inventory (including vendor-managed systems you don't directly patch), and pressure integrators to confirm remediation timelines. Where patching within CISA's due-date window isn't possible on production systems, document the compensating controls — network segmentation, ACLs, disabled services — as part of your 62443 zone and conduit records.Read more →
