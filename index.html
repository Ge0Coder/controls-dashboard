Today's Knowledge Bite

The KNX protocol, now in CISA's KEV list, was originally standardized in 1990 as EIB (European Installation Bus) and predates most modern IT security thinking by a decade. Its authentication model was designed around the assumption that the twisted-pair bus was physically inaccessible — an assumption that quietly collapsed the moment KNXnet/IP routers started bridging building networks to Ethernet in the 2000s. Many buildings commissioned before 2018 still run with the factory-default BCU key of all zeros.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was formalized not by a mathematician but by Nicolas Minorsky in 1922, while he was trying to figure out how to automatically steer the USS New Mexico. He derived the three-term control law by watching how experienced helmsmen anticipated the ship's motion — proportional to error, integral to correct drift, derivative to counter momentum. Nearly every temperature loop, flow controller, and motion drive you commissioned this year traces its lineage directly to a naval officer studying human intuition on a battleship's bridge.

Tech Spotlight

OPC UA over TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking) continues its slow but real march from marketing slides to real deployments, and 2026 has been the year it finally showed up in mainstream PLC firmware from Siemens, B&R, and Rockwell. The premise is simple: combine the semantic richness of OPC UA (typed information models, PubSub, security) with deterministic Ethernet timing at the switch level, so you can run motion control, safety, and enterprise telemetry across the same physical network without a proprietary fieldbus. Practically, what this means for a controls engineer today is that you can begin specifying a single Ethernet backbone from the sensor up to the MES, but only if your switches support IEEE 802.1Qbv (scheduled traffic) and your endpoints support the OPC UA FLC (Field Level Communications) profile. The catch is configuration complexity — TSN scheduling requires a centralized network configurator (CNC) that most sites don't yet have skills to operate, and interoperability testing between vendors is still uneven. Expect to run TSN in a single-vendor island for at least another two years before true multi-vendor integration is production-ready. The bigger implication is architectural: if OPC UA/TSN delivers on its promise, the classical Purdue model flattens considerably, and your segmentation strategy has to shift from layer boundaries to zone-and-conduit models per IEC 62443. Start planning that transition now, even if you're not deploying TSN yet.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) is seeing renewed attention as insurers begin explicitly requiring documented zone-and-conduit diagrams and target security levels (SL-T) as a condition for cyber coverage on industrial policies. Several major carriers updated their OT questionnaires in Q2 2026 to ask for SL-T assignments per zone, which is a significant shift from the previous checkbox-style questions about firewalls and antivirus. If you haven't formalized your zone and conduit model, this is now a business risk, not just a compliance exercise. On the NIST side, the CSF 2.0 OT Community Profile continues to mature, with new mappings published between CSF 2.0 subcategories and IEC 62443-2-1 requirements. This is useful because it lets a plant that already reports to corporate under NIST CSF avoid duplicative work when preparing for a 62443 audit — the crosswalk is now official rather than tribal knowledge. Worth watching: ISA/IEC 62443-4-2 edition 2 draft has entered committee review with proposed changes to component requirements around secure boot, cryptographic key storage, and software bill of materials (SBOM) delivery. If you specify PLCs, RTUs, or IEDs, the edition 2 requirements will materially raise the bar on what vendors must ship — start referencing the draft in new procurement specs now to avoid buying end-of-life-adjacent hardware.Read more →

Incident of the Week

The unfolding Iran-linked water utility campaign is this week's obvious focus. Based on public reporting and CISA advisories, the intrusion pattern is depressingly familiar: internet-exposed Unitronics Vision-series PLCs and similar small controllers with default passwords (often 1111) and factory Modbus/TCP or PCOM ports open to the world. Attackers logged in, changed HMI splash screens to political messages, and in at least one case manipulated setpoints on chemical dosing before operators noticed. No confirmed public health impact so far, but the potential was real. What makes this significant is scale — seven states now, with the actors clearly running Shodan-style sweeps rather than targeting specific utilities. The compromises succeeded not because of sophisticated tradecraft but because small municipal utilities lack dedicated OT staff and often accept whatever configuration the integrator left behind. Three lessons for controls engineers: first, Shodan-check your own external IP ranges monthly — if you don't, an adversary will. Second, any small PLC with a web server or remote programming port must sit behind a firewall with explicit allow-list rules, never a NAT hole punched by an integrator for convenience. Third, change every default credential at commissioning and document it in your CMMS; the 1111 password on Unitronics has now caused incidents on three continents and remains the single most exploited OT credential in the field.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The most consequential story for plant floor engineers today is the widening Iran-linked campaign against US municipal water systems. What began as a Minnesota-focused incident has now confirmed victims in at least Michigan, South Dakota, and Georgia, with three additional states reportedly targeted. Early indicators point to internet-exposed HMIs, weak or default credentials on remote access portals, and unpatched VPN concentrators as the primary entry vectors. Controls engineers at any water, wastewater, or adjacent utility should assume they are in scope and begin hunting today. SonicWall SMA1000 appliances are being actively exploited by the INC ransomware crew, chaining SSRF (CVE-2026-15409) with authenticated code injection (CVE-2026-15410) to achieve root and pivot into flat OT/IT networks. Many industrial sites still use SMA appliances for vendor remote support tunnels, making this a direct OT concern rather than a pure IT issue. If your integrator or OEM reaches your DCS through an SMA1000, that path is now a documented ransomware highway. Secondary but relevant: Midnight Blizzard (Russian SVR) is compromising hospitality Wi-Fi gateways to harvest Microsoft credentials — a reminder that engineers traveling to site commissioning or trade shows should treat any hotel or convention Wi-Fi as hostile and rely on cellular tethering or vetted VPNs. Separately, a critical Ruby on Rails file-read/RCE flaw affects a surprising number of vendor asset-management and CMMS web frontends; check your maintenance portals. On the KEV side, an unusual entry stands out: CVE-2023-4346 covering KNX protocol connection authorization was added, which is directly relevant to building automation, HVAC integration, and any facility using KNX for lighting or environmental control. This is one of the rare pure-OT protocol vulnerabilities to reach the KEV list.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA's recent KEV additions lean heavily toward perimeter and identity infrastructure that OT networks routinely depend on. The Cisco Secure FMC hard-coded password (CVE-2026-20316), Fortinet FortiOS symlink-persistence bypass (CVE-2025-68686), Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator command injection (CVE-2026-16812), and Check Point SmartConsole auth bypass (CVE-2026-16232) collectively cover most of the firewall management planes used to segment Purdue Level 3 from Level 3.5/DMZ. If your OT firewall management console is exposed to the IT network — or worse, reachable from the internet for MSSP access — patch these this week and audit admin sessions. The KNX Association entry (CVE-2023-4346) is the standout for facilities and building-controls engineers. The flaw lets an attacker purge devices and set a persistent BCU key lockout, effectively bricking a KNX installation until factory recovery. Mitigation is IP-level: block KNXnet/IP (UDP 3671) at the boundary, enable KNX Secure where hardware supports it, and inventory any KNX IP routers or interfaces that may have been installed by an MEP contractor without your knowledge. The SharePoint deserialization pair (CVE-2026-50522, CVE-2026-58644) and SonicWall SMA1000 chain are the most likely to appear in an OT-adjacent breach path this month, since both platforms commonly host engineering document libraries, P&IDs, and vendor remote-access sessions.
