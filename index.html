☀ Morning Briefing 2026-08-23 07:34 UTC

The single most important thing this weekend: the current CISA KEV list is stacked with actively-exploited vulnerabilities in the IT infrastructure that sits directly in front of your OT network — Cisco ASA/FTD and FMC, VMware vCenter, Fortinet FortiOS, and Progress LoadMaster. None are ICS-specific, but all are the load-bearing components of a typical Purdue Level 3.5 DMZ. Before Monday, confirm with your IT counterparts that your OT-adjacent firewalls, hypervisors, and load balancers are patched and that their management interfaces are not exposed beyond the OT management VLAN. If you can't answer that quickly, that gap is your first action item of the week.

Today's Knowledge Bite

The ubiquitous 4–20 mA current loop was standardised in the 1950s specifically because current, unlike voltage, does not degrade over long cable runs — and the 4 mA 'live zero' was chosen so a broken wire (0 mA) is instantly distinguishable from a legitimate zero-process reading. That single design decision has quietly prevented countless dangerous 'silent failure' scenarios for seventy years, and it's why the standard has outlived nearly every digital fieldbus that promised to replace it.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller has a stranger origin than most engineers realise: the proportional-integral-derivative algorithm was formalised not in a chemical plant or a factory, but in 1922 by Nicolas Minorsky while studying how helmsmen steered US Navy battleships. Minorsky observed that experienced helmsmen didn't just correct for the current heading error — they anticipated based on the rate of change and remembered persistent offsets from wind or current. He mathematised what those sailors were doing intuitively, and the result is the algorithm running in essentially every process loop on Earth today. Every time you tune a PID, you are formalising the instincts of a 1920s quartermaster at a ship's wheel.

Tech Spotlight

Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) with OPC UA over the top is finally moving from trade-show demos into real deployments, particularly in automotive and semiconductor fabs. The pitch is straightforward: standard Ethernet hardware, deterministic sub-millisecond latency good enough to replace EtherCAT or PROFINET IRT for motion control, and a single converged network carrying safety, motion, process, and IT traffic without the traditional segregation. OPC UA PubSub provides the semantic layer — self-describing tags, companion specifications for device profiles — while IEEE 802.1 TSN handles the timing. For controls engineers, the practical implication is that the historical trade-off between deterministic fieldbus and open Ethernet is dissolving. You can, in principle, put a servo drive, a vision system, and a historian on the same switch fabric. In practice, adoption is being slowed by three things: TSN-capable switches remain 3–5x the cost of standard managed switches, configuration tooling (CNC/CUC) is still vendor-fragmented despite the standards, and troubleshooting a converged network requires skills that blend traditional network engineering with real-time systems thinking. Most greenfield projects still default to PROFINET or EtherNet/IP because the ecosystem is mature and integrators know it. Expect TSN to arrive first in high-value motion applications where its determinism justifies the price premium, then gradually spread as switch silicon commoditises.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) continues to be the standard most controls engineers actually encounter in procurement language, and it's worth revisiting given this week's KEV list. The standard's core discipline — defining zones and conduits, then assigning a target Security Level (SL-T) to each — is exactly the framework that would have contained many of the IT-side vulnerabilities from bleeding into OT. If your vCenter managing OT VMs sits in the same zone as your enterprise vCenter, your zone model is wrong on paper before any attacker touches it. On the NIST side, the SP 800-82 Rev 3 guidance on OT security remains the most practical free reference for aligning IEC 62443 concepts with US regulatory expectations, and it explicitly addresses identity federation and remote access — the very topics this week's SANS posts covered. For organisations subject to NIS2 in Europe or the TSA pipeline directives in the US, the auditor's questions increasingly track 62443-2-1 (patch management, account management, backup) rather than pure technical controls. Documentation discipline is now as important as the controls themselves. A quiet but important trend: ISA/IEC 62443-4-2 component-level certification is showing up more frequently as a purchasing requirement, and several major PLC vendors now ship product lines with SL-2 certification out of the box. If you're speccing new hardware, ask for the certification report, not just the marketing claim — the scope statement tells you what was actually tested.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit is the 2023 Aliquippa, Pennsylvania water authority incident, where the Cyber Av3ngers group compromised a Unitronics Vision-series PLC controlling a booster station. The attackers didn't exploit a zero-day; the PLC was directly reachable from the internet on TCP/20256 with the factory-default password '1111'. They defaced the HMI with an anti-Israel message and, more importantly, forced the station into manual operation. Water pressure was maintained because operators noticed quickly and switched to manual control, but the incident triggered a CISA advisory and Congressional attention. The attack technique was almost trivially simple: Shodan-scan for exposed Unitronics devices, try the default credential, push a modified program. There was no sophistication, no supply chain compromise, no insider — just an internet-exposed controller with a well-known default password. Similar exposures for Unitronics, Siemens LOGO!, and various small process controllers remain in the tens of thousands globally. Three lessons stand true for every controls engineer. First, no PLC should ever be directly reachable from the internet, full stop — if remote access is genuinely required, it belongs behind a VPN with MFA and an intermediate jump host. Second, factory-default credentials must be changed at commissioning and audited annually; this is basic and yet still routinely missed on small skid-mounted systems delivered by integrators. Third, operator training on switching to manual control is what actually saved this facility — muscle memory for graceful degradation is a legitimate cybersecurity control.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

This week's news cycle is dominated by IT-side threats that nonetheless matter to plant floor engineers because they sit at the OT boundary. Banking trojans Manic, Grandoreiro, and ToxicPanda 2.0 continue to expand — mostly a corporate/finance problem, but a reminder that engineering workstations sharing user accounts with corporate email are still the most common initial-access route into ICS networks. If your HMIs and engineering workstations still let operators check webmail, this is your recurring risk. A new phishing toolkit called iAuthFlow V2 is drawing serious attention: it registers an attacker-controlled passkey on a compromised account, meaning even a password reset and session revocation won't kick the attacker out. For OT organisations that have been steadily rolling out passkey and FIDO2 authentication for engineers and remote-support vendors, the incident response playbook needs updating — recovery from account compromise now requires explicit enumeration and removal of registered authenticators, not just credential rotation. AI-focused researchers also demonstrated 'Cryptographic Context Injection,' which smuggles malicious prompts past Grok and Gemini guardrails by hiding them until they're decrypted inside a trusted execution environment. As more sites experiment with LLM copilots for procedure lookup, alarm summarisation, or maintenance chat, this is a preview of the prompt-injection problems that will follow those tools into control rooms. On the tooling side, SANS ISC ran a strong week of practical Microsoft Graph and PowerShell posts on hunting stale accounts, risky logins, and missed MFA rollouts. These are the exact hygiene tasks that identity-federated OT environments (SCADA authenticating to Entra ID, remote vendor access via Azure) should be doing quarterly at minimum. If you don't know which engineering or vendor accounts are still exempt from MFA, assume an attacker already does.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The KEV additions over the past month are heavy on perimeter and management-plane infrastructure that OT networks lean on. Cisco Secure Firewall ASA/FTD (CVE-2026-20349) and Firewall Management Center (CVE-2026-20316, a hard-coded password) are actively exploited — these are the exact devices commonly deployed at the IT/OT DMZ. Broadcom VMware vCenter path traversal (CVE-2026-59310) matters because a large fraction of SCADA, historian, and engineering workstation estates now run on vSphere. Fortinet FortiOS (CVE-2025-68686) and Progress LoadMaster (CVE-2026-8037) round out a very ugly picture for edge infrastructure. For controls engineers, the practical action is coordination with IT: confirm that the firewalls, load balancers, and hypervisors managing your Purdue Level 3.5 and Level 3 assets are on the patched builds, and verify that management interfaces are not reachable from the enterprise network — let alone the internet. Also worth flagging internally: Microsoft IKE Service Extensions (CVE-2026-33824) is a wormable-looking pre-auth RCE on any Windows host running IPsec, which includes many jump hosts and site-to-site VPN endpoints used for remote plant access. No purely ICS-vendor entries this cycle (no Rockwell, Siemens, Schneider, or Emerson in KEV this month), but the exploited IT stack around ICS is where adversaries are living. Treat these as OT-relevant even though the CVEs don't say 'PLC.'Read more →
