Today's Knowledge Bite

The original 4–20 mA current loop standard traces its dominance not to a committee decision but to a practical accident: 4 mA was chosen as the 'live zero' because it was the lowest current that would reliably power a two-wire transmitter's electronics while still leaving headroom for a 0–100% signal. That single design choice — driven by 1950s transistor efficiency — is why a broken wire still reads as a fault today rather than a legitimate zero, a diagnostic feature we now take for granted.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller predates electronics entirely — Elmer Sperry's ship-steering 'metal mike' autopilot of 1911 already contained proportional, integral, and derivative action implemented with gyroscopes, springs, and hydraulic servos. Nicholas Minorsky formalised the mathematics in 1922 while analysing why the USS New Mexico's steering behaved the way it did, and his equations are essentially the ones we still tune today. Every time you adjust a Ki term in a modern DCS, you are calibrating a control law that was first proven at sea on a battleship, using no transistors or software at all.

Tech Spotlight

Ethernet-APL (Advanced Physical Layer) is quietly reaching the point where it will change field-level instrumentation in process plants. Built on the IEEE 802.3cg 10BASE-T1L standard, it delivers 10 Mbit/s Ethernet plus power over a single twisted pair, in intrinsically safe variants, at cable runs up to 1 km — matching the reach that HART and fieldbus users have relied on for decades. This means transmitters, valve positioners, and analysers can speak native Ethernet (typically PROFINET, EtherNet/IP, or OPC UA FX) all the way down to the sensor, without gateways translating between protocols. For controls engineers, the practical implication is a collapse of the traditional protocol hierarchy. Diagnostics that today live inside HART secondary variables — valve travel counts, sensor drift, partial-stroke test results — become first-class data available to historians and asset management systems without needing HART multiplexers or WirelessHART overlays. It also means field devices can be firmware-updated remotely with reasonable bandwidth, which is both a benefit and a new attack surface. Adoption challenges are real: intrinsically safe power budgets are tight, most existing installations use screened twisted pair that will need re-verification for APL segment specs, and the ecosystem of certified APL devices from vendors like Endress+Hauser, Emerson, Yokogawa, and ABB is still maturing. Expect greenfield projects and major turnarounds through 2027–2028 to be where APL first appears in earnest, with brownfield retrofits following once installed base and diagnostic tooling catch up.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) continues to be the standard most often cited in tender documents that controls engineers actually have to respond to. If you have not read it recently, the zone-and-conduit methodology it prescribes is worth revisiting in light of the recent perimeter-device KEVs: many sites drew zone boundaries around the plant DMZ years ago and never re-evaluated when new remote-access tools like ScreenConnect were bolted on. Those tools often create de facto conduits that were never risk-assessed, and 62443-3-2 gives you the formal language to push back when management asks why a new remote-support contract needs a security review. On the US side, NIST SP 800-82 Revision 3 remains the practical reference for OT security programmes, and its alignment with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 'Govern' function is prompting more asset owners to formalise OT-specific governance rather than treating it as an IT afterthought. If your organisation is drafting an OT security policy this quarter, the 800-82r3 appendices on overlays for specific sectors (water, energy, manufacturing) are the most under-used part of the document. ISA-TR84.00.09 on cybersecurity for safety instrumented systems is also worth flagging: it explicitly addresses the question of whether cyber events should be considered in SIL determination, which is increasingly relevant as SIS engineering workstations end up on shared networks with the BPCS. If you are involved in a HAZOP or LOPA this year, expect that question to come up.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the 2023 Aliquippa, Pennsylvania water authority incident, where the CyberAv3ngers group compromised a Unitronics Vision-series PLC controlling a booster station and left a defacement message on the HMI. The attack was not sophisticated: the PLC was exposed to the internet on TCP port 20256, still using the factory default password of 1111, and the group was scanning broadly for Unitronics devices matching that fingerprint. Operators noticed the alarm, switched to manual control, and no water quality event occurred — but the reputational and regulatory fallout drove CISA advisories and eventually contributed to EPA enforcement actions across the sector. The mechanics matter for controls engineers because they highlight how mundane the entry vector was. There was no zero-day, no supply-chain compromise, no insider — just an internet-exposed controller with a default credential. Similar exposures exist today across small water, wastewater, and irrigation districts that use Unitronics, Automation Direct, and low-cost brick PLCs precisely because they are affordable and 'just work.' Three lessons remain durable. First, never trust that a PLC is 'not on the internet' without confirming with an external Shodan or Censys query using the actual public IP of the site. Second, change default credentials as part of commissioning sign-off — and make it a checklist item the SI must demonstrate, not a promise. Third, ensure operators have practised manual fallback recently; Aliquippa avoided a real incident precisely because staff knew how to run the station without the PLC in the loop.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The past several days have been dominated by IT-side security news, but several items carry direct implications for plant floor engineers. ConnectWise ScreenConnect — widely used for remote support of HMIs, historians, and vendor maintenance access — has a new actively exploited flaw allowing file transfer and code execution within active sessions without host confirmation. Any engineering workstation reachable through a ScreenConnect relay should be considered exposed until patched, and vendor remote-support arrangements should be audited this week. On the identity and perimeter front, Citrix NetScaler, Cisco Secure Firewall Management Center, and Fortinet FortiOS all landed on CISA's KEV list within a few days of each other. These devices commonly sit at the IT/OT boundary, terminating VPNs used by integrators and remote operators. A compromise there is effectively a compromise of the DMZ, so IR teams should be verifying configurations, checking for unexpected admin accounts, and reviewing session logs against the specific IoCs published by each vendor. SANS ISC continues to track opportunistic scanning of Proxmox VE — increasingly common as a virtualization host for SCADA servers and engineering VMs in smaller sites. The advisory only affects the long-unsupported version 7, but many OT deployments run stale hypervisors precisely because they were 'installed and forgotten.' A quick asset check for Proxmox 7 hosts is worth an hour of someone's time. The AI-themed stories dominating headlines are mostly enterprise noise, but the SANS diary on autonomous coding agents harvesting stolen LLM inference is a preview of what OT will face: semi-autonomous attackers that chain trivial web flaws at machine speed. For controls engineers, the practical takeaway is that exposed engineering portals and vendor web interfaces will be discovered and probed faster than ever, so obscurity-based protection continues to erode.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA's KEV additions this month lean heavily on remote access and identity infrastructure that OT environments depend on. The ConnectWise ScreenConnect flaw (CVE-2026-84869) is the standout for controls teams: many OEMs and system integrators use ScreenConnect for remote diagnostics of PLCs, drives, and HMI applications, and the vulnerability bypasses the host-confirmation prompt that operators typically rely on as a last line of defence. Verify your ScreenConnect version, disable unattended access where feasible, and require operator approval through an out-of-band channel until patched. The Citrix NetScaler auth bypass (CVE-2026-19490), Cisco FMC/SCC auth bypass (CVE-2026-20079), and Fortinet heap overflow (CVE-2025-25249) all target perimeter devices commonly deployed at the IT/OT boundary or as the VPN termination point for remote engineering access. Treat any unpatched instance as presumed-compromised: rotate credentials, review admin account inventories, and inspect NetScaler /var/log and Fortinet event logs for unusual authentication events. MikroTik RouterOS also received two new KEV entries (CVE-2026-86060 and CVE-2026-67277); MikroTik gear shows up surprisingly often in remote pump stations, wind farms, and small water utilities, so field asset inventories are worth revisiting. The JFrog Artifactory cluster of CVEs matters if your team uses Artifactory to stage firmware, container images, or OT software packages — an authentication bypass in your artefact repository is a supply-chain compromise waiting to happen. Rotate tokens, confirm anonymous access is truly disabled, and verify signatures on any firmware pulled from repos during the exposure window.Read more →
