An unpatched GeoServer zero-day (SQL injection to RCE) is being actively exploited today, so if you have any geospatial services feeding your SCADA or GIS-adjacent utility systems, get them behind VPN or off the internet before the weekend. Combine that with a new Cisco ASA/FTD KEV entry (CVE-2026-20349) that can crash the device unauthenticated remotely, and this week's priority is your IT/OT perimeter. Microsoft's 418-CVE Patch Tuesday, including critical DNS Server and QUIC RCEs, needs to be on your engineering workstation and jump server maintenance schedule. Verify exposure, patch what you can, and document compensating controls for anything you can't.

Today's Knowledge Bite

GeoServer, the software behind today's zero-day, quietly underpins a surprising amount of critical infrastructure visualisation — from the US National Weather Service's flood mapping to the mapping backends used by several transmission system operators for outage management. Its Open Geospatial Consortium (OGC) filter language, which is where today's SQL injection lives, was originally designed in the late 1990s as an XML-based query language that predates and heavily influenced XPath.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was formalised not in a research lab but on a US Navy destroyer. Nicholas Minorsky derived the three-term control law in 1922 while working on automatic ship steering for the USS New Mexico, by watching how experienced helmsmen actually behaved — they corrected for current heading error (P), how long the ship had been off course (I), and how fast it was swinging back (D). Almost every temperature loop, flow loop, and motion axis you tune today is running an algorithm reverse-engineered from human intuition about steering a battleship.

Tech Spotlight

Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) on standard Ethernet is finally moving from vendor demos to production plant floors, and OPC UA over TSN is the combination getting most of the attention. The idea is straightforward: instead of running Profinet IRT, EtherCAT, EtherNet/IP CIP Motion and Sercos III on separate physical infrastructures, TSN provides deterministic sub-microsecond scheduling on plain 1 GbE and 10 GbE switches, and OPC UA PubSub carries the payload in a vendor-neutral information model. In practice this means one converged network can carry motion control, safety, process I/O, condition-monitoring telemetry, and MES traffic simultaneously with guaranteed latency for the deterministic flows. The practical implications for controls engineers are significant. Cable and switch counts drop, and the historical wall between the automation network and IT infrastructure becomes an administrative rather than physical boundary. That is also the biggest challenge: TSN configuration (IEEE 802.1Qbv gate schedules, 802.1AS-Rev time sync, 802.1CB frame replication) is materially harder than setting VLANs, and most sites do not have the network engineering depth in-house. Adoption is currently strongest in semiconductor fabs, automotive body shops, and greenfield packaging lines. Expect the next 18 months to see broader PLC vendor support (Siemens S7-1500 TSN, Rockwell 5069 TSN modules, Beckhoff already there) and, importantly, managed switches from Cisco IE, Hirschmann and Moxa that make schedule provisioning tractable.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2, the risk assessment and system design standard, remains the most frequently misapplied part of the family. The common failure mode is treating the zone and conduit exercise as a documentation task rather than a design-driving activity — engineers draw zones around what already exists on the network, rather than using the target Security Level (SL-T) to justify segmentation changes. If your zone diagram has no conduits crossing security levels, you have probably drawn it wrong: the whole point is to make the trust transitions visible so you can specify the conduit's protective functions. Related: NIST SP 800-82 Revision 3, published in late 2023, continues to be the reference most US operators cite in regulator conversations, and it now aligns explicitly with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 'Govern' function. If you have not refreshed your ICS security programme mapping since CSF 2.0 landed, the Govern function's expectations around supply chain risk management (GV.SC) are the ones auditors are increasingly asking about — particularly for firmware provenance and third-party remote access. This dovetails with the EU Cyber Resilience Act obligations coming into force for products placed on the EU market, which will start showing up as SBOM requirements in your next PLC and drive tenders.Read more →

Incident of the Week

A useful case to revisit this week is the 2024 attack on a US water utility in which Iranian-linked actors compromised Unitronics Vision series PLCs by exploiting internet-exposed devices still running the factory default password of 1111. The affected utility in Aliquippa, Pennsylvania had its booster station PLC defaced with an anti-Israel message, forcing operators to switch to manual control while the device was recovered. There was no drinking water impact, but the operational disruption was real and CISA subsequently published an advisory highlighting the wider exposure across multiple US water systems. What made this incident notable was not sophistication — the attackers used Shodan-discoverable devices on TCP/20256 with default credentials — but the sheer scale of exposure it revealed. Follow-up scanning identified dozens of similar utilities running Unitronics gear directly on the internet, many with identical configurations shipped by the same integrator. Three lessons for controls engineers still hold up. First, treat any device shipped with a default password as compromised until you change it; the vendor's quick-start guide is also the attacker's playbook. Second, if a PLC has an Ethernet port, someone will eventually put it on the internet — assume that and design VPN-only remote access into the integrator's standard build sheet. Third, know your integrator's fleet: if the same systems integrator built ten of your sister sites, they very likely all share the same weakness, and a coordinated remediation is faster than ten independent ones.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The headline item for controls engineers today is an unpatched zero-day in GeoServer being actively exploited in the wild. The flaw is an SQL injection that can be escalated to remote code execution. While GeoServer is not a traditional ICS component, it turns up more often than you would expect in utility GIS deployments, pipeline mapping, land management for mining and rail, and municipal water/wastewater environments where geospatial data feeds SCADA visualisation layers. Any GeoServer instance reachable from an enterprise or DMZ network should be considered at risk until a vendor patch lands; interim mitigations should include WAF rules, blocking OGC filter parameters known to be abused, and pulling exposed instances behind VPN. Microsoft's August Patch Tuesday delivered fixes for 418 vulnerabilities, one exploited in the wild and two publicly disclosed zero-days. Notable items include critical remote code execution bugs in the Windows QUIC stack and DNS Server, plus a container tampering flaw. Engineering workstations, historians, and jump servers running Windows Server 2019/2022 in Purdue Level 3 zones should be scheduled into the next maintenance window. The DNS Server RCE is particularly relevant for sites running Active Directory-integrated DNS on the OT domain controller. On the endpoint side, the Rust-based AmnesiaStealer targeting macOS is a reminder that engineering laptops used for field service — increasingly Macs at integrators and OEMs — are firmly in scope for credential theft. Cached VPN tokens, browser-stored HMI passwords, and keychain-stored engineering tool licences are all fair game. Adobe Commerce is also under immediate exploitation post-disclosure, reinforcing the accelerating disclosure-to-exploit window that now also applies to industrial vendors.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

CISA added a Cisco Secure Firewall ASA/FTD denial-of-service issue (CVE-2026-20349) to the KEV catalogue on 11 August. Because ASAs are still widely deployed as the IT/OT boundary firewall in refineries, water utilities and manufacturing sites, an unauthenticated remote reload is effectively a plant-network availability threat. Pair that with the earlier Cisco FMC hard-coded password entry (CVE-2026-20316) from late July and it is worth auditing management-plane exposure and confirming ASA/FTD/FMC are on current trains this weekend. Also notable for OT-adjacent infrastructure: Progress LoadMaster (CVE-2026-8037) command injection, Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator OS command injection (CVE-2026-16812), Fortinet FortiOS (CVE-2025-68686) and FortiSandbox (CVE-2026-25089) — the usual suspects for site-to-site VPN and SD-WAN between plants and corporate. If your remote-access path into the control network traverses any of these, prioritise patching or compensating ACLs. The N-able N-central authentication bypass pair (CVE-2026-18556 and its incomplete-patch sibling CVE-2026-18577) is a wake-up call for anyone using MSP tooling to manage OT assets — that management plane is a hypervisor-equivalent trust boundary. The remaining KEV additions (Metabase SQLi, TeamCity deserialisation, Langflow RCE, SharePoint deserialisation, Apache Tomcat) mostly hit IT services, but historian reporting layers, MES-adjacent dashboards, and internal document portals holding P&IDs and control narratives frequently ride on these stacks.
