Today's Knowledge Bite

The Bendix EC80 disclosure highlights a quirk of transport-sector regulation: safety recalls issued through NHTSA are legally mandated and heavily publicised, while cybersecurity fixes are not. Manufacturers have discovered they can piggyback silent security patches onto safety recalls to guarantee near-100% uptake — something the ICS world, with its voluntary advisories and low patch rates, can only envy.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was mathematically formalised not in a chemical plant or power station, but on a US Navy destroyer. In 1922, Nicolas Minorsky published his analysis of the automatic steering of the USS New Mexico, deriving the three-term control law by watching how experienced helmsmen anticipated the ship's yaw — they applied rudder proportional to the current heading error, integrated past error to correct for steady wind, and countered by the rate of change. Nearly every temperature loop and flow controller in your plant is, in a very real sense, still steering that battleship.

Tech Spotlight

This week's spotlight is on Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) as the transport for OPC UA FX (Field eXchange), the emerging profile aimed at replacing traditional fieldbuses like PROFINET IRT and EtherCAT for deterministic controller-to-controller and controller-to-device traffic. OPC UA FX brings the semantic richness of OPC UA information models down to the field level, while TSN provides the sub-microsecond scheduling and bounded latency needed for motion control. In principle, this means one Ethernet fabric can carry engineering data, MES traffic, and hard-real-time I/O without the historical need for separate parallel networks. The practical reality is messier. TSN requires managed switches that support IEEE 802.1Qbv scheduling, precise time synchronisation via 802.1AS, and stream reservation via 802.1Qcc — and interoperability between vendors is still uneven. Early adopters are finding that mixing TSN-capable and legacy switches within a single domain often produces unpredictable behaviour, and configuring the traffic schedules requires engineering tools that most OT teams don't yet have. Expect the next two to three years to be dominated by pilot cells and vendor-specific islands rather than plant-wide rollouts, but the direction of travel is clear: the fieldbus wars are ending with Ethernet winning again.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-2-1, the standard covering security program requirements for asset owners, saw its second edition finalised last year and is now being widely referenced by insurers and regulators as the baseline for demonstrating a defensible OT security program. Unlike the first edition, which read more like a management-system checklist, the second edition aligns security program elements to specific security levels and maturity levels, making it much more auditable. If your organisation is being asked by cyber insurance underwriters to evidence its OT security posture, expect 62443-2-1 mappings to appear in questionnaires this renewal cycle. On the NIST side, the CSF 2.0 Community Profile for OT continues to mature, and the NIST SP 800-82r3 guide to OT security remains the most practical free reference for engineers building a program from scratch. The r3 revision integrates the CSF 2.0 Govern function and expands treatment of IIoT and cloud-connected OT — both areas where earlier guidance was thin. Worth watching: ISA is progressing work on ISA-TR84.00.09, which addresses cybersecurity related to the safety lifecycle for SIS. The direction is toward explicit requirements that cybersecurity risk be considered in HAZOP and LOPA studies, not just bolted on afterwards. For process engineers, this is the standard that will most directly change how you conduct safety studies over the next few years.Read more →

Incident of the Week

This week's case study is the 2023 Aliquippa, Pennsylvania water authority incident, which remains one of the clearest illustrations of low-effort, high-impact OT compromise. Attackers affiliated with the CyberAv3ngers group compromised a Unitronics Vision-series PLC controlling a booster station, displaying an anti-Israel message on the HMI and forcing the utility to switch to manual operation. No customer water supply was disrupted, but the incident triggered a joint CISA/FBI/EPA advisory and congressional attention. The root cause was mundane: the PLC was exposed to the internet on its default port 20256, with the factory default password of 1111 unchanged. There was no exploit, no zero-day, no sophisticated tradecraft — just a scan-and-login. The attackers reportedly used Shodan-style discovery to find hundreds of similarly exposed Unitronics devices across multiple sectors. Three lessons endure. First, small utilities and remote pump/lift stations are disproportionately exposed because they often use cellular modems configured for engineer convenience rather than security; inventory every routable OT device you own. Second, default credentials on OT equipment remain the single most exploited weakness — a change-and-verify sweep costs almost nothing and would have prevented this incident. Third, having a rehearsed manual-operations fallback is what turned this from a potential public-health event into a nuisance; if your operators cannot run the process without the PLC for a shift, that gap is a bigger risk than any specific CVE.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

The most consequential story for controls engineers this week comes from NMFTA researchers who revealed that a safety recall for the Bendix EC80 heavy-truck brake controller quietly patched remote code execution and denial-of-service vulnerabilities alongside its stated safety fix. This is a textbook example of an OEM using the safety-recall channel to push a security fix without labelling it as such, a pattern we see in industrial equipment too. For fleets and adjacent process industries running vehicle telematics gateways on the same networks, the takeaway is that recall bulletins should be treated as security intelligence, not just maintenance paperwork. On the enterprise-IT side, Microsoft released fixes for critical Azure, Entra, and SharePoint vulnerabilities, and Apple patched a high-severity authentication bypass. Two more SharePoint deserialization bugs were added to the CISA KEV list, which matters for OT teams because SharePoint frequently hosts engineering document libraries, MOC workflows, and shift-handover records that bridge the L3/L4 boundary. If your plant relies on SharePoint for procedure control or drawing management, patch cadence there is now an operational safety issue. SANS ISC published a striking analysis showing automated SSH attackers can move from initial login to installed persistence in just 22 seconds — faster than most human responders can even acknowledge an alert. For jump hosts, historian servers, and Linux-based edge gateways exposed even briefly to the internet, this reinforces that SSH keys, MFA, and connection allow-lists are non-negotiable. A separate ISC piece on the keyv/cacheable npm worm warns defenders not to reflexively revoke tokens before triage, a nuance worth remembering if your MES or dashboard stack pulls Node dependencies. Black Hat USA 2026 continues to generate a steady stream of vendor announcements, and the AI-security theme dominates, with Atlassian's Rovo AI hit by a one-click data exfiltration flaw (RovoBlast). As more engineering teams pilot AI copilots against Confluence and Jira containing P&IDs, tag databases, and incident reports, prompt-injection and connector abuse are becoming credible data-loss vectors for OT documentation.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

The KEV additions most directly relevant to OT this fortnight are CVE-2023-4346 in the KNX protocol's connection authorization scheme, and the pair of N-able N-central authentication bypasses (CVE-2026-18556 and its incomplete-patch sibling CVE-2026-18577). The KNX flaw allows an attacker to abuse the lockout mechanism to purge devices and lock out the BCU key, effectively bricking building automation installations — a real risk for facilities running KNX for HVAC, lighting, and access control adjacent to production areas. Mitigation requires enabling KNX Secure (Data Secure and IP Secure) and restricting IP-side access to the KNXnet/IP routers. Several remote management and perimeter products are also on the list: Progress LoadMaster (unauth command injection), JetBrains TeamCity (deserialization RCE via agent polling), Cisco Secure FMC (hard-coded password), Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator (OS command injection), Check Point SmartConsole (auth bypass with token theft), and multiple Fortinet FortiOS/FortiSandbox issues. Any of these commonly sit at the IT/OT boundary or in the DMZ; treat KEV listing as an implicit exploitation-in-the-wild flag and pull patch windows forward accordingly. Finally, IBM Langflow appears twice (CVE-2026-9198 and CVE-2026-0770) with unauthenticated RCE on default deployments. If any group in your organisation is prototyping LLM pipelines that touch operational data — chatbot access to historian tags is a common early experiment — inventory those Langflow instances now and keep them off any routable OT network segment.Read more →
