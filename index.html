Today's Knowledge Bite

OPC-UA's ANSL (Automation Network Service Layer) — referenced in today's B&R Automation Studio advisory — is a B&R-proprietary protocol that predates OPC-UA in the company's stack and is still used for engineering connections to controllers. It can ride over TLS, but historically many sites left it on plain TCP because that's what worked out of the box. The advisory is a useful nudge: just because the protocol supports TLS doesn't mean your project actually negotiated it.

Engineering Curiosity

⚛

The PID controller as we know it was largely codified by Nicolas Minorsky in 1922, not for chemical plants but for steering US Navy battleships — he derived the three-term law by watching how a skilled helmsman compensated not just for current heading error, but for how fast it was changing and how long it had been off course. The USS New Mexico trials proved the algorithm could hold a heading to within two-thirds of a degree, a performance no human helmsman could sustain. Every cascade loop you tune today traces its lineage back to a problem of keeping a 32,000-ton ship pointed at the horizon.

Tech Spotlight

Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) over standard Ethernet is finally moving from trade-show demos to plant deployments, particularly paired with OPC-UA's PubSub model. The promise is that you can run deterministic motion-control traffic, standard PLC I/O, and convergent IT data on a single switched fabric, replacing parallel Profinet IRT, EtherCAT and Ethernet/IP CIP-Sync networks. The IEEE 802.1 standards (Qbv scheduling, Qci policing, AS time sync) are stable, and silicon from Marvell, NXP and Intel now supports them at line speeds. In practice, controls engineers face two challenges. First, configuring TSN is fundamentally different from configuring a Profinet network — you need a centralised network configuration model (CNC/CUC), and engineering tools must agree on which streams are scheduled to the microsecond. The OPC-UA FX (Field eXchange) specifications attempt to standardise this, but tool maturity varies wildly between vendors. Second, troubleshooting requires hardware-timestamping-capable test gear; a conventional managed-switch SPAN port plus Wireshark won't tell you whether a Qbv gate closed a microsecond too early. Adoption today is strongest in semiconductor fabs and automotive body shops where the cost of mixed-vendor synchronisation pain justifies the early-adopter tax. For most brownfield process plants, the practical near-term play is TSN-capable switches in greenfield cells while the rest of the site remains on Profinet RT or Ethernet/IP — leaving the option open without betting the farm.Read more →

Standards Watch

IEC 62443-3-2 (security risk assessment for system design) continues to be the standard that sites stumble over the most. The 2020 edition requires you to define zones and conduits, perform a detailed cyber risk assessment per zone, and document tolerable risk — yet most plants still operate from a single 'OT network' diagram. Recent IECEE certification activity shows asset owners increasingly being asked by insurers and regulators to demonstrate 62443-3-2 evidence, not just 62443-2-1 management-system evidence. On the product side, 62443-4-1 and 4-2 certifications are now a routine line-item in tenders for new DCS, PLC and safety-system procurements. Today's ABB Automation Runtime and Automation Studio advisories are useful here: 4-1 mandates a documented vulnerability handling process and CSAF-formatted advisories are exactly that process working as intended. When you see a vendor publishing CSAF, that's a tangible 62443-4-1 deliverable. Worth watching: NIST SP 800-82r3 (Guide to OT Security) is now the de facto reference US federal integrators map to, and it explicitly aligns with 62443 zones-and-conduits language. If you're writing a site security plan, structuring it around 800-82r3 sections while citing 62443 controls gives you a document that satisfies both US federal and international auditors without duplication.Read more →

Incident of the Week

The Daemon Tools supply-chain compromise reported this week is a useful cautionary tale even though it isn't strictly an ICS incident. Attackers trojanised the installer for a long-running disk-image utility and pushed it through normal distribution channels; thousands of installations occurred globally, but a custom backdoor was selectively dropped on roughly a dozen government and scientific research targets. The attacker was clearly profiling victims before deploying second-stage payloads. Why it matters on the plant floor: engineering workstations accumulate utilities like Daemon Tools, 7-Zip, PuTTY, WinSCP, Notepad++ and assorted serial terminal programs over years of integrator visits. These tools are almost never tracked in a software inventory, rarely patched, and often installed with local admin rights. A targeted supply-chain attack against any one of them gives an adversary a foothold on a machine that talks directly to controllers. Three takeaways. First, build and maintain an actual approved-software list for engineering workstations and enforce it with allow-listing (Windows Defender Application Control or equivalent). Second, source utilities from a single internal repository where hashes are pinned, rather than letting each engineer download from the open web. Third, treat the engineering workstation as the highest-value asset in the cell — because to an attacker, it is.Read more →

ICS / OT News Digest

CISA released a busy slate of ICS advisories today, dominated by ABB and B&R products. Automation Studio carries a TLS/OPC-UA trust flaw allowing an attacker to masquerade as a trusted server during ANSL or OPC-UA session establishment — a serious concern given that engineering workstations frequently reach across plant boundaries to controllers. Automation Runtime (CVE-2025-11044 and siblings) can be crashed by a remote attacker, an availability hit for any B&R-based line. The PVI client logs sensitive data when verbose logging is enabled, and ABB Ability OPTIMAX has an authentication bypass in its Azure AD SSO integration — relevant to anyone running cloud-tied performance optimisation for power generation. Outside the ABB stack, Hitachi Energy PCM600 (the protection and control IED manager used widely in substations) has an integrity-impact vulnerability requiring prompt review of engineering laptops touching protection relays. Johnson Controls CEM AC2000 access-control system carries a CVSS 8.7 local privilege-escalation flaw across versions 10.6 through 12.0 — a physical-security platform that often shares networks with building OT. On the IT side that bleeds into OT, Palo Alto Networks is rushing a patch for CVE-2026-0300, a zero-day in the PAN-OS Captive Portal already being exploited against PA and VM-series firewalls. Many sites use these as the IT/OT boundary, so confirm whether Captive Portal is enabled and apply mitigations as soon as the fix lands. Oracle has also moved to a monthly critical patch cadence, shortening the window between disclosure and fix for database and middleware components common in MES and historian stacks. A supply-chain incident around trojanised Daemon Tools installers reminds us how unsanctioned utilities on engineering workstations remain a credible vector — a dozen high-value targets, including government and scientific sites, received the second-stage backdoor. Combined with Microsoft warning about an AitM phishing campaign aimed at US organisations, the human-factor risk on OT-adjacent endpoints is elevated this week.Read more →

CISA Vulnerability Digest

Two KEV entries this week have direct OT relevance. CVE-2024-1708 in ConnectWise ScreenConnect (added 28 April) is a path-traversal-to-RCE flaw — ScreenConnect and similar remote-support tools are frequently installed on HMIs and engineering workstations by integrators, often without IT visibility. Hunt for unauthorised ScreenConnect agents in your DMZ and engineering VLANs, and confirm any sanctioned deployments are on the patched build. The two SimpleHelp entries (CVE-2024-57726/57728) are similar tools used by OEMs for remote diagnostics and warrant the same sweep. CVE-2025-29635 (D-Link DIR-823X command injection) and CVE-2024-7399 (Samsung MagicINFO 9 path traversal writing files as SYSTEM) are both being actively exploited; the latter is notable because MagicINFO drives digital signage that occasionally lives on the same flat networks as building automation. Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager picked up three KEV entries — relevant if your enterprise SD-WAN fabric extends into plant sites for remote backhaul. The oldest entry, CVE-2009-0238 in Microsoft Excel, is a stark reminder that legacy engineering workstations running unpatched Office still get hit.
